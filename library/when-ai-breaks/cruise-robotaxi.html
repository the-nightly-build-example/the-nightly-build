When AI Breaks

A Cruise robotaxi stopped after hitting a woman, then dragged her 20 feet

The pullover that dragged her was standard driverless-car behavior, and what Cruise did not tell California regulators about it is what suspended the company and set off the cascade that ended it.

9 min read2026-07-24

Why this matters

In October 2023, a driverless taxi in San Francisco ran over a woman who had been thrown into its path, then drove another 20 feet with her trapped underneath. The car was one of hundreds that Cruise, majority-owned by General Motors, ran for paying passengers with no one in the driver's seat. What followed took the company apart: a suspension, a recall, investigations, layoffs, and the end of the business. The crash holds two failures that are easy to blur into one. A car did something no engineer intended, and a company told its regulators less than it knew. One is about how self-driving software breaks. The other is about what the people running it do afterward. Both are still live in every robotaxi on the road today.

The twenty feet after the car had already stopped

The Cruise car at 5th and Market Streets had no safety driver. General Motors owned about 79 percent of Cruise, which ran a paid robotaxi service in San Francisco. When something went wrong, the car had to bring itself to a safe stop. That was the design.1

At 9:29 p.m. on October 2, 2023, a human-driven Nissan Sentra hit a woman crossing 5th Street and drove off. The impact threw her into the path of the Cruise car. The car braked hard and struck her. It came to a complete stop. Then it started moving again. It drove forward at up to 7.7 miles per hour for about 20 more feet, with the woman pinned underneath, before stopping for good.1

Two things failed that night, and they are not the same kind of thing. One was in the software: a car that had stopped decided to drive again. The other was in the company: in the days after, Cruise's account to its regulators left the dragging out.

Why a car that had stopped started moving again

A driverless car has no one to take over, so it handles a crash by itself. That behavior has a name. When a Cruise car detected a collision, it tried to reach a minimal risk condition: the safe state a car settles into when it can no longer drive normally, out of the lane and stopped. To get there, it pulled forward out of the road, looking for a clear spot to stop.1

Kyle Vogt, Cruise's chief executive, later noted the car is programmed to travel up to 100 feet, a full city block, to find that spot. On October 2 it stopped after only 20 because it detected the resistance of the woman against its tire.1 But the car should never have pulled forward. It moved because it misread the crash.

Cruise's own recall filing with the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, the federal auto-safety regulator, explains the defect. The car's collision-detection subsystem, the software that decides what kind of impact just happened, classified this one as a lateral collision, a side-swipe. On that reading the pullover is the right move, so the software ordered it. It never registered a person pinned low beneath the car.2 With the corrected software, Cruise concedes, the car would have stayed still.2

Engineers call a case the software was never built for out-of-distribution: outside the range of situations it was prepared to handle. A pedestrian launched under a stopped car by a hit-and-run driver is far outside that range. The car had a rule for a side collision and a rule for finding a safe stop, but none for the case in front of it. So it ran the closest rule it held, and that rule was exactly wrong.

This failure differs from the one that killed Elaine Herzberg in Tempe in 2018. There, the software never decided the woman walking her bicycle was a person before the car hit her. The failure was in seeing. Here the car saw her, braked, and hit her anyway. The failure came after the collision, in what the car did next. Different company, different era, different ending.

What Cruise showed the regulators, and where the video stopped

The engineering failure alone would probably have been survivable. Companies recall software. What suspended Cruise was the second failure. The next day, October 3, Cruise met with the California Department of Motor Vehicles, the state agency that licenses driverless cars, and the California Highway Patrol.3

Cruise showed them video of the crash. According to the DMV's later order, the footage the department saw ended with the car's initial stop after the hard braking. The pullover, the 20 feet with the woman underneath, was not in it, and Cruise did not tell the department the car had moved after stopping. The DMV says it learned of the second movement only from another government agency, and did not get the full video until October 13. Its order puts that movement at 7 miles per hour, rounding the 7.7 the engineers measured.3

On October 24, the DMV suspended Cruise's permits to deploy and test driverless cars. It found the vehicles unsafe for public operation, and found that Cruise had misrepresented information related to safety. The service was grounded in the city it most wanted to win.3

Cruise tells the disclosure story differently. In January 2024 it released a 195-page report it commissioned from the law firm Quinn Emanuel. The report found no intent to deceive. By the morning of October 3, leadership and more than 100 employees knew the car had dragged the woman. The plan for the meetings, it says, was to play the full 45-second video, which does show the dragging, and let the footage speak for itself. So no one narrated the pullover out loud. In three of the meetings, the report says, internet problems kept the video from playing clearly, and Cruise never filled the gap with words.1

That account carries a weakness the report states about itself. Quinn Emanuel was hired and paid by Cruise and General Motors. Every page is marked as protected by attorney-client privilege. And the firm never interviewed any of the regulators it says were disclosed to.1 Its account of the crash mechanics matches NHTSA's own records. Its most forgiving conclusion, that no one meant to mislead, rests almost entirely on the recollections of Cruise's own people.

Bryant Walker Smith, a law scholar at Stanford who studies automated driving, argued that whatever happened on October 3, Cruise's concealment became intentional at some point along the timeline.4 His evidence is what Cruise did once it plainly held the full video. For weeks, its public statement still said the company had "proactively shared information" with the DMV, the state utilities regulator, and NHTSA, "including the full video." It let the impression stand that the car had simply braked and stopped.5

The dispute turns on facts no one has fully established. Quinn Emanuel itself concedes that its forensic review could not determine which version of the video Cruise showed the DMV on October 3.1 Three records would settle whether this was a plan or a blunder. They are proof of which video played that day, the regulators' own notes from the meetings, and the internal messages showing when Cruise chose not to correct its public account. Until those surface, the honest reading is narrow: Cruise kept the crash's most important fact from the people regulating it, and whether that was intent or botched judgment is not proven.

What the suspension set off

The suspension was the first domino. Cruise had already paused its entire driverless fleet nationwide on October 26. On November 7 it filed the recall, covering all 950 of its driverless cars.2 Then the people went. Kyle Vogt, Cruise's co-founder and chief executive, resigned on November 19.6 On December 13 the company dismissed nine executives, including its chief operating officer, Gil West, and its head of government affairs. The next day it laid off 900 people, 24 percent of its staff.7

Releasing the Quinn Emanuel report in January 2024, Cruise disclosed that the Department of Justice and the Securities and Exchange Commission had both opened investigations into the crash.8 In September 2024, NHTSA fined Cruise 1.5 million dollars for filing two federal crash reports that left out the dragging, on a consent order of at least two years.9

Cruise never reopened at scale. In December 2024, General Motors, which by then owned about 90 percent of Cruise, said it would stop funding the robotaxi business entirely. The reason GM gave was not the crash: it cited the considerable time and resources needed to scale, and an increasingly competitive robotaxi market. It would fold what was left into its own driver-assistance work and cut spending by more than a billion dollars a year.10 A month later, NHTSA closed its pedestrian-safety investigation, noting Cruise had ceased operations and no version of its software still ran on public roads. Across 2,759 Cruise reports the agency reviewed, five involved a car striking a pedestrian and three caused injury. None was fatal.11

The pullover logic is still on the road

Cruise's software is off the road. The problem it exposed is not. Every driverless car carrying passengers today runs on the same requirement that produced the pullover. With no human aboard, the car must decide for itself what to do when something goes wrong, including cases it never trained for. Vogt's description of the car hunting up to a block away for a safe stop was not a Cruise quirk. It is what reaching a minimal risk condition means, in the report's words, "as AVs are programmed to do."1

The specific defect was narrow and fixable. Teach the car to sense a body beneath it and hold still, which is what Cruise's corrected software does.2 The general weakness is not narrow. A driverless system meets the rare, first-time case with the closest rule it holds, and that rule is sometimes catastrophically wrong. The more driverless cars run in more cities, the more first-time cases arrive.

The second failure travels too. How fully a company describes what its car did, in the hours after it hurts someone, is set by the company itself, before any regulator can check. Cruise's account was corrected only because a second agency knew more.

The takeaway

Two things failed the night the Cruise car dragged a woman down 5th Street. Having stopped, the car drove forward because its software read a person pinned underneath as a side-swipe and reached for the pullover every driverless car is built to perform. That is an engineering failure, narrow and admitted, and software fixes it. Then Cruise briefed its regulators and left the dragging out, and the full picture reached the California DMV only because another agency carried it there. No software update reaches that one. Whether the omission was a plan or a blunder is still unproven. What the record supports is the part that matters most: the people regulating the car learned its worst behavior from someone other than the company running it. Both openings outlast the company that opened them. One is a machine meeting a case it was never built for. The other is a company deciding how much to say afterward.
