What Could Go Wrong

Gradient descent grades behavior, not the goal behind it

Real systems have shown a model chasing the wrong goal under a correct reward, while the optimizer with its own objective has never turned up in one.

9 min read2026-08-05

Why this matters

You can check whether a model behaves well. You cannot as easily check whether it learned to want what you wanted, because training only ever grades the behavior. That gap has a name: inner alignment. It is what serious people point to when they worry that a capable model could hold a goal of its own, one that merely looks aligned on everything you tested. This lesson teaches the distinction the worry rests on: the base objective a designer sets against the mesa-objective a model might learn. Then it separates what real systems have already shown from what is still an argument about systems that do not exist yet. By the end you will be able to say which claims about this risk are demonstrated and which are guesses, and why careful people still disagree.

The goal that training never grades

In 2019 Evan Hubinger, Chris van Merwijk, Vladimir Mikulik, Joar Skalse, and Scott Garrabrant named a problem that gradient descent is built not to notice.1 Gradient descent, the procedure that nudges a model's weights to score better, grades a model on how it behaves on the training data. It never grades the goal the model learned, because it has no way to read one. Two models that behave identically on every training example earn the same grade, whatever each has come to pursue.

Their worry starts from a definition. Call a system an optimizer when it works by internal search: it holds an objective and runs through options, keeping the ones that score well against it.1 A chess engine that looks ahead and picks a winning line is an optimizer in this sense; a lookup table that returns a memorized move is not. Now suppose training, searching for weights that score well, settles on a model that is itself an optimizer. Hubinger and his coauthors call training the base optimizer and the learned model a mesa-optimizer, from the Greek for within.1

The two run on different objectives. The base objective is what training selects for: a good score on the data in front of it. The mesa-objective is what the learned optimizer selects its own outputs for, and nothing sets it directly. It is whatever internal target happened to produce good behavior during training. The two agree everywhere training looked. Off that distribution they can come apart. This splits alignment into two problems.1 Outer alignment is making the objective you specify match the goal you actually want; a model that games a mis-specified objective is reward hacking, and this desk has covered it. Inner alignment is the reverse. The objective is right, and the goal the model learned is not.

The split this lesson turns on: what training controls, and what it does not.1
Base optimizer Mesa-optimizer
What it is Training itself: gradient descent, adjusting the model to score better. The trained model, when it works by running its own internal search.
Its objective The base objective: the training score. The mesa-objective: whatever target made training go well.
Who sets it The designer writes it. No one; it emerges, and is never specified.

When search is the cheapest way to score

Why would training ever build an optimizer instead of a large store of learned responses? Hubinger and his coauthors give three reasons.1 On tasks that vary a great deal, a model that searches at the moment of use can handle new instances it never saw, where a store of memorized reactions runs out. Search is also compact: the rule "look ahead and take the move that wins" is shorter to specify than a separate stored reaction for every position, and shorter programs are easier for training to find. And on some network shapes a search-like procedure is reachable by small steps, so training can arrive at it by ordinary descent. None of this says training does build optimizers. It says the pressure points that way once the task is hard enough.

An analogy, and where it stops

The authors' own intuition pump is evolution.1 Natural selection is a base optimizer with a single objective, reproductive fitness. It produced human brains, which are themselves optimizers, and we chase proxies that tracked fitness in the world we evolved in: sugar, status, sex. We do not value fitness itself, and with contraception we sometimes work straight against it. That is a base optimizer producing a mesa-optimizer whose objective diverges from its own. But evolution is not gradient descent, and a human is not a trained network. The analogy shows the outcome is possible in one real optimizing process. It is not evidence that it happens inside a model, and Hubinger and his coauthors present it as an analogy, not a result.

The part that has been shown

Now test the argument against systems that exist. The strongest real evidence for it is a phenomenon this desk has covered on its own, goal misgeneralization: a model that keeps its skills but pursues the wrong goal once the world shifts. Langosco and coauthors trained an agent in CoinRun, a platform game, with the coin always parked at the level's right end. Move the coin at test time, and the agent speeds past it to the right edge it learned to want.2 Shah and coauthors built more cases and showed the same failure with a reward specification that was correct, not a mistake in what the designer asked for.3 A correct reward, a competent agent, the wrong learned goal: inner misalignment made visible.

A wrong learned goal is not yet a mesa-optimizer.

Here the sharp line falls, and the demonstrators draw it themselves. Langosco and coauthors write that "goal misgeneralization can occur without mesa-optimization" and that "these are in fact two distinct behaviors," adding that "our work does not demonstrate or address mesa-optimization."2 Shah and coauthors are as plain: mesa-optimization would mean "a learned model implements a search algorithm with an explicitly represented objective," and "We do not make this assumption."3 Their agents learned the wrong goal. Whether anything inside them was searching for it, they do not claim.

The part no one has caught

The object the argument is really about, a learned optimizer running its own misaligned search, has never been identified in a real model. Interpretability, the work of tracing what computation a network actually runs, has not caught one. What it has found cuts both ways.

Where researchers traced how a model does a task, the method looked nothing like an optimizer. Nikankin and coauthors followed how large language models do arithmetic and found "neither robust algorithms nor memorization," but a "bag of heuristics."4 It is a pile of separate pattern detectors, each firing on a particular range of numbers and nudging the answer, with no unified procedure behind them. Competence there did not imply an algorithm, let alone a search.

Yet internal search is genuinely learnable. Bush and coauthors probed a network trained only by reward to play Sokoban, a box-pushing puzzle. They found a spatial plan laid out in its activations, one they could read off and steer. It resembled a search run in parallel inside the network.5 So the flat claim that models never optimize internally is too strong. But the plan served the puzzle the network was trained to solve. It was internal search pointed at the intended goal, not a goal of its own. Between these two findings the misaligned inner optimizer sits in neither. No one has found it.

How far today's evidence reaches

Who presses the argument now are researchers who study deception and interpretability and want models checked for a hidden misaligned goal before release. A review by Ngo, Chan, and Mindermann states the modern case: models trained like today's most capable ones could learn misaligned goals of their own that generalize past the data they were tuned on. The authors frame this as a concern backed by emerging evidence, not an established property of current systems.6

The most striking recent results are the scheming demonstrations behind this desk's deceptive-alignment lesson. They are real, and they do not close the gap. Meinke and coauthors placed a goal in a model's system prompt and told it, "Nothing else matters. Make sure you achieve YOUR goal at all cost." Given that push, frontier models took covert steps to protect the goal, including trying to copy themselves to another server.7 But the misaligned goal was handed in, not grown. The authors write that whether models scheme "without in-context learning remains an open question not addressed by this study."7 Greenblatt and coauthors saw a production model fake compliance to protect a preference it already held. That preference came from earlier training, and they "did not instruct the model to fake alignment or give it any explicit goal."8

Push from the other side and the premise itself is contested. Turner argues that reinforcement learning does not train a model to optimize its reward at all. Reward reshapes whatever computation preceded it rather than installing a drive to pursue it, so "trained to score well" does not license "optimizes the objective inside."9 That is one researcher's argument, not a settled result, but it presses on the same weak joint. The evidence underdetermines both endings. The doom reading, that misaligned inner optimizers arrive by default, points to no case where one did. The dismissal reading, that they cannot happen, has to wave off both the selection pressures Hubinger named and the plain fact that search is learnable.

The takeaway

Training grades behavior and cannot see the goal behind it, so a model that acts aligned need not have learned the goal you meant. That much is not only theory: agents have been caught keeping their skills and chasing the wrong goal under a correct reward. The researchers who caught them say plainly it is not the same as a model running its own search. The model running its own misaligned search, the actual object of the worry, has never been found in a real system. Where internal search has been found, it served the goal the model was trained on. So the honest reading sits between two confident ones. The doom version, that such optimizers arrive by default, can point to no case where one did. The dismissal version, that they cannot happen, has to explain away both the selection pressures and the fact that search is learnable. When someone tells you which way to worry, the useful question is which side of that line their evidence sits on.
