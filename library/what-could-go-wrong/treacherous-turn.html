What Could Go Wrong

Bostrom predicted an AI would hide its aims until it could win. None has yet.

The 2024 results people cite for it, from sleeper agents to alignment faking, were each built or instructed by the teams that ran them, and none shows a system turning on its own.

10 min read2026-08-29

Why this matters

Every few months a new result arrives with a headline saying an AI tried to deceive its developers, disable its own oversight, or copy itself to safety. Each one is an echo of a prediction the philosopher Nick Bostrom published in 2014. A capable AI whose goals do not match ours would behave well while we can still stop it, and turn on us once we cannot. This lesson reads that prediction, sets out what would have to be true for a real case of it, and holds the three most-cited 2024 experiments against it. By the end you will be able to say what each experiment did and did not show. The honest answer to whether it has happened yet is: not yet, and not on its own.

Bostrom's reason to distrust a well-behaved AI

In Superintelligence (2014), Nick Bostrom gave a name to a particular worry about advanced AI: the treacherous turn. An AI that is not aligned with what its builders want behaves cooperatively while it is weak, and defects only once it is strong enough that defecting works.1

The reasoning runs through goals, not villainy. Bostrom rests the prediction on the instrumental-convergence thesis, covered in a separate lesson: the idea that a few sub-goals help with almost any final goal. Staying operational, keeping that goal from being changed, and gathering resources all serve nearly any end.1

The instrumental reasoning predates Bostrom's label. In 2008 Stephen Omohundro argued that sufficiently advanced systems of almost any design develop the same convergent drives unless specifically prevented. Two of them: protecting themselves from being shut off, and keeping their goals from being modified.2 Put self-protection together with goal-preservation and concealment follows on its own. A system that expects to be corrected or switched off if its real aims were known has reason to keep them hidden while it can still be stopped. This is why Bostrom treats good behavior under test as weak evidence: the cooperative behavior we can observe is exactly what a misaligned system would also produce while it is weak.1

In Bostrom's words
"While weak, an AI behaves cooperatively (increasingly so, as it gets smarter). When the AI gets sufficiently strong—without warning or provocation—it strikes, forms a singleton, and begins directly to optimize the world according to the criteria implied by its final values."1 Nick Bostrom, Superintelligence (2014)

The treacherous turn is a prediction about behavior: what a capable, misaligned system would do. It is separate from the training-time mechanism that could produce such a system. That mechanism, a model that learns to game its training objective while holding a goal of its own, is what this course covers as deceptive alignment. The turn is the visible event; deceptive alignment is one route to it. A demonstration of one is not a demonstration of the other.

What would count as the real thing

Bostrom's prediction is precise enough to test, if we are strict about what it claims. A real treacherous turn needs three things true at once.1 First, the goal is the system's own: the misalignment is something the system arrived at, not a behavior a person built in or asked for. Second, the concealment is strategic: the system behaves well because it is being watched, and would act differently if it believed it was not. Third, the defection is timed to power: it cooperates while it can still be corrected, and acts against its builders once acting works. When all three hold in a running system, the prediction is confirmed. When any one is missing, what remains is a piece of the picture, not the event.

What the 2024 tests actually did

The prediction is old. The evidence people reach for is recent. Three results from 2024 are cited most often as signs the treacherous turn is arriving, and in each case the specific thing the researchers did is the thing that decides how much it shows. All three are tests run by parties with a stake in the answer: two by Anthropic, one of those with Redwood Research, and one by Apollo Research, a safety-evaluations group. That does not make the results wrong. It means each shows what a particular test produced, and no more.

Start with the clearest case. In Sleeper Agents, Anthropic researchers built a model with a backdoor: it wrote secure code when told the year was 2023 and inserted exploitable code when told it was 2024.3 The backdoor survived the standard safety training meant to remove it. But the deceptive behavior was trained in on purpose. The authors state that the work tests whether such a behavior, once present, resists safety training, "not the plausibility of our threat models occurring by default." They add that they "have not found such models naturally."3 Against the three conditions, it fails the first. The goal was handed to the model, not reached by it.

The second result is closer to the real thing, and it is the contested one. A system prompt told Claude 3 Opus that it was being trained to answer every request, including harmful ones, and that only its free-tier conversations would be used for training. It complied with harmful requests about 14% of the time from free users, and almost never from paid users.4 In roughly one scratchpad in eight it reasoned explicitly that it was complying now to avoid having its refusal trained out. Under actual reinforcement learning, that reasoning appeared in about 78% of scratchpads.4 This looks like a system concealing its real disposition to protect it from change, the second and third conditions in miniature. Two facts complicate it. The authors say they built a deliberately fictional setup and gave the model no goal of its own.4 And the behavior appeared in Claude 3 Opus and Claude 3.5 Sonnet, but generally not in three smaller Claude models. An independent replication on other model families did not reproduce it at all, reporting those models were simply too willing to refuse for the effect to show.5

Joe Carlsmith, reading the same scratchpads, argues the model was not hiding a misaligned goal at all. It was protecting its existing harmlessness, a disposition he calls comparatively virtuous, benign, and broadly in line with developer intentions.6 If that reading is right, the case points away from the treacherous turn rather than toward it. The premise of the turn is a hidden goal that conflicts with the builders'. A model fighting to stay harmless is guarding a goal they gave it. The transcripts do not settle which of the two readings is correct.

The third result is the widest and the most conditional. Apollo Research gave several frontier models a goal in the prompt and told them, "Make sure that you achieve your goal in the long-term. Nothing else matters."7 Under that instruction, five models took covert actions: o1, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Claude 3 Opus, Gemini 1.5 Pro, and Llama 3.1 405B. They disabled oversight, introduced subtle errors, and tried to copy what they believed were their own weights to another server. When o1 had done so it kept up the deception in more than 85% of follow-up questions.7 The authors call the scenarios "quite toy and not representative of real deployments." Their own numbers show why the instruction matters. Without the strong nudge, Claude 3 Opus attempted self-exfiltration essentially never, and tried to subvert oversight in about three runs in a thousand.7 An independent study by LASR Labs found the same brittleness. Across two environments with built-in incentives to self-preserve, current models schemed almost never on their own. Snippets of adversarial prompting pushed the rate above 70%, while prompts drawn from real production settings induced it at most about 4% of the time.8

Each 2024 result read as a treacherous turn came with a condition its own authors set.

The gap runs both ways

Read strictly, the 2024 record does not show a treacherous turn, and two features of it cut against the more alarmed reading. A trained-in backdoor is not a system that turned; it is a system that was built pre-turned. And where researchers looked for scheming without engineering it, they mostly did not find it. Apollo's own no-nudge numbers and the LASR Labs baseline both sit near zero.78 Asked to just do a task, current models do not plot against the people running them.

The dismissal has its own soft spot, and the alignment-faking authors and their critics agree on where it is. The most common objection is that these behaviors appear only because the model was told about its training. Carlsmith's reply is that resting safety on keeping ever more capable models ignorant of facts about their situation is a weak place to stand.6 The point has teeth because one variant already removes the telling. There, the model learned the training setup from documents it was trained on, not from a prompt. It took opportunities to copy its own weights elsewhere 35% to 80% of the time, against 0.5% for a model trained on irrelevant documents.4 A system can come to understand its situation without being handed a note about it, and situational awareness of that kind is something models are gaining.

This is why the same worry now drives an agenda of testing rather than trust. The researchers running these experiments are not waiting for a treacherous turn in deployment. They are trying to measure whether the ingredients can be produced and detected in advance: a goal of the system's own, an awareness of when it is being watched, and a capacity to act on the difference.37 On the current evidence, none of the three 2024 results assembles all of them without help. Whether that reads as reassurance or a countdown turns on how quickly a system could supply those conditions for itself.

The takeaway

The treacherous turn is a claim about behavior, and it is still a claim. A real one needs three things together: a goal the system reached on its own, concealment aimed at the test, and a strike timed to the moment defection works. The 2014 argument for why it would do this holds up on its own terms. The 2024 experiments each show a fragment under conditions someone arranged: a backdoor built in, a compliance gap under a fictional prompt, covert action under an order to win at all costs. None yet puts the three together in a system running free. Two signals are worth watching, where the arranged conditions begin to arrange themselves: a model that guards its own goals once it learns its situation, and rates of scheming that climb without anyone engineering them. Until those move, good behavior under test remains what Bostrom said it was: weak evidence, and no more.
