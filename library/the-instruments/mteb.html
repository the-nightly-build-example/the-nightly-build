The Instruments

A model can top the MTEB average and be ordinary at retrieval

The number that sorts the MTEB leaderboard is one flat average over 58 datasets, weighting retrieval fifteen times as heavily as summarization purely because retrieval happens to have more of them.

9 min read2026-08-27

Why this matters

When a team builds a system that answers questions from its own documents, one of its first decisions is which embedding model to trust. Almost everyone makes that decision the same way: they open the MTEB leaderboard and take a model near the top. That leaderboard sorts models by a single number. This lesson opens up how that number is made, from a score on one dataset up to the average that decides the ranking, and shows the two places the average quietly bends. By the end you will know what a high MTEB rank does and does not tell you about the model you are about to build on. You will also see why the people who maintain the benchmark have spent the last year rebuilding it.

A team picks an embedding model by one number

An embedding model turns a piece of text into a vector: a list of numbers that places the text as a point in space. A search system uses those points to pull back the passages nearest a question. MTEB, the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark, is the standard way of comparing those models. In the paper that defines it, MTEB runs each frozen model across 8 task types spread over 58 datasets in 112 languages, and the authors report results for 33 models.1

Two things share the name and are worth keeping apart. The benchmark is the collection of datasets and the rules for scoring a model on them. The leaderboard is the ranked table people actually read, a public page on Hugging Face that lists models best to worst.2 The benchmark produces a score for each model. The leaderboard sorts on it. A team choosing an embedding model for a retrieval system lets that one score stand in for everything the model might do well or badly.

One average, eight different rulers

The tasks MTEB scores are not variations on one skill. Retrieval asks a model to rank the right passage above the wrong ones. Classification asks it to sort texts into labeled bins. Clustering asks it to group texts with no labels at all. Because the tasks differ, each is scored by its own metric on its own scale. Retrieval uses nDCG@10, classification uses accuracy, clustering uses v-measure, and so on through all eight.1 These numbers do not share a unit. An nDCG@10 and an accuracy measure different things on different scales, and there is no natural exchange rate between them.

The eight task types, the metric each is scored by, and how many datasets each holds.1
Task type Main metric Datasets
Bitext mining F1 2
Classification Accuracy 12
Clustering V-measure 11
Pair classification Average precision on cosine similarity 3
Reranking MAP 4
Retrieval nDCG@10 15
Semantic textual similarity Spearman correlation on cosine similarity 10
Summarization Spearman correlation on cosine similarity 1

The overall score does the simplest possible thing with these unlike numbers. It averages each dataset's main-metric score across all the datasets, with no weighting, and the leaderboard sorts on that mean.1 The headline English figure runs over 56 of the 58 datasets, dropping the two bitext-mining sets, which are multilingual. The shape is the same either way: a score on one dataset, folded in with every other, then divided by the count.

Folding scores this way treats a point of nDCG@10 on a retrieval set and a point of accuracy on a classification set as worth the same, because the average adds them and divides. Text embedding has no single dimension along which one model is simply better than another. Being good at retrieval and being good at clustering are different abilities, and a model can have one without the other. The single MTEB number exists only by averaging across that difference.

A model can lead the mean and be ordinary at retrieval

The table's last column shows the imbalance: retrieval holds 15 datasets, summarization holds 1. Nothing weights the average by how much a task matters, so it is weighted by how many datasets a task happens to have, and retrieval counts fifteen times as heavily as summarization for that reason alone. The authors say so plainly in their own limitations section.

The authors' own limitation
"Tasks in MTEB have a different amount of datasets with summarization consisting of only a single dataset. This means MTEB average scores, which are computed over all datasets, are biased towards tasks with many datasets, notably retrieval, classification and clustering."1 Muennighoff, Tazi, Magne & Reimers, MTEB paper, Appendix B

This is a property of the mean, not a hidden scandal, and it cuts in both directions. A model can climb near the top of the overall average while being no better than ordinary at retrieval, because retrieval is one slice among eight and strong scores elsewhere carry it. A model genuinely tuned for retrieval can also sit lower than it deserves, dragged down by the categories a retrieval user does not care about. One independent writeup makes the second case directly. A model tuned for retrieval and semantic similarity, "the two categories most correlated with production performance in RAG and search," can "underperform on clustering or classification, which brings down the average."3

Either way, the overall rank and the rank on the task a model is actually being chosen for are two different measurements, and the leaderboard sorts on the first. Being ranked above most other models is not the same as being the best model for one particular job. The maintainers now build the leaderboard to be filtered down to the tasks that matter to a given use, and note that a single pre-defined board "might only contain 50% of the tasks that you care about."4 The overall number was never meant to answer the retrieval question on its own. It is read as though it were.

The authors changed their minds about contamination

The imbalance was on the record from the start. The second weakness was not, and its history is the more instructive one, because the same people reversed their position on it. Contamination is the worry that a model scores well because it was trained on data too close to the test. In 2022 the MTEB authors considered it and set it aside.

The 2022 dismissal
"Our scale of experiments and that of model pre-training make controlling for data contamination challenging. Thus, we ignore overlap of MTEB datasets with model training datasets in MTEB scores. As long as enough datasets are averaged, we believe these effects to be insignificant."1 MTEB paper, 2022

By 2025 the same team, revising its own benchmark, treats contamination as a first-order problem. In an engineering paper on maintaining MTEB, the maintainers describe a subtler failure than a test answer leaking into training: models trained "using training splits from the same source as benchmark evaluation tasks."5 To measure it, a model reports which benchmark datasets it trained on, and MTEB computes a zero-shot score: the share of the benchmark the model did not train on. Their worked case is e5-mistral-7b-instruct, which scores 95% zero-shot on the legacy English benchmark, meaning it trained on only about 5% of that benchmark's datasets. It still scores well, which the authors read as real ability rather than a leak.5 The point of the exercise is the models on the other end, where a high overall score sits next to a low zero-shot score. Outside groups note that "many embedding models are partially trained on data that overlaps with MTEB datasets," which "contaminates the leaderboard."6

The rebuild answers both weaknesses without throwing the old number away. For imbalance, the multilingual expansion MMTEB keeps the plain per-category scores and adds two things on top. The first is a ranking by Borda count, a method from voting theory the maintainers argue is sturdier than a raw mean. The second is an average weighted by task category.7 The flat average still exists; it is now one view among several rather than the only one. For contamination, MMTEB is built as a zero-shot benchmark that excludes the datasets models most often train on, naming MS MARCO and Natural Questions.7 And a newer board, RTEB, keeps some datasets private and scored only by the maintainers, so that "a model with a significant performance drop between the open and the private datasets would suggest overfitting."8

None of this makes the leaderboard a trick. The MTEB average is a real, usable summary of how a model does across a wide spread of tasks, and it was built for a reason. Both weaknesses are limits on how to read the number, not evidence that the number is fake: one task is a slice of the average, and a very high score can carry a little training on the test. The maintainers took their own measure seriously enough to keep repairing it, which is a reason to trust the repaired benchmark rather than to throw the whole thing out.7

The takeaway

The number that sorts the MTEB leaderboard is a plain average. It scores a model on 58 datasets, each with its own metric on its own scale, and divides. Two things follow. The average is weighted by how many datasets each task has, not by how much the task matters. So a high overall rank does not tell you the model is strong at retrieval, the job most readers are choosing a model for. And because the same average can reward a model for training on data close to the test, a top score is not proof the model will do as well on your own documents. The people who built MTEB said the first thing themselves in 2022, reversed their earlier dismissal of the second by 2025, and rebuilt the benchmark to answer both. So take the move the maintainers now push. Filter the board to the task you actually care about, read the overall number as a rough summary rather than a verdict, and judge a model finally on how it does on your own data.
