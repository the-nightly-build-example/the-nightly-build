# Voice guide: the-instruments/task-time-horizon (01)

## How this piece should sound

This lesson explains where one number comes from. A task time horizon is a length of task, timed by people, at which a model succeeds half the time, read off a curve fitted to how often it finishes tasks of each length. The reader is quick and widely read and has never watched a benchmark or a trend line get built. Every part the number is made of, the human timings, the pass or fail at each length, the fit through those points, and the doubling across release dates, has to be constructed in front of them, in the plain and unhurried voice Matt Yglesias uses when he would rather be understood than sound impressive.

Define the number the way Ortiz-Ospina defines period life expectancy. He states the procedure and the condition it holds fixed in the same breath as the name, so the reader holds the construction before the word is used again. A task time horizon is a length at which success reaches 50%, on a particular set of tasks, timed by particular people. Where it first appears, the reader may need those conditions stated beside it, the way his Japan example repeats "under the assumption that" and spells the condition out a second time.

The 50% threshold invites a clean misreading, that a model with an N-minute horizon can do any task of N minutes. Ortiz-Ospina meets the matching misreading of life expectancy with one concrete case and a short sentence that resets what the number is. A worked case showing that a task sitting at the threshold is one the model finishes only half the time may be what keeps the horizon from being read as a promise.

Alexander's bell curve shows how to make the fit itself intuitive. He draws the scatter of study results as a shape the reader can see, one plain sentence at a time, before he names it. The success-versus-time relationship can be built the same way, from tasks of known length and whether each was finished, with the fraction finished falling as tasks get longer, so the fitted curve arrives as a description of a picture the reader already has.

The measurement and the line drawn forward from it need separate handling. On released models the horizon is a real measurement. Continued past the last release date, the doubling trend is a weaker and separate claim, and the lesson owes each its honest weight, with neither hyped nor waved away. Alexander sets five hundred economists against six hundred in the same words and lets the reader feel the standoff. The same evenness fits the distance between what the fitted trend records and what it is taken to predict.

Evans shows the last move. She ties a new fact to something the reader already knows, and she names the edge of her own knowledge in exact terms instead of a vague nod at limits. Each new part of the construction can be pinned to something the reader can already picture, and the lesson can say just as plainly where the number stops supporting claims about dates it has not reached.

## Esteban Ortiz-Ospina, "Life expectancy: what does this actually mean?"

Source: https://ourworldindata.org/life-expectancy-how-is-it-calculated-and-how-should-it-be-interpreted

> "An alternative approach consists in estimating the average length of life for a hypothetical cohort assumed to be exposed, from birth through death, to the mortality rates observed at one particular period – commonly a year. This approach leads to what is known as 'period life expectancy' and it is the much more commonly used life expectancy metric."

The number is defined by stating the exact procedure and the assumption it holds fixed, in the same breath as the name. The reader learns that a life expectancy figure is a hypothetical cohort under frozen mortality rates before the word is used again. Ortiz-Ospina is visible in the patience of it: he does not assume the familiar term is understood, and he spends a full sentence on the assumption most writers leave out.

> "For Japan, we can see that life expectancy in 2005 was 82.3 years. This means that a hypothetical cohort of infants living through the age-specific mortality of Japan in 2005 could expect to live 82.3 years, under the assumption that mortality patterns observed in 2005 remain constant throughout their lifetime."

A single real figure is given a plain reading right after the definition, so the abstract construction is attached to a concrete case. The "under the assumption that" clause repeats the condition rather than trusting the reader to carry it from the paragraph before. That repetition is the writer choosing clarity over economy.

> "For example, very few of the infants born in South Africa in 2009 will die at 52.2 years of age, as per the figures in the map above. Most will die much earlier or much later, since the risk of death is not uniform across the lifetime. Life expectancy is the average."

The likeliest misreading, that the number is the age people tend to die at, is met with a specific counter-case and then a three-word sentence that fixes what the number is. The short closing sentence corrects the reading without adding emphasis. The example is chosen because the figure, 52.2, invites exactly the mistake the passage removes.

## Scott Alexander, "Beware The Man Of One Study"

Source: https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/12/12/beware-the-man-of-one-study/

> "But there will also be random noise caused by inevitable variation and by some of the experiments being better quality than others. In the end, we might expect something looking kind of like a bell curve. The peak will be at "weakly effective", but there will be a few studies to either side."

A statistical idea, that results scatter around a true value, is built as a picture the reader can see, one sentence at a time, before any term for it is used. Alexander is visible in how little he assumes: he names the causes of the spread first, then draws the shape, then places the true answer on it. The concreteness does the teaching, and no definition is needed.

> "I think medical science is still pretty healthy, and that the consensus of doctors and researchers is more-or-less right on most controversial medical issues. (it's the uncontroversial ones you have to worry about)"

After a long stretch on how evidence gets cherry-picked, he states plainly where the process still works, which stops the piece short of blanket distrust. The parenthetical aside carries a real second thought. Crediting the thing he has spent paragraphs qualifying is the evenness a reader trusts.

> "Well, five hundred economists signed a letter to policy makers saying that the science of economics shows increasing the minimum wage would be a bad idea. That sounds like a promising consensus… ..except that six hundred economists signed a letter to policy makers saying that the science of economics shows increasing the minimum wage would be a good idea."

Two opposed figures are laid side by side in identical wording, so the reader feels the deadlock instead of being told about it. The repeated sentence frame, changed only at "bad" and "good", is what makes the reversal land. He reaches the point through the numbers.

## Julia Evans, "Get better at programming by learning how things work"

Source: https://jvns.ca/blog/learn-how-things-work/

> "I find that if I can use my newfound understanding to do something concrete like implement a new feature or fix a bug or even just write a test program that demonstrates how the thing works, it feels a LOT more real than if I just read about it."

The claim is that understanding sticks when it is tested on a concrete case, and the sentence lists what "concrete" means so the point is not left abstract. Evans is visible in the plain first-person register and the ordinary examples she reaches for. Nothing is dressed up, and the sentence is easy to follow on one read.

> "When learning a new fact, it's easy to be able to recite a sentence like "ok, there are 8 bits in a byte". That's true, but so what? What's harder (and much more useful!) is to be able to connect that information to what you already know about programming."

She separates reciting a fact from understanding it, using a fact so small the difference is unmistakable. The blunt "but so what?" is the voice of someone who remembers not yet getting it. The passage teaches by contrast between two states the reader can recognize in themselves.

> "And I know what I don't know – I know it's hard to write numerically stable linear algebra algorithms and I have no idea how to do that."

She marks the edge of her own understanding in specific terms, naming the exact thing she cannot do. Admitting it plainly costs her nothing and makes the confidence in the rest of the passage credible. The specificity keeps it from sounding like false modesty.
