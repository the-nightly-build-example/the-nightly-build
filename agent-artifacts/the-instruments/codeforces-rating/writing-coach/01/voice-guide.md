# Voice guide: the-instruments/codeforces-rating (01)

## How this piece should sound

This lesson takes apart the Codeforces rating: a single number, updated after each rated contest, that sorts competitive programmers into tiers and has lately been quoted to say how good an AI system is at writing code. The reader is quick and widely read but has never entered a contest or watched a rating get computed. Write for that reader in plain claims, the way the series does. Say what the number is and how it is made before saying what it is worth.

Teach the mechanism the way Ritchie teaches the Living Planet Index. She gives the plain definition first and then pins it to one real case, two black rhino populations she carries through the arithmetic. Where this piece explains how a rating moves after a contest, or how a placing among the other entrants becomes a number, the material may support carrying one concrete case the same way: a specific coder or system, a specific round, and the actual rating change it produced. O'Neil does the same with a scoring system that is hard to see into, turning it into a biased coin the reader can compute by hand.

Separate what the rating counts from what people take it to certify, and state the split flatly. Ritchie's plain "the LPI does not tell us X; it tells us Y" is the move. A Codeforces rating is built from where a contestant finished on a set of problems of set difficulty within a time limit, against whoever happened to enter that round. Whatever the piece finds the number does and does not support, the distinction reads clearest in that bare form.

Be exact about the choices built into the number, and name who made them, the way Gladwell handles the U.S. News weights and Robert Morse's flat answer that "we think these measures measure quality schools." A rating system embeds decisions: how far a single contest moves the number, how the difficulty of a problem is set, how a field of unequal entrants is compressed to one figure. Where a choice is doing work the reader would never guess at, the piece can state the choice plainly rather than let the number look inevitable. If the rating feeds back into the thing it is meant to measure, name that loop the way Gladwell names the reputation loop, in plain restatement the reader can follow.

Hold the judgment to the evidence, in both directions. Ritchie credits the metric's real signal while she corrects its misreading, and O'Neil calls a score a "random number generator" only once the year-to-year correlation gives her the right to. This series does not hype and does not doom. A verdict about the Codeforces rating, however plain or blunt, is owed to the arithmetic that came before it, and the reader should be able to see that arithmetic on the page.

## Malcolm Gladwell, "The Order of Things"

Source: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2011/02/14/the-order-of-things

> "A ranking can be heterogeneous, in other words, as long as it doesn’t try to be too comprehensive. And it can be comprehensive as long as it doesn’t try to measure things that are heterogeneous. But it’s an act of real audacity when a ranking system tries to be comprehensive and heterogeneous—which is the first thing to keep in mind in any consideration of U.S. News & World Report’s annual “Best Colleges” guide."

Gladwell states the rule in two plain parallel sentences and then marks the exact case that breaks it. The writing is patient. He builds the principle first on an ordinary example, a magazine's car rankings, and only then turns it on the college rankings he is really after.

> "There’s no direct way to measure the quality of an institution—how well a college manages to inform, inspire, and challenge its students. So the U.S. News algorithm relies instead on proxies for quality—and the proxies for educational quality turn out to be flimsy at best."

This is the move at the center of the piece. A rating that cannot reach the thing it claims to measure substitutes stand-ins for it, and Gladwell then grades the stand-ins. He repeats "proxies" instead of reaching for a synonym, which keeps the reader on the single idea he is testing.

> "A school like Penn State, then, can do little to improve its position. To go higher than forty-seventh, it needs a better reputation score, and to get a better reputation score it needs to be higher than forty-seventh. The U.S. News ratings are a self-fulfilling prophecy."

Gladwell lays out a circular mechanism as a loop the reader can walk around: to rise you need the score, and to get the score you need to have already risen. He resists dressing it up and lets the plain restatement do the work.

## Cathy O'Neil, "The arbitrary punishment of New York teacher evaluations"

Source: https://mathbabe.org/2015/04/02/the-arbitrary-punishment-of-new-york-teacher-evaluations/

> "The Value-Added Model for teachers (VAM), currently in use all over the country, is a terrible scoring system, as I’ve described before. It is approximately a random number generator."

O'Neil opens with the verdict, flat and early, and spends the rest of the post on the arithmetic that earns it. The bluntness is hers, and it is not loose: "random number generator" is a specific claim about the score's year-to-year correlation, which she goes on to work out.

> "Think of it as a biased coin flip, and 30% of the time – for any teacher and for any year – it lands on “ineffective”, and 70% of the time it lands on “effective.” We will ignore the other categories because they don’t matter."

She turns an opaque scoring system into something the reader can compute by hand: a biased coin, a probability, a count of years. The voice is a teacher's, deciding out loud what to keep and what to set aside, as in "We will ignore the other categories because they don’t matter."

> "This is the political power of a terrible scoring system. More than a third of teachers are being arbitrarily chosen to be punished by this opaque and unaccountable test."

After the calculation, O'Neil says in one sentence what the score does to people, with the figure carrying the weight. The judgment is strong, and it arrives after the math rather than in place of it.

## Hannah Ritchie, "Living Planet Index: what does it really mean?"

Source: https://ourworldindata.org/living-planet-index-decline

> "I sympathize with the journalists. Interpreting this metric is hard. I’m sure I’ve made similar mistakes in the past: using the terms ‘decline’, ‘lost’, and ‘fall’ interchangeably in biodiversity discussions."

Ritchie corrects a widespread misreading of a headline number without treating the people who made it as fools, and she puts herself among them. Her voice shows in that evenness. She is exact about the error while she credits how easy it is to make.

> "The Living Planet Index (LPI) measures the average change in the number of individuals across the world’s animal populations. A ‘population’ is defined as a species within a geographical area. So, despite being the same species, the African elephant in South Africa and Tanzania are counted as different populations."

The plain definition comes first, and then one concrete case pins it down: the same species counted as two populations because it lives in two places. Ritchie defines the term and immediately shows it operating on real animals, so the reader holds the idea and an example at once.

> "To be clear once again: the LPI does not tell us the number of species, populations or individuals lost; the number of extinctions that have occurred; or even the share of species that are declining. It tells us that between 1970 and 2020, on average, there was a 73% decline in population size across the 34,000 studied populations."

This is the difference between what the number measures and what people read into it, stated flatly: a list of what it does not tell you, then the single thing it does. Ritchie repeats it near the end on purpose, because it is the sentence a reader is most likely to get wrong.
