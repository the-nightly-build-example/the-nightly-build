# Voice guide: the-instruments/brier-score

## How this piece should sound

This lesson should define the Brier score the way Harford defines a base
rate: pose the wrong, intuitive read first, then produce the true number by
counting through a small population the reader can follow, so the base-rate
trap and the always-guess-the-base-rate baseline both come out of arithmetic
the reader can rerun rather than a rule the writer asserts. Harford's
blood-test paragraph is the model for the worked example the commission
calls for. State the number a reader would guess, then work the real one
from a handful of actual people, not from restating the formula in words.

The lesson also has to hold apart two things a single number hides:
calibration and resolution, or a comparable question set and an
incomparable one. Smith's handling of "real" versus "nominal" numbers is
the template for that move. Name the two terms plainly, side by side, state
the mechanism that opens a gap between them, and only then point to the
actual case where someone printed the wrong pairing as if it were the
right one.
The same order should carry into the base-rate trap itself: say what a
model that always guesses the base rate would score, and only after that
say why the number is hollow, mechanism before verdict.

The AI-forecasting case this lesson has to carry is this piece's version of
Rubinstein's P.S. 196 teacher: a real figure set against another real figure
that should not have diverged if the metric meant what people said it
meant, reported so the incredulity comes from the numbers instead of being
declared ahead of them. If the sourcing supports it, the case can close the
way Rubinstein's newspaper paragraph does: name who took the number at face
value and what they did with it, so the cost of the misreading is one
checkable event rather than a general caution about misused statistics.

When the lesson states its own verdict on a claim like "AI is approaching
human-level forecasting," Smith's "This chart is completely wrong" is the
register to match: the judgment given once, plainly, with the specific
reason in the very next clause, not softened or repeated for emphasis.
Harford's "we don't know, because we don't know the base rate" is the other
half of the same habit. Where a comparison can't be made because the
question sets or the dates differ, the lesson should say so in exactly
those plain terms rather than gesturing at uncertainty in general.

## Tim Harford, "How to Make Good Guesses"

Source: https://timharford.com/2016/03/how-to-make-good-guesses/

> "Imagine that you see someone reading the Financial Times. Would you say
> that this individual, clearly a person of discernment, is more likely to
> have a PhD or to have no college degree at all? The obvious response is
> that the FT reader has a PhD. Surely people with PhDs better exemplify
> the FT reader than people with no degree at all, at least on average —
> they tend to read more and to be more prosperous. But the obvious
> response is too hasty. First, we should ask how many people have PhDs and
> how many people have no college degree at all? In the UK, more than 75
> per cent of adults have no degree but the chance that a randomly chosen
> person has a PhD is probably less than 1 per cent."

Harford lets the reader reach the wrong answer before he names what made it
wrong. The wrongness is demonstrated with the actual UK figures, more than
75 per cent against under 1 per cent, rather than asserted, and the term
"base rate" only arrives once the reader has felt the mistake happen.

> "At a recent Financial Times event, psychologist and forecasting expert
> Philip Tetlock explained that good forecasters pay close attention to
> base rates. Whether one is forecasting whether a marriage will last, or a
> dictator will be toppled, or a company will go bankrupt, Tetlock argues
> that it's a good idea to start with the base rate. How many marriages
> last? How many dictators are toppled? How many companies go bankrupt? Of
> course, one may have excellent reasons to depart from the base rate as a
> forecast but the base rate should be the beginning of the journey."

The rule arrives attached to a named person and three concrete forecasting
questions, not as a free-standing maxim. Harford also states the limit of
his own claim within the same passage: a forecaster may have good reason to
move off the base rate, which keeps the rule from overreaching what it can
support.

> "Imagine a blood test for a dangerous disease that is 75 per cent
> accurate: if an infected person takes the test, it will detect the
> infection 75 per cent of the time but it will also give a false positive
> 25 per cent of the time for an uninfected person. Now, let's say that a
> random person takes the test and seems to be infected. What is the
> chance that he really does have the disease? The intuitive answer is 75
> per cent. But the correct answer is: we don't know, because we don't know
> the base rate. Once we know the base rate we can express the problem
> intuitively and solve it. Let's say 100 people are tested and four of
> them are actually infected. Then three will have a (correct) positive
> test, but of the 96 uninfected people, 24 (25 per cent) will have a false
> positive test. Most of the positive test results, then, are false."

The wrong, intuitive answer is stated before the true one, and the true one
comes from counting an actual 100 people rather than from restating the
rule in words. A reader can redo this arithmetic themselves and land on the
same place Harford does.

## Noah Smith, "How Not to Be Fooled by Viral Charts"

Source: https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/how-not-to-be-fooled-by-viral-charts

> "This chart is completely wrong. Although it says 'Note: Rent and income
> are inflation-adjusted' at the bottom of the screen, this is either a
> mistake or a lie. Only income is inflation-adjusted. The red rent line is
> not. A community note has since been added, pointing out the error, but
> not before Potash's tweet was able to rack up an incredible 77,000 likes
> and 17,000 reposts."

Smith names the specific error, that only one of the two lines was actually
adjusted, instead of saying the chart was misleading in general, and gives
the exact reach of the bad chart rather than calling it viral. The verdict
("either a mistake or a lie") is stated once and left there.

> "Comparing an inflation-adjusted (or 'real') economic number with one
> that's unadjusted ('nominal') is one of the easiest ways to make a graph
> that turns heads and gets clicks but which is complete B.S. If you
> compare an inflation-adjusted number to one that's not adjusted, there
> will be a big gap between them over time. Instead, to make an
> apples-to-apples comparison, you should always compare either two
> adjusted numbers or two unadjusted numbers."

The mechanism comes before the rule: Smith says why the mismatch produces a
gap, then states the fix, rather than only naming the mistake and moving
on. The two terms it depends on, "real" and "nominal," are defined in the
same sentence that uses them.

> "A lot of people know that averages (means) are susceptible to outliers,
> such as when Elon Musk walks into a bar and immediately the average
> wealth goes up by a factor of 100. So a lot of people who are concerned
> about inequality have taken to demanding that everyone use medians
> instead of averages for everything. But averages are often perfectly
> fine measures — if you're looking at average calorie consumption, or
> average time spent playing video games, there's really no need to use a
> median. There are only 24 hours in a day, so there's no Elon Musk of
> video game time (no, not even that one friend of yours)."

The joke depends on the actual mechanism of an average, that one large
outlier can move it, and would not survive swapping out the named person.
Smith also states the limit of his own point directly, that averages are
often fine, instead of leaving the reader with only the failure case.

## Gary Rubinstein, "Analyzing Released NYC Value-Added Data Part 2"

Source: https://garyrubinstein.wordpress.com/2012/02/28/analyzing-released-nyc-value-added-data-part-2/

> "Looking through the data I noticed teachers, like a 5th grade teacher at
> P.S. 196 who scored 97 out of 100 in language arts and 2 out of 100 in
> math. This is with the same students in the same year! How can a teacher
> be so good and so bad at the same time? Any evaluation system in which
> this can happen is extremely flawed, of course, but I wanted to explore
> if this was a major outlier or if it was something quite common."

The claim is carried by one named school and two numbers set against each
other, not by an adjective. Rubinstein's own question, asked and left open
for a moment, keeps the incredulity tied to this specific pair of scores
rather than turning into a general complaint before the reader has seen the
scores that earn it.

> "Out of 5,675 elementary school teachers, the average difference between
> the two scores was a whopping 22 points. One out of six teachers, or
> approximately 17%, had a difference of 40 or more points. One out of 25
> teachers, which was 250 teachers altogether, had a difference of 60 or
> more points, and, believe it or not, 110 teachers, or about 2% (that's
> one out of fifty!) had differences of 70 or more points. At the risk of
> seeming repetitive, let me repeat that this was the same teacher, the
> same year, with the same kids."

Each sentence counts the same finding a different way: an average, then a
share past one threshold, then a share past a higher one. That gives the
reader the shape of the whole distribution rather than a single summary
figure. Repeating "the same teacher, the same year, with the same kids" at
the end keeps the number pinned to what was supposed to stay constant.

> "Rather than report about these obvious ways to check how invalid these
> metrics are and how shameful it is that these scores have already been
> used in tenure decisions... newspapers are treating these scores like
> they are meaningful. The New York Post searched for the teacher with the
> lowest score and wrote an article about 'the worst teacher in the city'
> with her picture attached. The New York Times must have felt they were
> taking the high-road when they did a similar thing but, instead, found
> the 'best' teachers based on these ratings."

The cost of the bad number is made concrete by naming which two papers used
it and what each one did with it, not by saying the coverage was unfair.
Putting the tabloid's worst-teacher story next to the paper-of-record's
best-teachers story, doing the same thing from opposite ends, makes the
point without Rubinstein having to add a verdict on top of it.
