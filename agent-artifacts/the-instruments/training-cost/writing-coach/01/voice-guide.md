# Voice guide: the-instruments/training-cost

## How this piece should sound

This lesson explains an arithmetic construction, GPU-hours times an assumed
rental price, and then a real event where a market treated that arithmetic as
something it never claimed to be. Both halves have the same material to work
with: a number the reader can watch get built, and a case where other people
didn't watch it get built before acting on it.

The multiplication can happen on the page, once, with the real figures: the
2.788 million GPU-hours and the assumed $2 rate meeting in front of the
reader and producing $5.576 million there, rather than being stated as
inputs and left to combine off the page. Joseph Politano does this kind of
thing with an abstract accounting rule, "inventory growth counts as
investment," by turning it into a specific Georgia peach moving from harvest
to warehouse. Whatever the report's own breakdown gives for pre-training,
context extension, and post-training has room for the same treatment where
it earns its place.

Before the figure gets unpacked into what it leaves out, one sentence can
say what a training-cost number is for, what "$X million to train" gets used
to judge, the way Brian Potter states why cost is worth tracking before he
starts differencing indexes against inflation. A sentence like that is what
earns the unpacking after it.

The $5.576 million figure can be credited with its real, sourced use before
the piece turns to what it can't support. Justin Sandefur's paragraph on
cost-effectiveness analysis credits the method with "good intellectual
discipline" before it turns on "but" and shows where the discipline broke
down; the GPU-hour count and the $2 assumption, both real and reported, have
the same kind of standing available to them before the piece gets to what a
reader who takes the total as an all-in budget would be missing.

Where the piece reaches the Nvidia selloff, the reported figure and the
market-value loss can sit near each other and let the size of the gap do the
work, the way Sandefur sets the textbook counterfactual next to the actual
one, a 0.1 percent line in a federal budget, and leaves the distance between
them for the reader to register unassisted. The date, the percentage, and
the dollar loss are available as their own figures, the way Politano names
2001 and 2020 as specific years: a reader can look up 27 January 2025 and
find the same numbers this piece gives.

The sentences doing the technical unpacking can stay short and literal.
Potter's sentence on quality adjustment states the omission and then the
mechanism in one breath, with nothing dressing the omission up as a bigger
claim than the arithmetic supports; that same discipline is available to a
passage explaining that a training-run total excludes prior research,
architecture experiments, data, and salaries. What the report says is
missing can be stated and then left, without editorializing on the size of
the gap.

The reader this lesson assumes has never opened a GPU pricing page and does
not need the arithmetic simplified to follow it. GPU-hours, H800, and the
dollar figures can stand as themselves, the way these three writers let DALY,
a fixed-budget assumption, and inventory accounting stand as themselves,
defined once in plain words at first use and trusted afterward.

## Brian Potter, "Construction Costs Rarely Fall"

Source: https://www.construction-physics.com/p/construction-costs-rarely-fall

> "Cost is a good improvement metric because it directly tracks what we actually care about: we would like the costs of building housing, buildings, and infrastructure to fall and become more affordable, and we basically care about more abstract measures like productivity to the extent that they're a proxy for costs."

Before he defines the metric he is about to spend the piece measuring, he
states in one sentence why a reader should care about it, then works from the
concrete goal, cheaper housing, down to the more abstract measure,
productivity, rather than the reverse. The colon does real work: everything
after it cashes out the claim before it.

> "It's not always straightforward to tell whether an index is an output index or an input index, because exactly how indexes are constructed can be somewhat opaque. An index that initially appears as if it's an output index, because it apparently tracks changes in a particular type of construction (like new apartment buildings), may actually function more like an input index if it is constructed from price changes in inputs specific to that type of construction."

He admits the classification he is about to use is not clean, then shows
exactly where it breaks, an index that looks like one type but is built from
another type's inputs, instead of asserting a taxonomy and moving past it.
The hedge is specific enough that a reader could check it against a real
index.

> "In particular, it can be difficult to adjust cost indexes for quality; a modern building might cost more per square foot, but be built to higher standards or otherwise have higher performance than an older building, which looking only at changes in costs won't capture."

The sentence names the specific thing the number misses, quality, and then
gives the mechanism: a building that costs more for a real reason a price
alone won't show. Nothing here is inflated into a bigger claim than the
arithmetic supports.

## Justin Sandefur, "PEPFAR and the Costs of Cost-Benefit Analysis"

Source: https://asteriskmag.com/issues/05/pepfar-and-the-costs-of-cost-benefit-analysis

> "Oster's argument was not that the lives of those with HIV weren't worth the cost — that retroviral drug prices exceeded the 'value of a statistical life,' as economists might phrase it — but rather that if we take the budget as fixed, the money could do more good if spent on other health programs."

The sentence separates two arguments that get conflated in casual retelling
and names the second one's hidden premise, a fixed budget, in the same breath
as stating it. The economist's term in the middle is defined in passing
rather than left to sit there unexplained.

> "Economists are trained to look for trade-offs. This is good intellectual discipline. Pursuing 'Investment A' means forgoing 'Investment B.' But in many real-world cases, it's not at all obvious that the realistic alternative to big new spending proposals is similar levels of big new spending on some better program. The realistic counterfactual might be nothing at all."

Four short sentences build the standard case for a way of thinking before the
fifth, longer and turning on "but," takes it apart. He grants the method its
due before showing where it fails, and the short final sentence lands because
the sentences before it earned the room to build toward it.

> "A broader lesson here, perhaps, is about getting counterfactuals right. In comparative cost-effectiveness analysis, the counterfactual to AIDS treatment is the best possible alternative use of that money to save lives. In practice, the actual alternative might simply be the status quo, no PEPFAR, and a 0.1% reduction in the fiscal year 2004 federal budget."

He states the textbook version of the comparison a cost-effectiveness number
assumes, then replaces it with what the comparison actually was in this case,
a specific and small line item. The two versions sit next to each other so
the reader sees the gap between them without being told there is one.

## Joseph Politano, "Are We In a Recession?"

Source: https://www.apricitas.io/p/are-we-in-a-recession

> "As a bit of background, growth in private business inventories are counted as investments in the GDP data. If Georgia peaches are harvested in August, canned in September, and sit in the company's warehouse until December, then GDP data accounts for the peach's production in August as a boost in private inventories."

A rule of GDP accounting that would otherwise read as bureaucratic is
followed immediately by a specific case with a specific product, month, and
location. The example does the defining that the abstract sentence alone
could not.

> "The NBER Business Cycle Dating Committee determines when a recession is in the US, and they do not just follow the 2Q rule, because any single-data-point test for a recession will inevitably be flawed. The 2001 recession did not have two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth, nor did the 2020 recession—and there is one instance of two quarters of negative GDP growth occurring without an official recession."

He states why the popular two-quarters rule fails in the abstract, a
single-data-point test, and then proves it with three specific cases rather
than repeating the assertion more emphatically. Each case is a named year,
not a category, so the claim can be checked.

> "This essentially measures total consumption and fixed investment by private American consumers and businesses—so it excludes volatility in net exports, inventories, and government spending. As a result, it tends to only decline during periods of significant slowdown or recessions—as in 2001 and 2008."

He states what one number leaves out before he states what follows from
leaving it out, in that order, which is why the second sentence reads as a
consequence rather than a separate claim. The two years anchor an abstract
description to something the reader can look up.
