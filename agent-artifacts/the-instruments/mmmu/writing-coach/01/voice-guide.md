# Voice guide: the-instruments/mmmu

## How this piece should sound

This is a lesson about one number: the percentage a model prints when it reports its MMMU score. The reader is quick and reads widely but has never opened a benchmark, so the prose carries the whole explanation itself and never leans on jargon to sound authoritative. Hold the register Matt Yglesias holds when he explains something he understands well: plain claims, real figures, nothing announced before it is shown. The three exemplars below all explain how a number is made to readers who do not build the thing being measured, and all three manage it without hype and without sneering at the number.

Ritchie's energy piece is the closest model for the body's core work. She takes one everyday word, "energy," and shows in her opening that it names several different measurements; then she walks the arithmetic that turns solar generation into a "primary energy" figure and states the distortion in one flat sentence, inflated by a factor of around 2.5. MMMU's billed word is "multimodal," and the lesson has the same job with it: to follow how a model's answers become a single accuracy percentage and let the reader see what that percentage is counting. Her worked comparison, where the same kilowatt-hours become different shares depending on the accounting choice, is the move to study most closely, because it moves one variable at a time and leaves each step checkable.

Alexander's essay is the model for making a claim about a number concrete before any conclusion is drawn from it. He invents one drug and imagines the studies piling up around the truth, with plain stand-in figures, and his "Or maybe... Or maybe" run pulls a single label, effectiveness, apart into the separate questions it was combining. When the lesson explains what an MMMU score can and cannot support, that kind of patient unbundling does more than a flat assertion that the number is flawed.

Dan Luu's piece is the model for the stretch where a measurement meets the world. He stays concrete and gives the figures, the braking distance in feet and the manufacturer that changed its product after a number was published, and he treats the incentives around a measurement as part of the report rather than a moral added on top. The commission asks for a real case where an MMMU number misled people; a case reported Luu's way, with the figures named and whose they are, will carry its own judgment without a verdict laid over it.

Each of the three reaches for a specific case before a general claim, Ritchie's coal and lightbulb, Alexander's one invented drug, Luu's braking distance, and the MMMU lesson has the same materials available in the benchmark's disciplines, its image types, and the arithmetic that grades an answer. The word the whole lesson turns on is the one in MMMU's own billing, that a high score means a model can see and reason across expert subjects. Everything the body teaches about how the percentage is built is there so the reader can say, by the end, exactly how much of that billing the number supports.

## Hannah Ritchie, "How primary energy is measured has changed across our charts"

Source: https://ourworldindata.org/primary-energy-measurement-change

> But when people talk about “energy”, they’re not always talking about the same thing. There are different ways of measuring energy, from the very top of the chain — for example, coal going into a power plant — to the final link in the chain — the light that eventually comes out of a lightbulb.

Ritchie opens by taking a word the reader uses every day and showing it holds several distinct measurements, from coal at the top of the chain to light from a bulb at the end. The two concrete endpoints do the teaching; she does not define "primary energy" in the abstract, she points at where in a real chain it sits. This is a patient teacher picking the plainest possible pair of examples and trusting them.

> That means that 1 kWh of electricity from coal, 1 kWh from gas, and 1 kWh from solar will look very different when measured in primary energy terms. It would be equivalent to 3 kWh of coal, 2 kWh of gas, and 1 kWh of solar. Expressed as a share of primary energy, coal would make up half of our energy mix, gas one-third, and solar one-sixth. Even though the electricity we get out — and can actually use — is split evenly between coal, gas, and solar, each makes up one-third.

Here she runs one physical fact, equal electricity from three sources, through the accounting and shows it produces unequal shares, with every number on the page. The reader can follow each step and check it, so the surprising result reads as earned rather than asserted. Ritchie's control shows in keeping a single variable moving while everything else holds still.

> To get this figure, it divides renewable and nuclear electricity generation by a typical thermal power plant efficiency factor, which is around 38% to 41%. So, if a country generates 100 terawatt-hours (TWh) of electricity from solar, that amount is divided by 40% to get a “fossil equivalent” of 250 TWh.
>
> This means renewable energy figures are all inflated by a factor of around 2.5.

She walks the arithmetic that converts solar generation into a "fossil equivalent," then states the upshot in a single flat sentence. The plainness of that closing line is the point: she has done the work to earn it, so she does not dress it up. Watching a writer state a strong conclusion in the flattest words available is the habit to take from her.

## Dan Luu, "Measurement, benchmarking, and data analysis are underrated"

Source: https://danluu.com/why-benchmark/

> The typical response that I've seen when a catastrophic bug is reported is that the project maintainers will assume that the bug report is incorrect (and you can see many examples of this if you look at responses from the first few years of Kyle's work). When the reporter doesn't have a repro for the bug, which is quite common when it comes to distributed systems, the bug will be written off as non-existent.

Luu sets out a sequence of reactions as a plain ordered account, each stated flatly and qualified where it needs qualifying. The parenthetical pointing to the record, that you can go read the responses yourself, is where he shows up: he does not ask to be trusted, he says where to check. Nothing in the sentence reaches for a verdict, and the described mechanism does all the work.

> after Consumer Reports and Car and Driver found that the Tesla Model 3 had extremely long braking distances (152 ft. from 60mph and 196 ft. from 70mph), Tesla updated the algorithms used to modulate the brakes, which improved braking distances enough that Tesla went from worst in class to better than average

A full cause and effect fits in one sentence, and every load-bearing part is a figure: the two braking distances, the before-and-after of a measurement changing a product. Luu never says the measurement mattered; he gives the numbers that show it and stops. That restraint, reporting the figure and letting it make the point, is the thing to see here.

## Scott Alexander, "Beware The Man Of One Study"

Source: https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/12/12/beware-the-man-of-one-study/

> Suppose a certain drug is weakly effective against a certain disease. After a few years, a bunch of different research groups have gotten their hands on it and done all sorts of different studies. In the best case scenario the average study will find the true result – that it’s weakly effective.

Alexander makes an abstract statistical point tangible by inventing one concrete drug and imagining the studies collect around the truth. He commits to a plain stand-in result rather than talking about variation in the abstract, so the reader is holding a picture before any conclusion arrives. He builds the concrete case first and reasons from it second.

> But maybe the drug is more effective in bipolar i than in bipolar ii (Depakote, for example)
>
> Or maybe the drug is very effective against bipolar mania, but much less effective against bipolar depression (Depakote again).
>
> Or maybe the drug is a good acute antimanic agent, but less effective at maintenance treatment (let’s stick with Depakote).

The escalating "Or maybe... Or maybe" takes a single label, effectiveness, and pulls it into the separate questions it was quietly combining, each pinned to a real drug. The running parenthetical, "Depakote, for example," "Depakote again," "let's stick with Depakote," is Alexander on the page, holding a dry joke while the point sharpens. It shows a label being taken apart without any announcement that it is about to be.
