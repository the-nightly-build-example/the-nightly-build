# Commission: the-instruments/imo-gold

## Assignment

Teach the reader how the number behind "an AI won a gold medal at the
International Math Olympiad" is actually made, and what it can and cannot
support. One lesson on the lesson template for The Instruments: one measurement,
explained step by step, with at least one real case where it misled people.

## The measurement and the angle

The headline number is a medal, and under it a point total: six problems, seven
points each, forty-two possible, with the gold cutoff set each year by the
scores of the human contestants. The lesson should make the reader see that this
is a graded score, not a checked answer. Human markers, called coordinators,
read a full written proof against a rubric and award partial credit. That is a
different kind of measurement from the math benchmarks the reader may already
know, where a final answer is matched against a key.

Walk the number from problem to medal, and report honestly:

- How IMO scoring works: problems, points, coordinators, and how the medal
  thresholds are fixed relative to human performance that year.
- The 2024 result: which system (the DeepMind combination of AlphaProof and
  AlphaGeometry 2), how many points, what medal that reached, and under what
  conditions. Pin the conditions that change the reading: whether it worked from
  formalized statements a human translated, whether it ran within the human time
  limit, and who graded it.
- The 2025 results, where the number splits. At least two labs announced a
  gold-level score. Establish, from the primary announcements and from the IMO
  organizers' own statements, which result was produced and graded under the
  IMO's official coordination and which was self-reported and self-graded
  outside it, and what the organizers asked of labs about timing. The two golds
  are not the same measurement, and that is the lesson's real case of a number
  misleading its readers.

Then say what a gold score certifies and what it does not: it certifies solving
particular competition problems under particular conditions, and the conditions
(time, tools, whether problems were formalized, who graded) decide what the
medal means. It does not certify general mathematical research ability. Where
confidence outran the process, name the gap.

## Boundary against the published course

The Instruments already teaches answer-checked math benchmarks. Contrast with
them, do not repeat them, and link them as Background:

- `frontiermath` — research-level problems graded by final answer, with the
  conflict-of-interest angle. Different measurement.
- `aime` and `gsm8k` — short-answer math benchmarks matched against a key. The
  contrast that carries this lesson is proof-grading by humans versus
  answer-checking, so name it and move on.

No published lesson covers a human-proof-graded competition score, so the
measurement here is new ground for the course.

## Template, sources, production

- Template: lesson. Word band 1200–2200. Bookends are citation-exempt; every
  body section carries its own citations.
- Source policy: at least 8 sources, at least 4 primary and at least 1
  secondary. Primary includes the labs' own announcements, the IMO's official
  problems and results, and on-record statements by IMO organizers and
  coordinators. Independent reporting is secondary. Every contested figure and
  every claim about who graded what needs the primary that owns it; an
  accusation about the grading process needs two independent confirmations by
  parties in a position to know.
- Production policy (balanced, model tier "capable", nothing `required`):
  researcher high, writing-coach low, writer medium, editor high. Roles run as
  isolated subagents on the runtime's default capable-tier model. No deviation
  recorded.

## Recent shapes to break

Recent The Instruments lessons open on "what X is counting" or "a quiz score,
reported as..." and close on a bare "what a lower score buys" assessment. Deks
state a finding with one number and can drift toward the comma-triad mold. Do
not inherit the opener mold, the grading-only closer, or the comma-and heading
join. Headings are concrete and varied, in this lesson's own nouns. The bookend
bands and the Sources heading are the only mandatory fixtures.
