# Voice guide: the-instruments/auroc (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a lesson that teaches one measurement, the area under the ROC curve, to a
reader who is quick but holds no statistics, and then turns to a real case where a
high AUROC misled the people who trusted it. Hold the register Gerd Gigerenzer holds
in the BMJ: a term of art enters as a plain definition in the sentence where it first
appears, before it is ever used to judge anything. When sensitivity, specificity, or
the area under the ROC curve arrives, the reader should be able to say what it counts
before the piece asks what it is worth. Gigerenzer states flatly that these
conditional ratios "cloud the minds of many people, including health professionals";
this lesson can be just as plain that the number is slippery, and just as unhurried
in working it back to something a reader can hold. Let the completeness of the
explanation carry the authority. The reader trusts the piece because nothing was
skipped.

The first thing the lesson owes a reader who fears math is to make one number
approachable. Gigerenzer disarms a doctor, and Nuzzo handles the odds behind a P
value, by turning a rate into something countable and a probability into an everyday
event. Where an AUROC rate is doing the work, Gigerenzer's move of breaking a
population into whole people who test one way or the other is available. Where an
abstract probabilistic claim needs grounding, what a score near 0.9 does and does not
promise about one patient, Nuzzo's headache-and-tumour comparison shows how an
everyday analogy can carry a Bayesian idea before the reader ever meets a formula.
AUROC is a statement about correctly ranking a pair, not about being right on a single
case. If the reader is to feel that difference, it likely needs a concrete pair or a
small worked count rather than a definition alone.

The Instruments desk teaches where a number is made, and Spiegelhalter's reality
check on the £5.3 billion figure is the clearest model of that work. He states the
method in plain words, then walks the arithmetic one concrete step at a time and
holds the total against the population it describes. An AUROC is built from a test's
scores across everyone it was tried on. Showing how the curve is traced as the
decision threshold moves, and then asking what that curve is worth once the tool
meets a population with a different prevalence, is the same discipline. Keep the ratio
honest about its denominator: a score that separates cases and non-cases well on the
study sample can mean something different for the patients a hospital actually admits.

When the lesson reaches the case where the number misled, the figures should deflate
the claim the way Nuzzo deflates the online-dating study, "the effects were actually
tiny" followed by the actual before-and-after numbers, rather than a sentence
announcing that the tool failed. Spiegelhalter follows his own arithmetic to an
implication that cannot be true and registers the judgment with restraint. A verdict
on the tool is welcome here on the same terms, earned by the numbers and pressed no
harder than they allow. Any figure the reader cannot scale on their own can be
anchored to one they already hold, the way Spiegelhalter sets a slice of GDP beside
agriculture.

## Gerd Gigerenzer, "What are natural frequencies? Doctors need to find better ways to communicate risk to patients"

Source: https://www.bmj.com/content/343/bmj.d6386 (freely accessible copy: https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2099208_9/component/file_3562683/content)

> "What is a natural frequency? It is a joint frequency of two events, such as the number of patients with disease and who have a positive test result, and is an alternative to presenting the same information in conditional probabilities, such as sensitivities and specificities. Conditional probabilities tend to cloud the minds of many people, including health professionals, as the following problem illustrates (for convenience, probabilities are expressed in percentages)."

Gigerenzer defines the term in the same breath he introduces it, then says plainly who
struggles with the alternative. The parenthesis is a true aside the sentence survives
without. He is visible in the flat confidence that a working doctor can be told,
without softening it, that a standard tool clouds their thinking.

> "Natural frequencies facilitate insight. Even 10 year olds can determine the positive predictive value when given natural frequencies, often to the astonishment of their teachers, but are helpless when given conditional probabilities."

The short opening sentence states the claim, and the long sentence behind it carries a
concrete and surprising fact that proves it. Gigerenzer reaches for children and their
astonished teachers rather than an abstraction, so the reader believes the point
because the example is specific.

> "It does not matter to a computer program whether the input is conditional probabilities or natural frequencies, but to a human being it clearly does. Healthcare providers need to know how to represent information so that their patients can actually understand what it means."

He draws a plain line between what a machine reads and what a person needs, then lands
on a concrete requirement rather than a sentiment. The care for the reader on the far
end of the number runs through the whole piece.

## Regina Nuzzo, "Scientific method: Statistical errors"

Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/506150a (freely accessible copy: https://sciences.ucf.edu/biology/d4lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/23/2017/03/Nuzzo2014.pdf)

> "For a brief moment in 2010, Matt Motyl was on the brink of scientific glory: he had discovered that extremists quite literally see the world in black and white. The results were “plain as day”, recalls Motyl, a psychology PhD student at the University of Virginia in Charlottesville."

Nuzzo opens on one named person with something concrete at stake, and the colon lets
the promise of glory sit right against the plain finding. The voice is a reporter's: it
trusts the story to pull the reader in and adds no adjective the facts have not earned.

> "That requires another piece of information: the odds that a real effect was there in the first place. To ignore this would be like waking up with a headache and concluding that you have a rare brain tumour — possible, but so unlikely that it requires a lot more evidence to supersede an everyday explanation such as an allergic reaction."

She turns an abstract point about prior probability into a bodily event anyone has
lived, a headache and the leap to the worst explanation. The comparison does the
teaching, and the em-dash aside keeps the qualification honest without slowing the
sentence down.

> "That might have sounded impressive, but the effects were actually tiny: meeting online nudged the divorce rate from 7.67% down to 5.96%, and barely budged happiness from 5.48 to 5.64 on a 7-point scale."

Nuzzo grants the impression first, then spends the colon on the actual figures, and the
small verbs, nudged and budged, size the effect exactly. She is visible in the refusal
to let a significant result sound larger than the numbers behind it.

## David Spiegelhalter, "Is prostitution really worth £5.7 billion a year?"

Source: https://theconversation.com/is-prostitution-really-worth-5-7-billion-a-year-33497

> "Some of this is because the prostitution market is now considered as part of our national accounts, contributing an extra £5.3 billion to GDP at 2009 prices, which is 0.35% of GDP – half that of agriculture. But is this a reasonable estimate?"

Spiegelhalter states the official figure and its scale before he doubts it, anchoring an
abstract slice of GDP to a comparison the reader can hold, agriculture. The four-word
question then turns the whole piece without raising its voice.

> "As always, it’s best to do a simple reality check. The ONS assumptions come to around 75m visits a year. Let’s say 60m are from locals rather than foreign visitors, which is more than a million a week. There are around 20m men aged between 18 and 65 in the UK (taking an arbitrary upper limit), so this would mean that on average each of them buys sex three times a year."

He names the method in plain words, then does it in front of the reader, one round
number at a time, until an official total is standing next to the population it
implies. The reasoning is fully shown, and nothing is asserted that the arithmetic has
not reached.

> "In fact the proportion who pay for sex each year will probably be less than 2%, which means that less than 400,000 men are taking up more than a million visits each week – that’s around once every three days for each of the 400,000. I am no expert on the behaviour of this sub group, but this does seem rather high, to say the least"

The arithmetic arrives at an implication that cannot be true, and Spiegelhalter says so
with a deliberately small hedge rather than a flourish. He does not raise his voice to
deliver the verdict, and the phrase "rather high, to say the least" carries what a
louder writer would overstate.
