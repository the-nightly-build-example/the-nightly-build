# Voice guide: the-instruments/inception-score (01)

## How this piece should sound

This lesson teaches one number, the Inception Score, to a reader who is quick and
widely read but has never watched a metric get built. Hold the register plain and
concrete, in the voice of someone who understands the score well and is walking a
newcomer through it. Explain each step of how the number is produced at the point
you reach it, and give any term the reader is not assumed to hold a plain
definition in the sentence that first uses it. All three writers below are
explaining a measurement to a general reader, and none of them lifts the register
to signal that the material is hard. That restraint is the register to match.

When the lesson lays out how the score is computed, from what inputs and by what
procedure, the material may call for the kind of concreteness Allen Downey and
Cathy O'Neil use. Downey follows a single runner into the middle of a relay to
show where a skewed sample comes from. O'Neil renders a policing model's output as
squares "each one just the size of two football fields." A procedure described in
things the reader can count and picture stays checkable, where an abstract summary
of the same procedure asks the reader to take it on faith.

The center of the lesson is what the Inception Score can and cannot support.
Downey's class-size passage sets two true averages beside each other, 56 and 31,
and says plainly that both can be right. His next line is the teaching: a number
shaped by how it was gathered can still be the right one to report, so long as
"you have to be clear about what you are measuring and how you report it." Where
this lesson separates what the score's procedure actually measures from what a
reader assumes it measures, that stance is available, and the plain worked numbers
carry more than any characterization of them would.

When the lesson reaches the case where the Inception Score misled researchers,
Gould offers a way in. He states the wrong reading of a statistic in the reader's
own words, "I will probably be dead in eight months," before he takes it apart,
and he sets a mean and a median side by side so the reader sees two true figures
describing the same people. Naming plainly the reading of the score that turned
out not to hold, as the people relying on it at the time would have put it, is one
honest way to open that case. The correction then comes from what the procedure
does. A reversal written for effect is the failure to steer around here.

O'Neil supplies the shape for showing a blind spot. She credits what the policing
model genuinely does before she shows where it fails, and she exposes what it
misses by imagining it turned on the crimes it never looks at. A lesson meaning to
show a blind spot in the Inception Score can move the same way: say first what the
number really does well, then show what its procedure never sees. A verdict
reached in that order is earned, and the reader can follow every step that led to
it.

## Stephen Jay Gould, "The Median Isn't the Message"

Source: https://people.umass.edu/biep540w/pdf/Stephen%20Jay%20Gould.pdf

> "A politician in power might say with pride, "The mean income of our citizens is
> $15,000 per year." The leader of the opposition might retort, "But half our
> citizens make less than $10,000 per year." Both are right, but neither cites a
> statistic with impassive objectivity. The first invokes a mean, the second a
> median."

Gould puts two true numbers next to each other and lets the reader see that they
describe the same citizens. He explains the difference between a mean and a median
without a definition-shaped sentence, by staging it as an argument between two
people who each pick the figure that suits them. What is visible here is a teacher
who trusts a small concrete scene to do the work.

> "I suspect that most people, without training in statistics, would read such a
> statement as "I will probably be dead in eight months" - the very conclusion
> that must be avoided, since it isn't so, and since attitude matters so much."

Gould writes out the wrong reading of the number in the plain words a frightened
person would actually use, and only then explains why the number does not say it.
The misreading is named exactly, so the correction that follows has something real
to push against. He is present as someone who remembers what the statistic sounded
like before he understood it.

> "The distribution of variation had to be right skewed, I reasoned. After all,
> the left of the distribution contains an irrevocable lower boundary of zero
> (since mesothelioma can only be identified at death or before). ... But the
> upper (or right) half can extend out for years and years, even if nobody
> ultimately survives."

Gould shows his reasoning one step at a time, and each step is a fact the reader
can check rather than a conclusion handed over. He is thinking on the page, and
the reader gets to watch a shape emerge from constraints instead of being told the
shape. The plainness of "years and years" keeps a technical point about
distributions readable.

## Allen Downey, "The Inspection Paradox is Everywhere"

Source: https://allendowney.blogspot.com/2015/08/the-inspection-paradox-is-everywhere.html

> "A common example is the apparent paradox of class sizes. Suppose you ask
> college students how big their classes are and average the responses. The result
> might be 56. But if you ask the school for the average class size, they might say
> 31. It sounds like someone is lying, but they could both be right."

Downey leads with two specific numbers and the reader's own suspicion that one of
them must be false, then holds that suspicion open instead of resolving it right
away. The concreteness is doing the teaching: 56 and 31 are easier to hold than
any general statement about biased averages. Downey is visible as someone who
enjoys the moment before the trick is explained.

> "That's not necessarily a mistake. If you want to quantify student experience,
> the average across students might be a more meaningful statistic than the average
> across classes. But you have to be clear about what you are measuring and how you
> report it."

Downey refuses the easy verdict that a skewed number is simply wrong, and instead
ties the number's value to the question it is meant to answer. The sentence is
careful without being hedged, and it draws the line the whole piece depends on.
This is a practitioner who has watched people report the wrong average and wants
the reader not to.

> "I ran the sixth leg for my team, so when I started running, I jumped into the
> middle of the race. After a few miles I noticed something unusual: when I
> overtook slower runners, they were usually much slower; and when faster runners
> passed me, they were usually much faster."

Downey grounds an abstract sampling effect in something that happened to his own
legs, and the reader can feel the effect before it is named. He builds the
observation from plain physical detail, one runner at a time, so the general point
arrives already believed. The first person here is a way of showing the reader
where to stand, not a digression.

## Cathy O'Neil, "Justice in the age of big data"

Source: https://ideas.ted.com/justice-in-the-age-of-big-data/

> "The program processed historical crime data and calculated, hour by hour, where
> crimes were most likely to occur. The Reading policemen could view the program's
> conclusions as a series of squares, each one just the size of two football
> fields. If they spent more time patrolling these squares, there was a good chance
> they would discourage crime."

O'Neil turns a software model's output into a physical object the reader can see,
squares the size of two football fields laid over a city. She explains what the
system does by describing what a user of it actually looks at, which keeps the
mechanism concrete. O'Neil is visible as a modeler who knows the tool from the
inside and will not let it stay abstract.

> "Predictive crime models like PredPol have their virtues. Unlike the
> crime-stoppers in Steven Spielberg's dystopian movie Minority Report, the cops
> don't track down people before they commit crimes."

O'Neil credits what the model gets right before she turns to what it gets wrong,
and she draws the boundary of her own criticism precisely. The reference lands
because it clears away the objection the reader was about to make. She is the kind
of critic who states the strongest version of the thing she is about to argue
against.

> "Just imagine if police enforced their zero-tolerance strategy in finance. They
> would arrest people for even the slightest infraction, whether it was chiseling
> investors on 401ks, providing misleading guidance or committing petty frauds. My
> point is that police make choices about where they direct their attention. Today
> they focus almost exclusively on the poor."

O'Neil exposes what the model never looks at by imagining it pointed somewhere
else, and the reframing does more than a direct accusation could. The specific
list of financial infractions makes the unwatched territory as concrete as the
watched squares from earlier. Her judgment is plain and she reaches it in the
open, from things she has shown the reader.
