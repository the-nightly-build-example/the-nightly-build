# Voice guide: the-instruments/comet-score

## How this piece should sound

This lesson explains a number that comes out of a trained model, not a formula,
to a reader who has no standing reason to trust or distrust either kind of
number. Fung's post on the CTE study is the clearest model for the piece's
central move: he doesn't tell the reader in general terms that "25 percent"
needs context, he walks the figure down from the headline to the 235 donated
brains it actually describes, one sentence at a time, before saying anything
about what the number can support. A COMET score can take the same walk: the
number taken down through what produced it (which model version scored it,
whether a reference was used, which language pair and test set it came from)
the way Fung takes 878 down to 235.

O'Neil's habit of stating a mechanism as a flat, unhedged sentence right after
she has earned it with a specific figure — the 24% correlation lands first,
then "nobody really understands how it works" — is worth the lesson's
attention wherever it explains what training on human judgments does and does
not give the model. A plain sentence about what the model does or doesn't
know can carry that weight on its own once a fact sits in the sentence just
before it, with no qualifier standing in to do the earning instead.

The piece has a real misuse case to tell, at least one moment when a COMET
number was read as more than it could support and something followed from
that. O'Neil's move in the same post is to tie the cheating scandal directly
back to the specific property of the model she had already named: its
opacity. The mechanism explains the misreading; it sits inside the sentence
that reports the cost, not beside it.

Fung's joke about the reporter's "no assumption" being the assumption is
useful for a different reason: it depends entirely on the specific claim under
attack and would not survive being moved to another argument. That is the
shape skepticism can take here, aimed at one named phrase or one named choice:
a reference-free score standing in for a reference-based one, a version
mismatch, a test set that doesn't cover the language pair in question.

Since the reader has never met a Direct Assessment or an MQM annotation,
Alexander's habit of reaching his worked case, a single weakly effective drug,
within two sentences of opening the topic is a useful model for how fast this
lesson can move from naming a kind of human judgment to showing what one
actually looked like. And his closing move, two sentences that tell the reader
what to check rather than how well the argument did, is closer to what a
takeaway earns than any sentence that sums up the lesson's own performance.

## Cathy O'Neil, "Value-added model doesn't find bad teachers, causes administrators to cheat"

Source: https://mathbabe.org/2013/03/31/value-added-model-doesnt-find-bad-teachers-causes-administrators-to-cheat/

> "Final thing you need to know about the model: nobody really understands how
> it works. It relies on error terms of an error-riddled model. It's opaque,
> and no teacher can have their score explained to them in Plain English."

This sentence about opacity comes right after O'Neil has already given the
reader a number: a 24% correlation between two scores for the same teacher.
The number sits in the sentence before it, so the flat claim that follows
carries no hedge and needs none.

> "Given that the VAM is nearly a random number generator, this is *good
> news* – it means they are not trusting the VAM scores blindly. Of course, it
> still doesn't mean that the right teachers are getting fired, since half of
> the score is random."

She takes a fact that sounds like praise for the system, calls it good news,
and then immediately shows how little that good news is worth. The correction
lands in the same breath as the compliment.

> "Let's face it, this cheating scandal, and others like it (which may never
> come to light), *was not hard to predict*... In fact, as a predictive
> modeler, I'd argue that this cheating problem *is the easiest thing to
> predict about the VAM*, considering how it's being used as an opaque
> mathematical weapon."

The scandal is presented as the direct output of the property she had already
named two paragraphs earlier, opacity, so the mechanism and the human cost
arrive in the same sentence as cause and effect.

## Kaiser Fung, "Note to NYT: Simple math ain't simple"

Source: https://www.junkcharts.com/note-to-nyt-simple-math-aint-simple/

> "The study did not look at 878 'former players'. Rather, the study covers
> 878 dead former players... The study actually concerned 235 dead former NFL
> players whose brains were donated for research. Over 90% of the 235 brains
> showed signs of CTE."

Fung doesn't open by telling the reader that the 25 percent figure needs
context. He starts from the headline number and narrows it, clause by clause,
down to the actual population behind it. Each sentence removes one more layer
of what the number had quietly been standing in for.

> "You know what, not making an assumption makes the silliest assumption of
> them all! Literally, the reporter's 'simple math' simply assumes that the
> dead players whose brains were not donated did not have CTE. Not a single
> one of them had CTE. If that's not an assumption, I don't know the meaning
> of the a-word."

The joke works only because it is aimed at one specific claim: the reporter's
insistence that leaving out the undonated brains was assumption-free. Move it
to a different argument and it says nothing, which is what earns it a place
here.

> "The researchers who wrote the underlying paper are the true culprits. They
> bask in the glory of 'the most conservative assumption'... The most
> conservative assumption is categorically an extreme assumption."

He picks one phrase out of the paper he is criticizing and shows the trick
inside it. The last sentence turns the researchers' own vocabulary back on
them.

## Scott Alexander, "Beware The Man Of One Study"

Source: https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/12/12/beware-the-man-of-one-study/

> "Aquinas famously said: beware the man of one book. I would add: beware the
> man of one study. For example, take medical research. Suppose a certain
> drug is weakly effective against a certain disease. After a few years, a
> bunch of different research groups have gotten their hands on it and done
> all sorts of different studies. In the best case scenario the average study
> will find the true result – that it's weakly effective."

The abstract claim (beware trusting a single study) gets a concrete case,
one drug against one disease, inside the second sentence. Nothing about
aggregating evidence is asserted before the reader has something specific to
hold onto.

> "But there will also be random noise caused by inevitable variation and by
> some of the experiments being better quality than others. In the end, we
> might expect something looking kind of like a bell curve. The peak will be
> at 'weakly effective', but there will be a few studies to either side."

He explains the cause (noise, uneven quality) before he names the shape it
produces (a bell curve). By the time the picture arrives, it is already
explained.

> "Decrease your confidence about most things if you're not sure that you've
> investigated every piece of evidence... Most important, even if someone
> gives you what seems like overwhelming evidence in favor of a certain point
> of view, don't trust it until you've done a simple Google search to see if
> the opposite side has equally overwhelming evidence."

The close names two things to do, not a rating of how well the essay made its
case. The second sentence specifies the actual check (search for the other
side's evidence) rather than asking the reader to be generally more careful.
