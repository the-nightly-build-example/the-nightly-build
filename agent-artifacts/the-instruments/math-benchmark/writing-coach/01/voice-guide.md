# Voice guide: how this piece should sound

## How this piece should sound

Build the score in front of a reader who has never opened a terminal, the way Silver builds his 3.5-point margin: start from a fixed, stated number, run it through an actual count or an actual check, and arrive at a specific result. Whatever the piece finds about how the MATH score actually gets produced, work it as arithmetic the reader can follow step by step, not as a named sequence of steps.

Hold the piece's skepticism the way O'Neil holds hers. Her bluntest line, "pathetically bad," lands only after five paragraphs that show exactly how the ranking model fails. The sharpest judgment in this lesson should also arrive at the end of the work that earns it, not open a section as a warning of what's coming.

When the piece gets to why a lab has reason to move the score, use O'Neil's move of putting the reader on the other side of the incentive: ask what a lab actually does, faced with a public benchmark and a leaderboard, the way she asks what a college administrator does with federal funding on the line, instead of describing "incentives to game benchmarks" from outside. The series wants a real case where the number misled people; report it the way Thomas reports the teacher-cheating scandals, by naming who did what and what it cost, rather than saying a benchmark "was gamed."

Where the lesson has to say what the score actually measures against what people take it to measure, follow Thomas's stroke-model passage: state plainly what the data underneath the score is, set it next to what the score gets used to stand for, and let the distance between the two do the work instead of narrating it as "the problem is."

Because the reader has no code to fall back on, whatever procedure the piece has to describe should get the plain verb a person outside a codebase would use for it, defined the moment it first appears, the way Thomas drops the physical definition of a stroke into the middle of a sentence built for a reader who might not have it.

The lesson's own final judgment, about what the score leaves out, should be as specific as O'Neil's list of what a college ranking can't count: not that the benchmark misses something important, but the particular kind of mathematical work this score cannot see.

## Cathy O'Neil, "College ranking models"

Source: https://mathbabe.org/2013/08/26/college-ranking-models/

> "What Obama seems to realize about the existing model is that it's had side effects because of the way college administrators have gamed the model. Presumably, given that this new proposed model will be directly tied to federal funding, it will be high-impact and will thus be thoroughly gamed by administrators as well."

This states the mechanism as a plain if-then: raise the stakes riding on a number, and the gaming rises with it. No adjective is doing the arguing; "high-impact" and "thoroughly gamed" are the whole claim. The judgment that this is a bad plan is never announced here; it is carried entirely by the mechanism she just laid out.

> "What are you going to do, as an administrator tasked with making sure you never lose federal funding under the new regime? Are you going to give all the students fairer terms on their debt? Or are you going to select for students that are more likely to get finance jobs? I'm guessing the latter."

She puts the reader inside the incentive instead of describing it from outside, asking the question an administrator would actually face and then answering it herself in five words. The two options she offers are the real options a person in that seat would weigh, which is what makes the flat "I'm guessing the latter" read as a verdict rather than a jab.

> "The problem is that it's actually really hard to model quality of education. The mathematical models that already exist and are being proposed are just pathetically bad at it, partly because college, ultimately, isn't only about the facts you learn, or the job you get, or how quickly you get it. It's actually a life experience which, in the best of cases, enlarges your world view, and gets you to strive for something you might not have known existed before going."

The closing move names the specific thing being left out of the model rather than gesturing at it: not "there's more to it than numbers," but an actual accounting of what she means by more. "Pathetically bad" is blunt, but it arrives only after several paragraphs that showed exactly how the ranking fails, so it reads as an earned conclusion rather than an opinion set down on top.

## Nate Silver, "Don't let randomness make a fool of you"

Source: https://www.natesilver.net/p/dont-let-randomness-make-a-fool-of

> "Let's say that if we had Nostradamus-like abilities, we knew that the true state of the race is that Kamala Harris would win Wisconsin by 1 percentage point in an election held today. A typical poll has about 800 respondents. Well, the margin of error in an 800-person poll is plus or minus 3.5 points. Except, that substantially understates the case because the margin of error pertains only to one candidate's vote share."

He builds the number from a fixed, known starting point, the imagined true 1-point lead, forward through an actual sample size to an actual margin, rather than opening with the abstract idea of a margin of error. The "except" that follows immediately corrects his own figure instead of letting it stand, which is what keeps the explanation moving rather than settling too soon.

> "That means if the true state of the race is Harris +1, you'll get some Trump +5s and Harris +7s just from sampling error alone (from surveying a random sample of the population rather than every voter). And that's before getting into all the other ways that polls can go wrong."

The two poll results he invents aren't hedging; they are the specific range the arithmetic in the sentence before actually produces. Naming one exact source of error, then flagging that there are others without listing them, tells the reader precisely which part of the uncertainty he has accounted for and which part he hasn't.

> "If one candidate has a big lead, there's a bit less mental strain from all of this. Harris +8 doesn't feel that different from Harris +4, and an 85 percent probability of her winning doesn't feel that different than 75 percent. But when the probabilities are in the vicinity of even, it can seem like you're ping-ponging between radically different universes. A forecast showing Harris as a 55/45 favorite will be interpreted much differently from one showing Trump as a 55/45 favorite instead, even though they're basically saying the same thing: the election is more or less a coin flip."

This describes a reading error, not a math error: the same underlying uncertainty produces two forecasts that people experience completely differently depending on which side of even they land on. The four numbers he sets side by side, 8, 4, 85, 75, do the demonstrating; the sentence after them only names what they already showed.

## Rachel Thomas, "The problem with metrics is a big problem for AI"

Source: https://www.fast.ai/posts/2019-09-24-metrics.html

> "It turned out that the model was just identifying people who utilize health care a lot. They didn't actually have data of who had a stroke (a physiological event in which regions of the brain are denied new oxygen); they had data about who had access to medical care, chose to go to a doctor, were given the needed tests, and had this billing code added to their chart."

The parenthetical definition of a stroke, dropped in without slowing the sentence down, marks exactly where a reader might not be following and clears it before moving on. The two "had data about" statements that follow do the real work: one names what the model was built to predict, the other what it actually had numbers on, and the gap between them is the whole finding.

> "Automatic essay grading software focuses primarily on metrics like sentence length, vocabulary, spelling, and subject-verb agreement, but is unable to evaluate aspects of writing that are hard to quantify, such as creativity. As a result, gibberish essays randomly generated by computer programs to contain lots of sophisticated words score well."

The first sentence lists exactly what the software checks, so "unable to evaluate... creativity" reads as a specific omission rather than a general complaint. "Gibberish essays... score well" states the consequence as plainly as it can be stated, with no adjective asking the reader to feel a particular way about it first.

> "As USA education policy began over-emphasizing student test scores as the primary way to evaluate teachers, there have been widespread scandals of teachers and principals cheating by altering students scores, in Georgia, Indiana, Massachusetts, Nevada, Virginia, Texas, and elsewhere."

Naming six states rather than writing "several states" is what makes "widespread" a fact instead of an intensifier. The sentence reports what happened once a test score decided a career, and the list of states carries the scale instead of a stronger adjective doing it.
