## The argument and why it

This desk teaches the arguments about how AI could go wrong, one at a time, so the
reader can judge each on its merits. Every desk piece so far has been a
technical-safety argument (alignment, deception, misuse). The economic-disruption
argument, that AI could throw large numbers of people out of work, is the one most
readers actually encounter in daily life and it is missing from the course. It has
a long pedigree and a real body of evidence and counter-evidence, which is exactly
what this desk is built to weigh.

The beat's shape governs the piece: open with the argument at full strength and
name who first made it and what worried them; test it against what real systems and
real labor markets have actually done, drawing a sharp line between what has been
shown and what is still projection; bring it to the present, name who makes it now
and what they want done, and check it against the most recent evidence, naming the
gap wherever confidence (doom or dismissal) outruns proof. Work from the original
documents and the tests behind the headline numbers, not the commentary. Name no
company as an authority. Leave the reader to decide how worried to be.

## The angle

The headline number is the spine. Frey and Osborne's 2013 estimate that about 47%
of US employment was at "high risk" of computerization is the figure the argument
still travels on, and the test behind it is a specific and contestable method
(expert-labeled occupations, whole occupations scored rather than tasks). The
task-based rebuttal (the OECD / Arntz-Gregory-Zierahn 2016 reworking that put the
figure near 9%) is the sharp line between "jobs" and "tasks," and it is the crux.
Then the present: the LLM-specific exposure work (Eloundou et al., "GPTs are
GPTs," 2023) estimates how many workers have how many tasks affected, but exposure
is not displacement; the earliest real-system evidence (a customer-support
field study showing productivity gains that compress the gap between novices and
experts; freelancer-market studies showing demand and earnings falling for the
most exposed writing and coding gigs after ChatGPT) is genuine but small and
mixed; and the macro estimate (Acemoglu's "Simple Macroeconomics of AI," 2024)
is modest. The reader should leave able to separate a real, measured early effect
from both the "half of all jobs" fear and the "automation always creates more jobs"
dismissal, and to see that the argument turns on tasks-versus-occupations and on
whether new work appears as fast as old work is automated.

Steelman both the alarm and the dismissal before weighing them. The historical
counter (past automation did not produce permanent mass unemployment because
automating some tasks raised the value of others and created new ones) is the
strongest case against, and it must be given at full strength, along with the
reason this time might differ.

## Sources

Source floor: at least 8 sources, at least 4 primary, at least 1 secondary.
Primary here is the paper or study that owns the claim or the headline number.

Direct the researcher to read, at minimum:
- Keynes, "Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren" (1930), for the coinage
  and original framing of "technological unemployment."
- Frey and Osborne, "The Future of Employment" (2013), for the 47% figure and its
  exact method and definition of "high risk."
- The OECD task-based rebuttal (Arntz, Gregory, Zierahn, 2016) for the ~9% figure
  and why the task framing changes the answer.
- Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin, Rock, "GPTs are GPTs" (2023) for LLM task-exposure
  estimates and their explicit exposure-not-impact caveat.
- Brynjolfsson, Li, Raymond, "Generative AI at Work" (2023) for the customer-support
  field results.
- A freelancer/labor-market study of post-ChatGPT effects (e.g., Hui, Reshef, Zhou)
  for early demand/earnings evidence.
- Acemoglu, "The Simple Macroeconomics of AI" (2024) for the macro estimate, and
  Autor's work on task complementarity / new tasks for the historical counter.
- At least one independent secondary source for how the argument is deployed now.

Verify every headline figure against the study that owns it, with its denominator,
period, and definition (a "high-risk" occupation and a "high-exposure" worker are
not the same object). Record where studies disagree and why (occupation vs task,
exposure vs displacement, productivity vs employment). Two retellings of one
estimate are one source.
