# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/technological-unemployment (01)

## How this piece should sound

This lesson weighs a real alarm: that AI will put large numbers of people out of work. It is written for a reader who is smart and widely read but has no way to check the automation numbers they keep meeting, and the value of the piece is that it lets them judge the argument instead of the people making it. The register that does this is calm and plain. State each claim directly, in the paper's Matt Yglesias register, and let the evidence rather than the volume decide how alarmed to sound.

Put the strongest form of the unemployment argument, and the strongest form of the dismissal, before weighing either. Timothy Lee praises the book he then dismantles, and Hannah Ritchie raises the best objection to her own claim before answering it. The alarm and the counter-case both read more convincingly to a skeptical reader when they arrive at full strength and are only then tested. If the argument is presented in a weakened form, the weighing that follows proves nothing.

The craft the piece turns on is saying what a number counts. When a figure enters, the reader can be told what it measures, over which workers, and for what period, so that what a study projects and what it has actually observed are never read as the same object. Lee's line that whether low rates help workers "isn't that kind of question," and his comparison to climate science, are two ways of holding an aggregate estimate and the limits of any single case in view at once. This lesson has the same work to do when a headline share of jobs and a small early result sit in the same section.

Where the evidence does not settle the question, say so and name what would settle it. Lee writes "I don't know" and claims only that his explanation is no less plausible than the one he doubts. Smith admits he cannot tell how much of a shift among economists was evidence and how much was ideology. This is the move the beat asks for by name: mark the gap wherever confidence, alarm or dismissal, runs past the proof.

When the loud prediction and the measured result disagree, state the measured result plainly and give the concrete reason, the way Ritchie states a flat fish catch against rising demand and then explains it. A real finding does not need hedging into mush, and a small one does not need inflating.

A verdict is welcome once the evidence reaches one. Smith calls a policy "pretty safe" only on the weight of the studies, and a grand or frightening word about AI and work earns its place the same way, after the figures have done the arguing. The exemplars all write in the first person and this body will not, but the honesty in Lee's "I don't know" lives in what the sentence admits, not in the pronoun, and the lesson can admit exactly as much without ever saying I.

## Timothy B. Lee, "Why easy money is (probably) good for ordinary workers"

Source: https://www.fullstackeconomics.com/p/easy-money-is-probably-good-for-ordinary-workers

> "Leonard is a skillful storyteller, and I enjoyed reading his book. But I found its economic analysis wanting. There are a lot of important questions you can answer by talking to ordinary people. But whether low interest rates help workers isn’t that kind of question. To answer this question in a rigorous way you need theories and data. And those are woefully lacking in The Lords of Easy Money."

Lee credits the book he is about to dismantle, and the praise is concrete enough that the reader believes it. He then draws a clean line between the questions reporting can settle and the ones only theory and data can, which tells the reader in advance why the disagreement that follows will be about evidence. The verdict is stated plainly and in the first person, so Lee owns it.

> "During this period, the value of the dollar rose, making US exports uncompetitive and outsourcing more attractive. Could weak demand and a strengthening dollar have encouraged Rexnord to outsource its factory to Mexico? I don’t know. But this theory doesn’t seem any less plausible than Leonard’s theory that low interest rates caused Rexnord to move the factory."

Lee builds a rival explanation for the same plant closure and then refuses to oversell it: he writes "I don't know" and claims only that it is no less plausible than the account he is questioning. Holding two explanations of one event open is how he shows the reader that a single vivid case does not settle a causal question.

> "Of course, if I found a specific person who fit one of these categories, I wouldn’t be able to prove that Fed policy helped that particular person—any more than Leonard can prove easy money hurt John Feltner. In this respect macroeconomics is a bit like climate science. We know that rising global temperatures make extreme weather more likely, but it’s hard to pinpoint how large-scale trends affect any particular person."

Lee reaches for a comparison the reader already holds, extreme weather and a warming climate, to explain why an aggregate effect can be real even when no single case proves it. The analogy carries the teaching, and it lets him keep the general finding and the limits of any one anecdote in view at the same time.

## Noah Smith, "Why $15 minimum wage is pretty safe"

Source: https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/why-15-minimum-wage-is-pretty-safe

> "That’s a pretty dramatic change, and you can see that it started before Card and Krueger did their famous study. So it’s not immediately clear how much of the shift was due to economists’ changing ideological views, and how much was due to the accumulating mountain of evidence."

Smith has just shown economists changing their minds, and rather than crediting the evidence he separates two possible causes and admits he cannot cleanly tell them apart. Naming the ideological pull on his own side of the debate is what keeps the passage from reading as a victory lap.

> "Note that while most studies conclude that the effects of minimum wage are small, many actually conclude that higher minimum wages create jobs."

The sentence reports what the body of studies found, including the part that cuts against expectation, in one plain line. Smith trusts the finding to hold the reader's attention on its own and does not inflate it.

> "In other words, the question “Does minimum wage kill jobs?” doesn’t have a definitive, universal answer. It depends on how high the minimum wage is. It depends on how high prevailing wages are. It depends on the local labor market, and how powerful local employers are. And it probably depends on other things too, such as whether we’re in a recession."

Given a yes-or-no question the reader wants answered, Smith replaces the single answer with the list of things the answer turns on. The repetition is not filler: each "It depends" names a different variable, so the reader leaves knowing what to ask about instead of which side to pick.

## Hannah Ritchie, "The Moloch Trap of Environmental Problems"

Source: https://hannahritchie.substack.com/p/moloch-trap

> "One way to limit deforestation and habitat loss is to improve agricultural productivity. More food from less land. You might argue that this gives them even more incentive to cut down forests because they’ll be able to produce even more crops on that land. There are certainly situations where that’s true. But it’s not always the case: if the market is close to being saturated, oversupply will simply push down prices and it might not be that economically advantageous at all."

Ritchie raises the strongest objection to her own point before the reader can, grants that it sometimes holds, and then answers it with a specific mechanism about saturated markets and falling prices. Conceding the cases where the objection is true is what earns the qualification that follows.

> "The amount of wild fish caught each year has barely increased over the last decade despite increased seafood demand. That’s because of the rise of aquaculture, where fish farmers quite literally grow their own. It’s no longer a zero-sum game."

A number that cuts against the obvious expectation, a flat catch against rising demand, opens the passage, and the next sentence supplies the concrete reason. Ritchie keeps the prose plain and lets the figure carry the surprise, with "quite literally grow their own" as a light aside that depends on the fish and not on decoration.
