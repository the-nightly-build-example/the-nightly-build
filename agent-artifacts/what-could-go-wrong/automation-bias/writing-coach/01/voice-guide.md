# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/automation-bias

## How this piece should sound

This is a lesson in the What Could Go Wrong desk, teaching the automation-bias
argument to a reader who is capable and widely read but has no way to check the
claims they keep hearing about people trusting automated systems too far. The
body should sound like the paper's house voice at its plainest: state the
argument in ordinary sentences, and let the cockpit, the clinical decision aid,
and the driver following the navigation into a field do the explaining.
Gawande's account of intensive care is written this way, and the plainness is
what makes a hard subject followable.

The desk opens the argument at full strength, and two of the passages below show
what that takes. Gawande, arguing that the checklists work, still stops to put
the skeptics' case in its strongest form and in their own terms. Langewiesche
blames NASA's managers in his first sentence and then, in fairness, reconstructs
why their confidence had felt earned. The lesson can state the case for
automation bias the same way, so that a reader understands why serious people in
aviation and medicine held it, before anything tests it. Where the piece meets
the opposing claim, that a human kept in the loop is enough to catch the errors
the machine makes, it can put that claim at its strongest rather than in the
weak form that is easy to knock down.

The commission asks for a sharp line between what deployed systems have already
shown and what is still extrapolation about AI assistants that are newer and
less studied. Luu's page settles a standard of evidence before it reaches a
verdict, judging someone who makes many bets by total return rather than by a
list of the failures. The lesson may need the same discipline when it separates
the measured record, automation bias observed and counted in real cockpits and
clinical systems, from the thinner and more recent claims about over-reliance on
chatbots and coding assistants. Saying plainly which kind of thing each piece of
evidence is keeps the two from blurring.

The verdict this lesson reaches can hold more than one true claim at once.
Gawande's close keeps the checklist's value and its limits in view together
without softening into vagueness. Automation bias can be both real and well
measured and still not be settled by keeping a human in the loop, and the piece
can say that without moving toward the alarm that over-reliance is a
civilizational threat or the dismissal that a monitor takes care of it. Where
the confidence outruns the proof in either direction, Luu's way of granting a
point and then rating how much it is worth is available: say how far the
evidence actually reaches.

Two more habits from these writers suit the material. When an abstract claim
about complacency or misplaced trust needs grounding, a single worked case can
carry it, the way Gawande spends the whole 1935 bomber story before he states
what it shows. And a large word can wait until the evidence has earned it:
Langewiesche reports exactly what the wing withstood before he calls the machine
heroic. If the record for automation bias as a serious failure is strong in
places, the strength can come from the studies themselves, with the grand word
held until they have supplied it.

## Atul Gawande, "The Checklist"

Source: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2007/12/10/the-checklist

> "Medicine today has entered its B-17 phase. Substantial parts of what
> hospitals do—most notably, intensive care—are now too complex for clinicians
> to carry them out reliably from memory alone. I.C.U. life support has become
> too much medicine for one person to fly."

After several paragraphs telling the story of a 1935 bomber that crashed because
it was too complex to fly from memory, Gawande states the comparison in three
short sentences. The claim is brief and definite because the story has already
supplied the evidence for it, and the last sentence borrows the airplane's own
words instead of restating the point in abstract terms. What is visible is how
much setup he does before making a general claim, and how plainly he states it
once he does.

> "There were various reasons. Some physicians were offended by the suggestion
> that they needed checklists. Others had legitimate doubts about Pronovost's
> evidence. So far, he'd shown only that checklists worked in one hospital,
> Johns Hopkins, where the I.C.U.s have money, plenty of staff, and Peter
> Pronovost walking the hallways to make sure that the checklists are being used
> properly."

Gawande is arguing that the checklists work, and here he stops to put the
skeptics' case in its strongest form: one hospital, with money and staff and the
inventor himself present. He calls the doubts "legitimate" and names the
specific reason for them rather than describing them in general. Stating the
objection this fully is what makes his later verdict credible, and the fairness
is his own.

> "It's ludicrous, though, to suppose that checklists are going to do away with
> the need for courage, wits, and improvisation. The body is too intricate and
> individual for that: good medicine will not be able to dispense with expert
> audacity. Yet it should also be ready to accept the virtues of regimentation."

The verdict keeps two claims in view at once: the checklist saves lives, and it
does not remove the need for judgment. Each sentence grants one side and
qualifies it without becoming vague, and the closing sentence states plainly
what the reader should accept. Gawande is visible in his refusal to let a strong
result harden into a slogan.

## William Langewiesche, "Columbia's Last Flight"

Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2003/11/columbias-last-flight/304204/

> "It was the responsibility of NASA's managers to hear those suspicions, and
> from top to bottom they failed. After the fact, that's easy to see. But in
> fairness to those whose reputations have now been sacrificed, seventeen years
> and eighty-nine shuttle flights had passed since the Challenger explosion, and
> within the agency a new generation had risen that was smart, perhaps, but also
> unwise—confined by NASA's walls and routines, and vulnerable to the
> self-satisfaction that inevitably had set in."

The first sentence assigns the failure without qualification, and the second
admits that this is clear only in hindsight. The third then reconstructs why the
managers' confidence had felt reasonable: many years and many flights without a
disaster. Langewiesche is visible in stating the blame and the mitigating
context together, without weakening either one.

> "Gehman suggested to them what Congress had insisted to him—that people simply
> cannot investigate themselves. Civics 101. Once stated, it seems like an
> obvious principle."

He gives the principle in one ordinary sentence, follows it with a two-word
sentence, then remarks that it seems obvious once said. The very short sentence
is doing real work, marking how basic the violated rule was. The move from a
long sentence to a two-word one is characteristic of how he controls emphasis.

> "By the time it got to Texas the Columbia had already proved itself a heroic
> flying machine, having endured for so long at hypersonic speeds with little
> left of the midsection inside its left wing, and the plume of hot gas still in
> there, alive, and eating it away."

The sentence describes what the wing physically withstood in exact terms, and
only after those facts does it use the word "heroic." Nothing is exaggerated;
the phrase "alive, and eating it away" is accurate before it is vivid.
Langewiesche is visible in holding back the one large word until the details
have supported it.

## Dan Luu, "Steve Ballmer was an underrated CEO"

Source: https://danluu.com/ballmer/

> "When people point to a long list of failures like Bing, Zune, Windows Phone,
> and HoloLens as evidence that Ballmer was some kind of buffoon who was holding
> Microsoft back, this demonstrates a lack of understanding of the tech
> industry. This is like pointing to a list of failed companies a VC has funded
> as evidence the VC doesn't know what they're doing. But that's silly in a hits
> based industry like venture capital."

Before reaching any verdict on Ballmer, Luu states the standard by which someone
who makes many bets should be judged, using venture capital, which the reader
already understands, as the comparison. He shows the reasoning in order: a list
of failures says nothing on its own; what matters is the total return. Luu is
visible in settling the correct measure before he argues about the person.

> "Of course it would be better if Ballmer was prescient and all of his bets
> succeeded, making Microsoft worth something like $10T instead of the lowly $3T
> market cap it has today, but the criticism of Ballmer that says that he had
> some failures and some $1T successes is a criticism that he wasn't the
> greatest CEO of all time by a gigantic margin. True, but not much of a
> criticism."

He states the criticism in its strongest form and then says exactly how much it
proves: that Ballmer was not the best CEO in history by a wide margin. The
six-word follow-up accepts the point and rates it as minor. Luu is visible in
conceding precisely instead of arguing past the objection.

> "Even when there's very clear data showing that bucking the status quo is the
> right thing, people usually don't do it, in part because you look like an
> idiot when it doesn't pan out, but Ballmer was willing to make the right bets
> in the face of decades of ridicule."

The sentence first explains why making such a bet is rare, since a person who
bets against the consensus looks foolish when it fails, and only then credits
Ballmer for doing it. The praise rests on that general point about incentives
rather than on admiration alone. Luu is visible in giving the reason before he
gives the credit.
