# researcher brief: what-could-go-wrong/automation-bias (01)

Inputs:
- ../../editorial-direction.md — citation standard, series territory, declared reader
- ../../commission.md — subject, angle, and source obligations

Output: evidence.md

Work from the original documents. Establish firsthand, with locators:
- The origin: Lisanne Bainbridge, "Ironies of Automation" (Automatica, 1983).
  Read it. Record the exact ironies she named (the human left to monitor loses
  the skill and the situational awareness needed to take over). Quote where the
  wording is the evidence.
- The automation-bias / complacency literature: Parasuraman and Riley, "Humans
  and Automation: Use, Misuse, Disuse, Abuse" (1997); and at least one empirical
  automation-bias study (e.g. Mosier and Skitka on cockpit automation bias, or
  Cummings, or Goddard et al. on clinical decision support). Record the measured
  effect: what the operators did, the error rates with and without the aid, the
  denominators.
- Documented real-world cases where deference to automation caused error
  (aviation incidents, clinical decision-support studies, drivers following
  navigation into hazard). Keep these to what a primary or authoritative record
  supports.
- The present-day claim: at least one recent study on human over-reliance on AI
  / LLM assistance (e.g. over-reliance on AI decision aids, or on code
  assistants). Record the effect size and the study's scope and limits.
Contradictions to search for and record in full: studies where the human in the
loop DID catch the automation's errors, where training or design reduced
automation bias, or where AI assistance improved outcomes without measurable
over-reliance. The desk's line between shown-in-a-working-system and
extrapolation depends on this.
Meet counts (min 8; primary >=4; secondary >=1) with sources that change the
interpretation. Give each named researcher their exact title/affiliation as the
primary states it. Confirm every URL resolves to the document's own page.
