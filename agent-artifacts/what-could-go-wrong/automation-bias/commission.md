# Commission: what-could-go-wrong/automation-bias

## Assignment
Teach the argument that automation goes wrong less through the machine's own
error than through the human who stops checking it: people come to trust an
automated system so far that they defer to it against their own judgment and
against evidence in front of them, and they miss the errors the system does make.
The desk teaches one argument at a time so the reader can judge it on the merits.

Open with the argument at full strength. Name who first made it and what they had
seen. The canonical origin is human-factors research, above all Lisanne
Bainbridge's "Ironies of Automation" (1983), and the automation-bias and
complacency literature that followed (e.g. Parasuraman and Riley; Mosier and
Skitka). Lay out the reasoning as its careful defender would: the more reliable
the automation, the less the human practices the skill and the more they trust
the output, so the human monitor is worst prepared exactly when the rare failure
arrives.

Then test it against what real systems actually do. This argument is unusually
well grounded: draw the sharp line the desk asks for, but here most of the weight
sits on the "already shown in a working system" side (documented automation-bias
studies in aviation and clinical decision support, drivers following navigation
into error), with the newer, thinner claims being about today's AI assistants.
Bring it to the present: who makes the argument now about large-language-model
assistants and AI decision aids, what they want done, and what the most recent
evidence shows about over-reliance on chatbots and coding assistants. Where
today's confidence outruns the proof — in either direction, the alarm that LLM
over-reliance is a civilizational problem or the dismissal that a human in the
loop fixes it — name the gap.

## Angle
Automation bias is one of the best-evidenced "how it goes wrong" arguments,
because it was measured in deployed systems decades before modern AI. The lesson
uses that solid base to test the fashionable version: is over-reliance on an LLM
the same failure as automation bias in a cockpit, or does the chatbot's
fluency and breadth change it? Keep the old evidence and the new claims clearly
separated, per the desk's line between the shown and the guessed.

## Template and form
Lesson template. Body first, then both bookends. 1200–2200 words. Work from the
original documents, not commentary about them. Name no company as an authority.
Sections named for this argument.

## Sources
Series floor is 8 sources, at least 4 primary and at least 1 secondary. Primary:
the founding human-factors papers (Bainbridge 1983; Parasuraman and Riley 1997;
a Mosier/Skitka or Cummings automation-bias study), read directly; plus at least
one recent primary study on human over-reliance on AI/LLM assistance. Secondary:
reputable synthesis or reporting for context on the present-day debate. Verify
every figure against the study that owns it; two retellings of one study count as
one.

name where it is proven (deployed systems, measured) and where it is still
