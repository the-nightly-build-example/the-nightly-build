# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/open-weights-release

## How this piece should sound

This lesson runs on three moves in sequence: the irreversibility argument at
full strength, the line between what a working system has shown and what is
still projected onto one that doesn't exist, and the present-day fight over
what to do about the gap. Each move has a different way of failing into either
doom or dismissal, and the register that holds all three without tipping is the
one Karnofsky, Tufekci, and Schneier below share: state the claim plainly, then
say in the same breath what would settle it and that it hasn't been.

Open the Seger et al. case the way Karnofsky opens his: name the risk in full,
then immediately say whether it's been resolved, rather than let a later
paragraph walk it back. "Maybe we'll succeed in reducing the risk, and maybe we
won't. Unfortunately, I think it could be hard to know either way" does the
believing and the qualifying in the same two sentences, not two paragraphs
apart where the qualifier reads like a retraction. The irreversibility argument
deserves that same directness before anything tests it.

When the piece reaches Qi et al. and Mouton et al., use Karnofsky's move on his
lab-mice problem: say what the finding does show, then say what it doesn't, in
one continuous unit rather than a claim followed by a hedge. A demonstrated
result (safety fine-tuning is cheap to strip) and a null result (no measured
uplift, yet) both need this treatment, and neither needs an apology for what it
can't yet say.

Where the doom case and the dismissal case sit side by side — the RAND null
result read as proof there's nothing to worry about, or as proof of nothing at
all about tomorrow's model — Karnofsky's "I'm not personally sympathetic to
having high confidence either way" is the right length for that sentence. It
names both extremes and declines both without a paragraph of throat-clearing
about the limits of knowledge.

Tufekci is the model for the worked example the template requires. She doesn't
explain overdispersion; she puts Jeff Bezos in a bar and lets the reader do the
arithmetic. The marginal-risk question (what open weights add over a
capability the internet already offers a determined bad actor) wants the same
treatment: a comparison the reader can picture rather than the word "marginal"
doing the work alone. And her hairstylist passage is the exact shape for
handling the RAND result: state plainly what a null finding would need to show
to count as evidence, and what it doesn't tell you about a system that doesn't
exist yet. That is the demonstrated/speculative line the commission asks for,
written as reasoning instead of asserted as a rule.

Schneier's reframe is worth the pivot from the opening argument to the
evidence section: a debate that's been run as two sides of one question can
often be turned, in one plain sentence, into the sharper question underneath
it. The marginal-risk framing is exactly that kind of reframe of "open versus
closed," and it can be stated with the same bluntness Schneier gives "less
security and more security."

None of these three writers soften a real finding to be even-handed, and
neither should this piece. Karnofsky still says the risk is real before he
says it's hard to measure. Tufekci still reports that masks work before she
qualifies what one clean case can and can't prove. Fairness here means holding
both the irreversibility case and the marginal-risk rebuttal at the strength
their own best defenders give them, not splitting the difference between them
in every sentence.

## Holden Karnofsky, "AI Safety Seems Hard to Measure"

Source: https://www.cold-takes.com/ai-safety-seems-hard-to-measure/

> "Maybe we'll succeed in reducing the risk, and maybe we won't. Unfortunately, I think it could be hard to know either way. This piece is about four fairly distinct-seeming reasons that this could be the case - and that AI safety could be an unusually difficult sort of science."

This opens by believing the risk and admitting the uncertainty about resolving
it in the same two sentences, rather than stating the claim and hedging it
several paragraphs later. The "unfortunately" is doing real work: it marks the
difficulty as a cost to the writer's own hope, not a rhetorical shrug.

> "This does not mean there's no productive AI safety research to be done! (See the previous sections.) It just means that the research being done today is somewhat analogous to research on lab mice: informative and important up to a point, but only up to a point."

He has just spent several paragraphs describing a real limit on what current
testing can show, and here he stops the reader from over-reading that limit as
nihilism. The exclamation point is earned: it corrects a misreading he can see
coming, not a flourish.

> "Some people think this sort of concern about 'superintelligent' systems is ridiculous; some seem to consider it extremely likely. I'm not personally sympathetic to having high confidence either way."

Three short sentences name both poles of an argument he has spent the essay
inside of, and decline to endorse either. Nothing here softens into "reasonable
people disagree." He states the two actual positions and then states his own
distance from both of them, specifically and briefly.

## Zeynep Tufekci, "This Overlooked Variable Is the Key to the Pandemic"

Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/health/archive/2020/09/k-overlooked-variable-driving-pandemic/616548/

> "If Amazon's CEO, Jeff Bezos, walks into a bar with 100 regular people in it, the average wealth in that bar suddenly exceeds $1 billion. If I also walk into that bar, not much will change. Clearly, the average is not that useful a number to understand the distribution of wealth in that bar, or how to change it. Sometimes, the mean is not the message."

A statistical idea (why an average misrepresents a skewed distribution) is
taught with a scene the reader can picture in one line, not a definition. The
closing sentence restates the point in six plain words instead of technical
language, and it earns that compression because the bar scene already did the
explaining.

> "Take the highly publicized case in Springfield, Missouri, in which two infected hairstylists, both of whom wore masks, continued to work with clients while symptomatic. It turns out that no apparent infections were found among the 139 exposed clients (67 were directly tested; the rest did not report getting sick). While there is a lot of evidence that masks are crucial in dampening transmission, that event alone wouldn't tell us if masks work. In contrast, studying transmission, the rarer event, can be quite informative. Had those two hairstylists transmitted the virus to large numbers of people despite everyone wearing masks, it would be important evidence that, perhaps, masks aren't useful in preventing super-spreading."

She takes one real, specific case and reasons out loud about what it can and
cannot prove, including the counterfactual that would have made it count as
evidence. She never claims the case settles anything, and she never uses it to
suggest masks don't work. She uses it to teach the reader how to read a null
result honestly.

> "It's not intellectually satisfying, but because of the overdispersion and its stochasticity, there may not be an explanation beyond that the worst-hit regions, at least initially, simply had a few unlucky early super-spreading events."

A plain admission that the tidy explanation the reader wants may not exist,
stated once and moved past rather than circled back to. The phrase "not
intellectually satisfying" names the reader's disappointment without
apologizing for it.

## Bruce Schneier, "Security vs. Surveillance"

Source: https://www.schneier.com/essays/archives/2016/02/security_vs_surveill.html

> "We're not being asked to choose between security and privacy. We're being asked to choose between less security and more security."

Two short sentences replace the debate's own framing with a sharper one, and
neither sentence argues for it. The substitution is the argument, and it lands
because everything before it has already earned the reader's agreement that
the first framing was too narrow.

> "Of course, criminals and terrorists have used, are using, and will use encryption to hide their planning from the authorities, just as they will use many aspects of society's capabilities and infrastructure: cars, restaurants, telecommunications. [...] Compare this with the tactic of secretly poisoning all the food at a restaurant. Yes, we might get lucky and poison a terrorist before he strikes, but we'll harm all the innocent customers in the process. Weakening encryption for everyone is harmful in exactly the same way."

He concedes the opposing side's real point first — bad actors do benefit from
the same tool everyone else uses — before making the case that blocking the
tool for everyone costs more than it saves. The restaurant analogy makes a
policy tradeoff (protect some targeted cases versus harm everyone) concrete
without needing the word "tradeoff."
