# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/ai-environmental-cost

## Hannah Ritchie, "You want to reduce the carbon footprint of your food? Focus on what you eat, not whether your food is local"

Source: https://ourworldindata.org/food-choice-vs-eating-local

> "'Eating local' is a recommendation you hear often — even from prominent sources, including the United Nations. While it might make sense intuitively — after all, transport does lead to emissions — it is one of the most misguided pieces of advice."

Checked: https://ourworldindata.org/food-choice-vs-eating-local, retrieved 2026-09-25
She states the popular claim in a form its holder would recognize and grants the intuition behind it, transport does cause emissions, before turning against it in the same sentence. The turn lands on one plain word, "misguided," not a stack of qualifiers building up to a verdict.

> "Let's take the example of beef from a beef herd — the average footprint is approximately 60 kilograms of CO2eq per kilogram of beef. Let's compare the transport footprint of buying from your local farmer (who turns out to be your neighbor) versus someone in the UK buying beef from Central America (approximately 9000 kilometers away)."

Checked: https://ourworldindata.org/food-choice-vs-eating-local, retrieved 2026-09-25
Instead of asserting the conclusion, she runs an actual case: one commodity, one specific distance, arithmetic a reader could redo. The parenthetical "(who turns out to be your neighbor)" is a small joke sitting inside a sentence that is otherwise pure setup for a calculation, and it costs nothing because the numbers still carry the sentence.

> "Many believe that air freight is more common than it actually is. Very little food is air-freighted; it accounts for only 0.16% of food miles. But for the few products which are transported by air, the emissions can be very high: it emits 50 times more CO2eq than a boat per tonne kilometer."

Checked: https://ourworldindata.org/food-choice-vs-eating-local, retrieved 2026-09-25
Before closing the argument, she grants the one case where the popular fear is actually right. The concession is a real figure, 0.16%, not a gesture toward balance, which is what keeps the piece from reading as though it had already decided the answer before checking.

## Brian Potter, "So you want to use less concrete"

Source: https://www.construction-physics.com/p/so-you-want-to-use-less-concrete

> "It's not easy to get a sense of scale once you start to deal with numbers this big (billion doesn't feel 1000 times bigger than million). One way of looking at it is we produce around 4 metric tons, or just under 60 cubic feet, of concrete for each person on the planet every year (this is a cube just under 4 feet on a side)."

Checked: https://www.construction-physics.com/p/so-you-want-to-use-less-concrete, retrieved 2026-09-25
He names the actual difficulty, a billion-scale number that doesn't register, and then supplies a human-scale substitute, a cube four feet on a side, instead of repeating the big number louder. The parenthetical about billion not feeling 1,000 times bigger than million reads as him working the problem in front of the reader, not as decoration.

> "You often see enthusiasm for the idea of replacing concrete buildings with mass timber ones. But assuming you could substitute all the world's concrete for an equal volume of wood, you'd need to more than triple the total annual volume of global wood harvested, which puts a somewhat different spin on the issue."

Checked: https://www.construction-physics.com/p/so-you-want-to-use-less-concrete, retrieved 2026-09-25
He takes an idea a reader may already hold, that timber can just replace concrete, and runs the substitution at the scale the world would actually need. "Puts a somewhat different spin on the issue" is dry understatement doing the work a stronger verdict would only announce.

> "It's not obvious what the best path forward is for addressing concrete carbon emissions (like with most things, I suspect it'll end up being a mix of different solutions), but understanding the parameters of the problem is necessary for solving it."

Checked: https://www.construction-physics.com/p/so-you-want-to-use-less-concrete, retrieved 2026-09-25
The close names what he doesn't know rather than manufacturing a resolution the piece hasn't earned. The parenthetical keeps his own uncertainty in view instead of hiding it behind a confident final line.

## Hannah Ritchie, "What's the carbon footprint of using ChatGPT?"

Source: https://hannahritchie.substack.com/p/carbon-footprint-chatgpt

This piece covers this article's own subject, so the passages below are quoted for craft only, never for AI energy figures. This piece is not a source for the article; the commission's primary documents (Strubell, Patterson, Luccioni, the IEA report, de Vries) are.

> "If I'm being honest, for a while I also felt a bit guilty about using AI. The common rule-of-thumb is that ChatGPT uses 10 times as much energy as a Google search [I think this is probably now too high, but more on that later]."

Checked: https://hannahritchie.substack.com/p/carbon-footprint-chatgpt, retrieved 2026-09-25
She admits to a feeling a reader carrying the same worry will recognize, and names the specific rule-of-thumb responsible for it in the same breath, so the personal aside is doing argumentative work rather than just building rapport before the numbers arrive.

> "Or to put it another way: average electricity usage in the UK is 12,000 Wh per day. A ChatGPT prompt is 3 Wh. 3 Wh!"

Checked: https://hannahritchie.substack.com/p/carbon-footprint-chatgpt, retrieved 2026-09-25
She puts the disputed figure beside a number the reader already has some feel for, then repeats the disputed figure alone. The exclamation point is the only display of emphasis in the passage, and it arrives after the arithmetic has already made the case, not instead of it.

> "I cannot say for certain that 0.3 Wh is the best 'updated' number. But I'd bet that the real number is closer to 0.3 than to 3 Wh."

Checked: https://hannahritchie.substack.com/p/carbon-footprint-chatgpt, retrieved 2026-09-25
The hedge is checkable: two figures, a stated bet, and a direction. She is not rating her own carefulness, she is telling the reader exactly where she'd put money if she had to.

## How this piece should sound

This is an explainer for a reader who already suspects the viral number is off but doesn't know how to check it, on a subject where the real worry (data-center electricity, and water use nobody discloses) is easy to lose underneath the fake one (a single query's water bottle). Open the way Ritchie opens on eating local: state the environmental case at full strength, in words its defenders would recognize, granting what's true in it, before the numbers start separating the measured part from the guessed part.

Where a figure needs scale, do what Potter does with the concrete cube and what Ritchie does with the UK's daily electricity use: put the disputed number beside one the reader already carries, in the same sentence, rather than restating the disputed number louder or reaching for an adjective like "huge" or "tiny."

The piece's central distinction, what one query costs against what the whole build-out adds to a grid, is the same move Potter makes running the timber substitution at true scale: an idea that sounds reasonable for one thing can collapse, or hold up, once it's run at the scale of everything. Do the arithmetic in view the way both writers do, rather than asserting which side wins.

Where the aggregate trend is measured but the projection past it isn't, hedge the way Ritchie hedges 0.3 watt-hours against 3: name the two figures, say which one the evidence favors, and stop there. A qualification earns its place by naming what would settle the question, not by announcing how carefully you've weighed it.

Grant the real exception before returning to the larger argument, the way Ritchie grants air freight its full 50-times figure before closing the case for local eating. This piece has its own version: the aggregate data-center load is real, rising, and thin on water disclosure, and that fact needs its full weight in the same paragraph that takes apart the per-query panic, not a concession tacked onto the end.
