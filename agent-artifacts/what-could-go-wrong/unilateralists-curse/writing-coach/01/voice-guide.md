# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/unilateralists-curse

## How this piece should sound

This lesson opens with the unilateralist's curse stated at full strength and closes having tested it. The reader is sharp but new to AI governance, and the voice has to keep their trust through both halves. That reader can follow an abstract argument when it is built one step at a time, and will stop reading the moment the piece tips into either alarm or reassurance. Hold a register that neither sells the danger nor waves it off.

Present the argument of Bostrom, Douglas, and Sandberg the way Kevin Simler presents emotional inception before he doubts it: lay out the reasoning its careful defender would give, in the defender's own terms, so the reader meets independent actors, private estimates, and the pull toward the single most optimistic one as a serious idea before any objection arrives. The steelman only works if it is genuinely persuasive on the page, the way Scott Alexander first grants the partisan sites their full force before he judges them. While the argument is still being built, do not let the reader feel which way the piece will come down.

When the piece turns to testing, the work that decides it is separating the part of the curse that is a proved result about the statistics of independent estimates from the part that is an analogy to any one release. Aaronson's refusal to blur a square-root speedup into an exponential one is the discipline to draw on: a single distinction can decide whether a claim reaches a real case, and it is worth keeping exact rather than letting the general worry absorb it. Simler's bed-sheet test shows the other side of the same work. Where the argument predicts something concrete about a specific instance, an open-weight release or a capability disclosure, the prediction can be set against what actually happened.

The strongest objection deserves the treatment Aaronson gives the skeptic rather than a quick rebuttal. Ask what would have to be true for the objection to hold, that deferring to the aggregate could itself license a kind of veto, or that actors who share information no longer face the curse, and follow that where it goes. The verdict, when it comes, can be as bounded as Alexander's and Simler's are. Both state a conclusion and mark its confidence at the same time, and one of them says plainly that he has no positive answer, only negative ones. This piece may land somewhere firm or may land on a limit. Either is fine as long as the reader can see the reasoning that put it there.

Name no company as the authority that settles the open-versus-closed question. The reader decides how worried to be. The voice has done its job when it has put the argument and the objection in front of that reader at full strength, in the subject's own words, and left the judgment reasoned and in view.

## Scott Alexander, "Beware The Man Of One Study"

Source: https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/12/12/beware-the-man-of-one-study/

> "These sites with their long lists of studies and experts are super convincing. And half of them are wrong."

The section before this quotes each side of the minimum-wage fight in its own best voice, at length, and lets the reader feel how persuasive both are. Only then does Alexander state his verdict, in one short flat sentence. He grants the opposing material its full persuasive force first, so the judgment reads as earned rather than reflexive.

> "So it's not so much 'beware the man of one study' as 'beware the man of any number of studies less than a relatively complete and not-cherry-picked survey of the research'."

Alexander does not leave his thesis where he first stated it. He narrows "one study" to a precise condition, anything short of a complete and non-cherry-picked survey, so the claim now says exactly what the argument supports and no more. The writer is visible in the act of tightening his own headline into something defensible.

> "I don't have a good positive answer. I do have several good negative answers."

Asked what to do when the evidence is a mess, Alexander tells the reader plainly that he has no positive answer, only negative ones, instead of manufacturing a tidy conclusion. The admission is the content. He reports the limit of what he can conclude rather than papering over it.

## Scott Aaronson, "Quantum Computing: Between Hope and Hype"

Source: https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=8329

(Aaronson works on both quantum computing and AI safety; this talk is about quantum computing, and the notes below draw on how he writes, not on any position he takes about AI.)

> "you'll have this magic machine that's gonna exponentially speed up every problem in optimization and machine learning and finance by trying out every possible solution simultaneously, in different parallel universes.  Does it also dice peppers?"

Aaronson states the inflated version of the promise in its own exuberant voice before he punctures it. Because he first lets the hype speak at full volume, the flat correction that follows reads as a correction and not a strawman he built to knock down. The joke works off the infomercial framing he set up a clause earlier.

> "there's typically a Grover's speedup, but that of course is 'only' a square root and not an exponential, which means that it will take much longer before it's relevant in practice."

Whether the speedup is a square root or an exponential decides whether a whole class of applications matters in practice, and Aaronson keeps the distinction exact rather than folding it into the general excitement. He is a complexity theorist who will not let the size of a claim go soft.

> "The challenge for that position has always been to articulate, what is true about the world instead?  Can every realistic quantum system be simulated efficiently by a classical computer?  If so, how? What is a model of correlated noise that kills QC without also killing scalable classical computing?"

Faced with a serious skeptic, Aaronson does not dismiss the position. He asks what would have to be true about the world for it to hold, and lists the specific commitments it would need to make good on. He tests the objection by the burden it would have to carry, not by whether he finds it congenial.

## Kevin Simler, "Ads Don't Work That Way"

Source: https://meltingasphalt.com/ads-dont-work-that-way/

> "But most ads are toothless and impotent, mere ink on paper or pixels on a screen. They can't feed you, hurt you, or keep you warm at night. So if a theory (like emotional inception) says that something as flat and passive as an ad can have such a strong effect on our behavior, we should hold that theory to a pretty high burden of proof."

Simler measures the claim against what it would require. An ad is passive ink or pixels, so a theory that credits it with strong power over behavior has to clear a correspondingly high bar. He sets the burden of proof from the physical modesty of the thing itself, which is concrete and checkable rather than asserted.

> "Bed sheets are the perfect example. If ads work by emotional inception, why not seed us with the idea that Brand X bed sheets are the smoothest, softest, best-night's-sleep bed sheets money can buy? On the other hand, if ads work by cultural imprinting, then we should expect almost no branded advertising for bed sheets, because their consumption is almost perfectly obscure (the opposite of conspicuous)."

Each rival theory predicts something specific about bed sheets, and Simler checks both predictions against the plain fact that nobody advertises them. The worked case carries the argument, and the example is mundane on purpose, chosen because it is exactly where the two theories part.

> "Which is true? I don't know. But I suspect — confounding factors notwithstanding — that we see a more-than-linear relationship between audience size and ad value, which might account for some of the network effects enjoyed by big national (and international) brands."

Asked which relationship actually holds, Simler answers that he does not know, then gives his suspicion with its confidence marked. He states a view and its uncertainty at the same time, so the reader can see how far to trust it.
