# Evidence record: what-could-go-wrong/unilateralists-curse (01)

The primary argument is fully sourced and read in the original. Bostrom, Douglas
and Sandberg (2016) is open-access and I read all 22 pages: the model, its
assumptions, the group-vs-individual over-action result with its figures, the
principle of conformity, the three compliance models, and the authors' own
counter-case are all recorded below in their own terms with page locators. The
shown-vs-analogy line the commission asks for is well supported: what is proven
is a decision-theoretic result that holds under stated assumptions (independent
actors, a shared common-good payoff, private symmetric errors, naive
non-strategic agents); what is analogy is that any particular AI release is the
curse at work, because real releases violate several of those assumptions.

Two things are thinner than the commission's framing assumes, and both matter to
the draft. First, the explicit, named invocation of "the unilateralist's curse"
is scarce in the formal AI-governance release literature: the papers that argue
against open-weight release of the most capable models (Seger et al. 2023) or
that model the publish/withhold decision (Shevlane and Dafoe 2020) reason in the
curse's own structure — coordination, counterfactual possession, independent
discovery — but do not use the label. The clearest named application to AI is by
a critic, Stuart Armstrong (2016), and by Bostrom himself in a 2017 openness
paper that cites the 2016 result only in its bibliography. Second, the 2016 paper
contains no AI example at all; its own cases are dual-use biology, geoengineering,
nuclear secrecy, and media leaks. Every AI application is a later extension by
other hands. The real-system instances (Meta's open-weight Llama releases; the
GPT-2 staged release and its independent replication) are documented and match
the structure, but the match is by analogy, not proof.

## Sources

```text
URL:         https://nickbostrom.com/papers/unilateralist.pdf
             (published: Social Epistemology 30:4 (2016), 350-371,
             doi:10.1080/02691728.2015.1108373; open access, CC-BY)
Kind:        primary. Owns the argument, the model, and the proposed remedy.
             Authors: Nick Bostrom (Professor of Philosophy and Director of the
             Future of Humanity Institute, University of Oxford), Thomas Douglas
             (Senior Research Fellow in Philosophy, Oxford), Anders Sandberg
             (James Martin Research Fellow, Oxford).
Establishes: The unilateralist's curse, its assumptions, the over-action result,
             and the principle of conformity. Read in full (all 22 pages).
Paraphrase:  When several agents can each act alone on the same initiative X, and
             each acts if and only if her own private estimate of X's value (to a
             shared common good, not to herself) is positive, then when X's true
             value is negative the chance that at least one agent overestimates it
             enough to act rises with the number of agents. The group acts too
             often even though every agent is well-meaning and errors are
             symmetric. The remedy is a principle of conformity: reduce your
             chance of acting alone to the level that would be expected, ex ante,
             to cancel the group-level bias. Three routes to comply: (1) collective
             deliberation (share data and reasoning); (2) meta-rationality (a
             Bayesian adjusts down, "bid shading," conditioning on being the
             decisive/most-optimistic agent); (3) moral deference ("comply in
             action, defy in thought") via deferring to institutions/laws, an
             actual majority vote, or an imagined majority vote.
Locators:    Abstract and scenarios, p.350-352. Model, p.353. Six features,
             p.354-356. Principle of Conformity stated, p.357 and again p.365.
             Three models, sec. 3.1-3.3, p.357-364. Authors' counter-case
             (mavericks/Ellsberg), sec. 4, p.365-366. Concluding, p.366-367.
Quote:       "if each agent acts on her own personal judgment as to whether the
             initiative should be undertaken, then the initiative will be
             undertaken more often than is optimal. We suggest that this
             phenomenon, which we call the unilateralist's curse, arises in many
             contexts" (p.350).
             "each agent decides whether or not to undertake X on the basis of her
             own independent judgment of the value of X, where the value of X is
             assumed to be independent of who undertakes X, and is supposed to be
             determined by the contribution of X to the common good. Each agent's
             judgment is subject to error" (p.351).
             Principle of Conformity: "When acting out of concern for the common
             good in a unilateralist situation, reduce your likelihood of
             unilaterally undertaking or spoiling the initiative to a level that
             ex ante would be expected to lift the curse." (p.357)
             On the highest estimate: "it is the highest estimate that will
             determine whether an initiative is undertaken, not the average
             estimate." (p.355)
             Self-critique: "what is now widely recognized as important progress
             was instigated by the unilateral actions of mavericks, dissidents,
             and visionaries" (p.365); "The claim that the unilateralist curse is
             an important phenomenon and that we have reason to lift it is
             consistent with the claim that the curse has provided a net benefit
             to humanity." (p.366)
```

URL:         https://social-epistemology.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/armstrong_reply_bostrom.pdf
             (Social Epistemology Review and Reply Collective, 2016, 5:4, 33-36;
             reader-facing landing page:
             https://social-epistemology.com/2016/04/13/response-to-the-unilateralists-curse-stuart-armstrong/ )
Kind:        primary. A published reply that owns its critique. Author: Stuart
             Armstrong, Oxford University (Future of Humanity Institute; also
             thanked in the 2016 paper's acknowledgments). Note the tie: he is not
             a fully independent outsider, but the piece is a stated dissent.
Establishes: The strongest argued primary critique in the "relaxing assumptions
             / existing institutions dissolve it in practice" family, and a named
             application to AI. Read in full (4 pages).
Paraphrase:  Armstrong grants the result is correct in theory ("has the advantage
             of being true") but argues its practical reach is narrow, because
             society already corrects for it. International agreements, regulation,
             markets, and other adversarial systems force information-sharing and
             overrule lone optimists in most domains, and those systems are
             designed to work without every actor being individually rational.
             He argues unilateral action is often good: entrepreneurship and
             scientific innovation depend on people pursuing projects the majority
             thinks worthless. So the curse "may be a valid individual warning for
             individual entrepreneurs ... but fails as an overall judgement of the
             entrepreneurship." It bites hardest, he says, only where society has
             not pre-installed protections: tail risks, under-regulated areas, and
             new technologies. He names "future -- not current -- artificial
             intelligence" as a case that is a tail risk, under-regulated, and new
             at once, where awareness of the curse becomes necessary. Crucially he
             adds the curse "doesn't mean they shouldn't do it, just that they
             shouldn't rely on their own naive initial judgement."
Locators:    Concession, p.33 ("has the advantage of being true"). Institutions
             argument, p.33-34. Entrepreneurship point, p.34. Three domains and
             the "future not current AI" line, p.35-36. Caution-not-prohibition
             gloss, p.36.
Quote:       "The Unilateralist's curse may thus be a valid individual warning for
             individual entrepreneurs ('you are unlikely to succeed') but fails as
             an overall judgement of the entrepreneurship ('it is good that your
             try')." (p.34)
             "areas where society has not pre-installed protective measures. In
             these areas (such as future -- not current -- artificial
             intelligence, which is a simultaneous under-regulated tail risk new
             technology) extra caution is necessary" (p.36).

## Contradictions

- The commission says AI open-weight release is "exactly the structure the model
  describes." It is the structure only if the assumptions are read loosely. The
  2016 model assumes agents are purely altruistic ("all agents act for the common
  good," p.354) and that errors are independent and symmetric (p.354). Commercial
  AI labs act on mixed motives (Meta's own stated reasoning is safety plus
  commercial and geopolitical interest), and lab judgments are likely correlated
  because they read the same safety literature. Bostrom, Douglas and Sandberg note
  that self-interested action is a different case with different failure modes
  (note 10, p.368), and that skewed or correlated errors change the analysis
  (note 11, p.368). So the AI mapping is an analogy that imports assumptions the
  situation may not meet. This is the shown-vs-analogy line, and it is the record's
  main tension with the commission's wording, not a refutation of the angle.

- The remedy can point the other way. Shevlane and Dafoe's "counterfactual
  possession / independent discovery" and Zuckerberg's "thumb drive" argument both
  say that when others will release or reproduce the capability anyway, withholding
  achieves little. The GPT-2/OpenGPT-2 case is the concrete proof: caution by one
  actor did not hold. This cuts against a strong reading of the principle of
  conformity and toward Armstrong's point that coordination and existing
  institutions, not individual restraint, are what actually manage the curse.

- The authors partly argue against their own remedy. Section 4 concedes that
  historically celebrated progress often came from unilateral mavericks (Ellsberg
  and the Pentagon Papers), and that if a group already has a bias against
  unilateralism, conformity would make it worse (p.365-367). Armstrong sharpens
  this into a general claim that the curse "fails as an overall judgement of the
  entrepreneurship" (p.34). Any draft that presents conformity as an unqualified
  good contradicts the primary source itself.

- The meta-rationality section shows the curse is a curse only for naive agents.
  Sufficiently rational Bayesian agents "can manage the curse even without
  communication" by conditioning on being the decisive actor (p.358), and Aumann's
  agreement theorem implies rational agents with common priors cannot persistently
  disagree (p.358). The result is robust for naive actors, fragile for ideal ones.
  Real actors sit in between, which is exactly why the AI mapping is contestable.
