## The argument and the angle

The desk teaches one argument about how AI could go wrong, at full strength, then
tests it. The argument: the unilateralist's curse. When many actors can each act
alone on the same choice — release a model, publish a capability — and each acts
whenever their own estimate of the value is positive, the action happens as soon
as the single most optimistic actor's estimate is too high. A group of
well-meaning, independent decision-makers therefore takes risky actions more
often than it should, even if the average judgment is against acting. The remedy
Bostrom, Douglas, and Sandberg propose is a principle of conformity: defer toward
the group's aggregate estimate rather than your own.

Open with it at full strength. Name who made it (Nick Bostrom, Thomas Douglas,
Anders Sandberg, 2016) and the reasoning its careful defender would give, so the
reader sees why serious people in AI governance invoke it before reading a word
against it.

Then draw the line between what is shown and what is analogy. What is shown: the
curse is a decision-theoretic result about the statistics of independent
estimates, demonstrable in the abstract, and it has concrete present-day
instances in AI — open-weight model releases and capability disclosures, where
one lab releasing what others held back is exactly the structure the model
describes. What is analogy: that any specific release will be catastrophic; the
model says the bias exists, not that a given action is wrong. Bring it to the
present: who invokes it now (frontier-safety and open-vs-closed-release debates)
and the strongest objection — that the same logic can license excessive
conformity or a heckler's veto, and that information-sharing and coordination
change the result. Name no company as an authority; leave the reader to decide.
