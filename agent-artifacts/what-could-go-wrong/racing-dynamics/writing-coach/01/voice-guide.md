# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/racing-dynamics

## Directive

Register: a strategist explaining a model to a smart reader who has never seen
it, not a debater trying to win a side. Reader relationship: no direct
address, no narrating your own move ("notice that," "consider," "as we'll
see"). Authority comes from precision, not rapport.

Build the model bottom-up, from one actor's decision, before naming the
general pattern. Give the actor a concrete identity — a specific lab, a
specific board, a specific funding deadline, a specific competitor it is
watching — the way a strategist assigns a name to a boat in a strait rather
than a slot in a matrix. State its situation as a short chain of plain
sentences ("It has reason to think X. If it does A, then B. But if it does C,
then D."), so the tension emerges from the chain instead of being announced.
Only after the single-actor case is built, generalize to the population of
actors and let the pattern earn its label. Never drop the model's name (or any
term of art) before the sentence that has built it.

For the counterintuitive result (more information making an actor less safe,
not more): walk it as a worked case with running numbers or a named sequence
of moves, the way a toy example carries a surprising equilibrium in one
arithmetic step the reader can check, not as an assertion backed by the word
"paradox." State the result once it's shown, don't announce that it's coming.

Mark the turn from steelman to scrutiny with a plain declarative move ("Test
this against what has happened" / "That argument rests on X; check X"), never
with a "not X but Y" contrast — reserve that contrast, at most once, for a
named misconception a real camp actually holds, never a strawman built to
correct. Most of the piece's work of correcting overconfidence should happen
by adding a fact the model's proponents or critics didn't have, not by
announcing that either side is wrong.

Keep report, the model's if-then logic, and your own synthesis on different
grammatical footing at every point they meet: a sourced claim gets a plain
subject-verb sentence and a citation; the model's conditional gets an
explicit "if... then"; your own read gets a marked first-person or
qualified claim ("the evidence so far suggests," not "clearly"). When you
don't know, say so in one flat sentence rather than a hedge stacked across a
paragraph. When a claim about future systems is analogy rather than
demonstrated fact, flag it in the sentence that makes it, not two paragraphs
later.

Name institutions and incentives by their concrete particulars — the
company, the contract, the regulator, the shareholder vote, the compute
budget, the specific person who made a decision and when — never by a
collective abstraction standing in for all of them. Two strings are banned
outright in this piece and must not be replaced by synonyms that carry the
same vagueness: don't reach for "arms race"-style collective-noun framing to
describe what specific labs and governments are doing, and don't reach for
generic machine/apparatus words to describe what specific incentives and
institutions are doing. Say who, which body, and what deadline instead.

Recently used, do not reuse:
- Comma-triad headings and deks (three clauses joined by commas and closed
  with "and").
- The jailbreaks-piece heading mold that resolves into an aphorism like
  "teaches a pattern, not a boundary" — any heading or closing line built to
  be quotable on its own.
- Any reusable closer formula or house catchphrase repeated across articles.
- The strings "AI race" / "artificial intelligence race", "load-bearing",
  and "machinery" are banned in this paper. Do not substitute a synonym that
  carries the same vagueness (e.g. "arms race," "engine," "apparatus"); name
  the actual institution, actor, or incentive instead.

## Exemplars

## Holden Karnofsky, "Racing through a minefield: the AI deployment problem"
Source: https://www.cold-takes.com/racing-through-a-minefield-the-ai-deployment-problem/
Craft:
- cadence: builds a single company's situation in short declarative steps
  ("It has good reason to think... If it deploys... But if it moves too
  slowly...") before naming the general dilemma, so the reader derives the
  tension instead of being told it exists.
- argument: introduces the strategic pattern as one imagined actor's decision
  problem first, so the abstraction arrives already anchored in a case a
  reader can picture.
- evidence: restates his own prior claims from earlier posts in one clause
  each before building on them, so what's assumed and what's newly argued
  stay visibly separate.
- stance: takes the dilemma seriously without joining either the "move fast"
  or "go slow" camp; names the actual tradeoff both camps are weighing.
- notice: flags his own analogy as an analogy in the sentence that
  introduces it, rather than letting a metaphor pass as description.
- diction: plain policy prose, short sentences, a coined term ("deployment
  problem") defined in the same sentence it's coined.
- reader: assumes someone following a series, restates prior ground in a
  single clause rather than re-arguing it.
- the move the axes missed: generalizes from one actor's incentives to many
  actors' incentives in sequence, so the population-level pattern is never
  asserted top-down.
Calibration: "So, one can imagine a scenario where some company is in the
following situation: It has good reason to think it's on the cusp of
developing extraordinarily powerful AI systems. If it deploys such systems
hastily, global disaster could result. But if it moves too slowly, other,
less cautious actors could deploy dangerous systems of their own."

## Scott Alexander, "Meditations on Moloch"
Source: https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/07/30/meditations-on-moloch/
Craft:
- cadence: carries the fish-farm example on running dollar figures instead
  of describing the trap abstractly, so the arithmetic itself delivers the
  surprise.
- argument: shows the "everyone acted rationally" reading of a bad outcome
  in full before naming the trap, so the logic lands before it gets a label.
- evidence: uses a constructed toy case as demonstration, then marks the
  shift when he moves to a real case as illustration rather than proof.
- stance: withholds judgment until the mechanism is fully built; doesn't
  editorialize mid-explanation.
- notice: states the exact comparison that makes the trap visible — what an
  actor earns under the bad equilibrium versus what they'd earn if everyone
  had cooperated.
- diction: conversational but exact; gives toy actors names instead of
  "Agent A," so the logic reads as a sequence of choices, not algebra.
- reader: narrates his own reaction mid-argument ("I mean, by what
  standard...") — a first-person interjection to avoid; the floor's ban on
  self-reference and on "Consider"/"Notice" openers rules this move out here.
- the move the axes missed: names the general pattern only after building
  the specific case, so the term is earned rather than dropped as jargon.
Calibration: "Steve is earning $600/month – less than he would be if he and
everyone else had kept their filters on!"

## Katja Grace, "Counterarguments to the basic AI x-risk case"
Source: https://aiimpacts.org/counterarguments-to-the-basic-ai-x-risk-case/
Craft:
- cadence: opens by stating exactly what kind of document follows (a list of
  holes in a named argument) instead of a rhetorical frame, so the reader
  knows the mode of scrutiny from sentence one.
- argument: restates the case under review as a numbered chain of claims
  before touching any of them, keeping steelman and critique in separate
  passes rather than blended paragraph by paragraph.
- evidence: attaches an explicit confidence qualifier to her own synthesis,
  distinguishing her estimate from the field's evidence in the same
  sentence.
- stance: grants the argument's strongest form real force even while
  cataloguing its gaps, refusing both easy dismissal and alarm.
- notice: coins a precise working term exactly where a standard term breaks
  down, and defines it in the sentence that coins it.
- diction: dry and technical without hedging into vagueness; qualifies scope
  precisely rather than reaching for an intensifier.
- reader: writes for someone already tracking the argument's structure, so
  she can refer to a premise by its place in the chain instead of
  re-explaining the case each time.
- the move the axes missed: keeps "the argument I'm listing objections to"
  and "my own view" on visibly different footing throughout, so a reader
  always knows which one is on the page.
Calibration: "My current guess (prior to reviewing other arguments and
integrating things carefully) is that enough uncertainties might resolve in
the dangerous directions that existential risk from AI is a reasonable
concern."

## Thomas Schelling, "An Astonishing Sixty Years: The Legacy of Hiroshima"
Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1458836/
Craft:
- cadence: opens on a fact stated as understatement, then lets the next
  sentence supply the scale, instead of front-loading the drama.
- argument: names the actual institution and person behind an abstraction —
  a specific Secretary of State, a specific year — rather than crediting
  "norms" in general.
- evidence: marks a whole category of claims as unknowable in one flat
  sentence instead of hedging clause by clause across a paragraph.
- stance: credits luck alongside design rather than claiming a good outcome
  was fully deliberate.
- notice: names the exact mechanism holding an equilibrium in place — a
  shared taboo, not a treaty — and what enforces it.
- diction: short, aphoristic sentences carry the analytic weight; no
  throat-clearing before the claim itself.
- reader: never addresses the reader directly; authority comes from
  precision, not rapport.
- the move the axes missed: treats a stable outcome (restraint holding) as
  the surprising result needing explanation, the same shape this article
  needs for an equilibrium that holds rather than collapses.
Calibration: "Taboos are not easily dispelled by pronouncing them extinct,
even in the mind of one who does the pronouncing."
