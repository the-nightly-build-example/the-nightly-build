## Source policy
what-could-go-wrong lesson: **min 8 sources; primary ≥ 4, secondary ≥ 1.**
Work from originals, not commentary. **Name no company as an authority** — a
developer's on-record statement is evidence of what that developer said, not
proof of a claim. Classify carefully.

## Required primary documents (read first-hand)
1. **Armstrong, Bostrom & Shulman, "Racing to the precipice: a model of
   artificial intelligence development," *AI & Society* 31:201–206 (2016)**;
   DOI 10.1007/s00146-015-0590-y. Open text: ORA Oxford
   (https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:d87d8e34-22d6-4597-ac31-041fcb63903f) or
   FHI. Read the whole model. Record precisely: the setup (n teams, the
   winner-take-all-ish payoff, safety as costly), the main result (competition
   lowers safety investment), and the **information result** (more information
   about capabilities can reduce safety; the role of "enmity"). Capture the
   exact statements and any toy numbers/figures.
2. **Bostrom, *Superintelligence* (2014)** — the treatment of technology races /
   collective-action failure in AI development, if you can read the relevant
   pages first-hand. Record the argument as he states it.

## Also gather (to test and update the argument)
3. **A present-day proponent primary**: a document stating what today's
   proponents of coordination want done — e.g. a responsible-scaling policy, a
   compute/frontier-governance proposal, or a formal coordination proposal. Get
   the specific asks, first-hand.
4. **Empirical evidence on competitive/schedule pressure and safety
   tradeoffs**: on-record statements or a governance/safety-index report
   bearing on whether developers actually cut safety under time pressure, and
   whether AI has shown a decisive first-mover advantage or has instead been
   incremental and fast-followed. Two independent confirmations for any specific
   accusation that a named developer cut safety.

## Research questions to answer with exact readings
- State the model's assumptions crisply enough to audit each: (a) decisive/
  winner-take-all first-mover advantage; (b) safety trades against speed; (c)
  small number of players; (d) any assumption about capability being observable.
- The information result, stated exactly, with the paper's reasoning.
- The empirical status of assumption (a): has any AI system produced a decisive,
  durable first-mover lock-in, or has the pattern been incremental gains and
  rapid fast-following? Gather concrete, cited datapoints (release timelines,
  capability gaps closing) — from the record, attributed carefully.
- Present-day: who presses the argument and who dismisses it, and the strongest
  statement of each side (steelman both), so the writer can weigh them.

## Contradictions to hunt (central to the piece)
- The winner-take-all assumption vs the observed fast-follow pattern.
- Doom framing vs dismissal framing of the same evidence: capture the strongest
  version of each.
- Whether "more information → less safety" survives outside the model's
  assumptions (any critique of the model on record).
