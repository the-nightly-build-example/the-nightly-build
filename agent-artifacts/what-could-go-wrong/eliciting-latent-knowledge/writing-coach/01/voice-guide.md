# Voice guide: eliciting latent knowledge (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a lesson for a reader who is sharp and widely read and has spent no time inside a model. Eliciting latent knowledge is abstract before it is anything else: the question of whether a system reports what it internally represents, or reports whatever a human checker will approve. Carroll's monkeys-and-Hamlet passage shows one way to seat an abstract mechanism in a small, checkable scene before the general point is named, and the problem here has a core that a single worked case can carry the same way. Hold the plainest register that still holds the idea, and where a sentence has to choose between sounding impressive and being followed, take being followed.

The desk opens each argument as its most careful defender would put it. Carroll's "cognitively unstable" passage states a worry at full force and names exactly where it bites, giving the term and its definition in the same breath, and his exclamation shows a writer who finds the conclusion unsettling without performing alarm. When the latent-knowledge worry is laid out, it can be given that seriousness: the strongest form stated plainly, with the one word of art the reader lacks defined where it first appears.

The discipline the series turns on is a clean line between what a working system has already shown and what is still analogy or thought experiment. Carroll's "loopy" passage draws that line by conceding how strange the claim sounds and then separating the sound from the grounded basis underneath it. Potter's close labels his own optimistic case as something that "could theoretically achieve" its aim and names the open question in the same paragraph. Where this argument rests partly on systems that do not yet exist, the lesson can mark that boundary as plainly, keeping the force of the worry and the absence of a demonstration in view together, and naming the gap on either side when confidence runs ahead of the evidence.

Luu's dialogue turns "how do you know?" into a four-line exchange whose point is that one side never looked, and the question of how anyone could check what a system knows sits close to the center of this lesson; a short dramatized case can make that gap concrete before the abstract statement of it. Luu's "A/B test of universes" passage marks the limit of his own evidence before he offers a calibrated bet, and Potter checks a claim of novelty against the history before crediting it. Where this lesson reports what has and has not been demonstrated about getting a system to tell you what it knows, those moves are available: state the limit of the evidence in the open, and prefer the specific case or figure where the evidence supports one.

Luu's "hope is not a strategy" verdict is blunt and specific, and it lands only because the documented cases came before it; a verdict in this lesson is earned the same way, after the evidence and in the reader's own terms. Carroll's "This is the real world, baby" and Potter's aside about the trademark are small, unforced touches that let a person show through explanatory prose, and the register stays plain even where it is most pointed.

## Sean Carroll, "The Higgs Boson vs. Boltzmann Brains"

Source: https://preposterousuniverse.com/blog/2013/08/22/the-higgs-boson-vs-boltzmann-brains/

> "Let’s say you find a monkey who has just typed Act I of Hamlet with perfect fidelity. You might think “aha, here’s when it happens,” and expect Act II to come next. But by the conditions of the experiment, the next thing the monkey types should be perfectly random (by which we mean, chosen from a uniform distribution among all allowed typographical characters), and therefore independent of what has come before. The chances that you will actually get Act II next, just because you got Act I, are extraordinarily tiny."

Carroll builds the abstract point about random fluctuations out of a small scene the reader can check: a monkey that has just typed Act I, and the reader's wrong guess that Act II follows. The technical rule of the experiment sits inside a parenthesis, so it arrives as part of the story rather than beside it. The writer is visible in the patience of walking all the way through one example before drawing anything from it.

> "That’s not really the point, though; the real problem is that such a fluctuation scenario is cognitively unstable — you can’t simultaneously believe it’s true, and have good reason for believing its true, because it predicts that all the “reasons” you think are so good have just randomly fluctuated into your head!"

Here Carroll states the worry at full strength and names exactly where it bites: the scenario is "cognitively unstable" because you cannot both believe it and have good reason to. He gives the term and its meaning together, and the exclamation mark shows a physicist who finds the conclusion genuinely unsettling. Note that he first sets aside the vivid image of disembodied brains to reach the point that actually matters to him.

> "And, let’s admit it, the very idea of orderly configurations of matter spontaneously fluctuating out of chaos sounds a bit loopy, as critics have noted. But everything I’ve just said is based on physics we think we understand: quantum field theory, general relativity, and the cosmological constant. This is the real world, baby. Of course it’s possible that we are making some subtle mistake about how quantum field theory works, but that is more speculative than taking the straightforward prediction seriously."

Carroll grants that the idea sounds "a bit loopy," and that critics have said so, before separating the strange sound of the claim from its grounded basis in known physics. "This is the real world, baby" is a plain aside that shows his conviction without inflating the claim, and the sentence after it weighs one uncertainty against another instead of declaring the matter settled. The person shows in the willingness to concede how odd it sounds and then defend it anyway.

## Dan Luu, "Measurement, benchmarking, and data analysis are underrated"

Source: https://danluu.com/why-benchmark/

> "Person A : Database X corrupted my data. Person B : It works for me. It's never corrupted my data. A : How do you know? Do you ever check for data corruption? B : What do you mean? I'd know if we had data corruption"

Luu turns an abstract question about reliability into a short exchange between two people, and lets it turn on the line "How do you know? Do you ever check for data corruption?" The dialogue makes the gap concrete: one person's confidence rests entirely on never having looked. The writer is visible in the ear for how these arguments actually run, down to keeping the second speaker's non-answer.

> "Although we don't have an A/B test of universes where Kyle exists vs. not and can't say how long it would've taken for distributed systems to get serious about correctness in a universe where Kyle didn't exist, from having spent many years looking at how developers treat correctness bugs, I would bet on distributed systems having rampant correctness problems until someone like Kyle came along."

Luu marks the limit of his own evidence before he states a view. He admits there is no "A/B test of universes" to settle the counterfactual, then gives a bet grounded in "many years looking at how developers treat correctness bugs." The person is visible in the refusal to round an honest "I would bet" up to a certainty.

> "I'm reminded of the SRE motto, "hope is not a strategy". Trusting vendors is not a strategy. We know that vendors will lie and cheat to look better at benchmarks."

Luu borrows an existing motto, "hope is not a strategy," and applies it in three short sentences that say plainly who does what: vendors lie and cheat to score better on benchmarks. The verdict is blunt and specific, and it holds because the paragraphs before it laid out the documented cases. The person is visible in the plain willingness to say vendors cheat and leave it unsoftened.

## Brian Potter, "The Prefab Pivot"

Source: https://www.construction-physics.com/p/the-prefab-pivot

> "And at the micro-geography level, every building site will be a little bit different, and impose its own set of constraints and uncertainties - a blocked view, an inconvenient boulder, a 40 inch gas line that must be built around, etc."

Potter takes an abstract claim, that geography constrains construction, and lands it on specifics: a blocked view, an inconvenient boulder, a 40 inch gas line to build around. The list of ordinary obstacles does the explaining, so the abstraction arrives already furnished with cases. The writer is visible in the choice of homely, physical detail over a second general restatement.

> "The idea of construction using a standard ‘kit of parts’ is also not new. But it’s previously taken shape differently"

Before treating the "kit of parts" idea as new, Potter says plainly that it is not, and that it had appeared before in a different form. He separates what is genuinely novel here from what the field has already seen. The person shows in the instinct to check a claim of novelty against the history before letting it stand.

> "This is an optimistic view of what a construction platform could theoretically achieve. It’s an open question if any of these companies will be able to achieve it, or even if they’re aiming at such an expansive vision."

After building the optimistic case across the whole piece, Potter labels it for what it is: an optimistic view of what the approach "could theoretically achieve," followed at once by the open question of whether any company will. He keeps the appeal of the idea and the absence of proof in the same paragraph. The person is visible in stating the open question outright instead of implying the case is closed.
