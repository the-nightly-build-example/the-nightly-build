# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/natural-selection

## How this piece should sound

The reader is smart and reads widely and has never been walked through the
natural-selection argument for AI risk. Write for someone who can follow real
detail and will notice if you dodge it. Scott Alexander assumes his reader can
sit with funnel plots, competing meta-analyses, and the difference between acute
and maintenance treatment; the piece never slows down to reassure them. Hold the
three Darwinian conditions (variation among systems, traits that carry across
copying and finetuning, competition for compute and market share and users) at
that level of detail, and define each term in the sentence it first appears
rather than softening it away.

Lay the argument out in its own believer's terms before you test any part of it.
Aaronson states the overclaim he is about to correct in the exact shape a
layperson holds it, a magic machine that speeds up every problem "by trying out
every possible solution simultaneously, in different parallel universes," and
only then answers it. Give Hendrycks's case that same courtesy: put selection
pressure at the level of a population of systems and firms, favoring AIs that are
more capable, more autonomous, better at acquiring resources, and better at
deceiving overseers, stated so a serious reader sees why it worries serious
people, before the line between shown and analogy is drawn.

When you say a fragment has actually been demonstrated, name the specific result
and the setup it ran in. Aaronson's optimism rests on a number he can point to,
two-qubit gate fidelities that "became 90%, became 95%, became 99%, and within
the past year, multiple groups have reported 99.9%," not on a mood. Reward
hacking, specification gaming, and lab-induced power-seeking or deception are the
places this piece can point to something real; say in what contrived conditions
each was produced, so the reader can judge for themselves how far it is from a
self-sustaining evolutionary dynamic in the wild.

Give each of the three conditions the gradation it earns, and refuse the collapse
into either doom or dismissal. Karnofsky calls his futurists "mediocre" and in
the same breath rules out "hopeless" or "clueless" or "a warning sign to all who
dare predict the long-run future," because the evidence supported the middle
verdict and not the extreme in either direction. Some of what the argument needs
is plainly present in AI development and some of it, particularly heritability in
the strong sense and any observed wild selection, is not, and the piece can say
so condition by condition without rounding to a single number.

Check the confidence on both sides against what has been shown, and hand the
burden to whoever is claiming more than the evidence carries. Aaronson takes the
skeptic Gil Kalai seriously, cites the specific experiments that bear on the
claim, then says "the ball is now firmly in their court." The boosters who treat
a wild evolutionary process among AIs as established and the dismissers who wave
the whole argument away both outrun the proof, and the piece can name each gap in
this argument's own nouns rather than grading the argument as a whole.

Be honest about the limits of your own answer where the evidence runs out. Scott
Alexander writes "I don't have a good positive answer. I do have several good
negative answers," and Karnofsky, having done the work, still says the reader
should "make a judgment call" because he "could easily imagine others having a
different interpretation." The question of how worried to be is left to the
reader here, and the closing can say plainly what is settled and what is still
analogy without manufacturing a resolution the evidence has not reached. A dry
aside is allowed when it depends on the subject and not on a wink at the reader.
Karnofsky can drop "(Well, maybe Heinlein was.)" because the joke is about
Heinlein. It is never a thing to reach for, only a thing to keep when the nouns
supply it.

## Scott Aaronson, "Quantum Computing: Between Hope and Hype"

Source: https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=8329

> "For the general public, maybe the overriding reason not to take QC seriously
> has just been that it sounded too good to be true. Like, great, you'll have
> this magic machine that's gonna exponentially speed up every problem in
> optimization and machine learning and finance by trying out every possible
> solution simultaneously, in different parallel universes. Does it also dice
> peppers?"

Aaronson states the inflated version of his own field in the words its believers
actually use, and the joke lands because it is aimed at that specific overclaim
rather than at the reader. He is a working quantum-computing theorist who has
spent years correcting this exact picture, and the impatience with it is his own.

> "If someone asks me why I'm now so optimistic, the core of the argument is
> 2-qubit gate fidelities. ... When I entered the field, in the late 1990s, it
> would've been like a Science or Nature paper to do a 2-qubit gate with 50%
> fidelity. But then at some point the 50% became 90%, became 95%, became 99%,
> and within the past year, multiple groups have reported 99.9%. So, if you just
> plot the log of the infidelity as a function of year and stare at it—yeah,
> you'd feel pretty optimistic about the next decade too!"

His optimism is pinned to one measurable trajectory, and he shows the numbers
that moved rather than asserting that progress happened. The plain "stare at
it—yeah" is a specialist inviting the reader to look at the same evidence he did
and reach the same judgment.

> "In any case, I think this position has been dealt a severe blow by the Random
> Circuit Sampling quantum supremacy experiments of the past five years.
> Scientifically, the most important thing we've learned from these experiments
> is that the fidelity seems to decay exponentially with the number of qubits,
> but "only" exponentially — as it would if the errors were independent from one
> gate to the next, precisely as the theory of quantum fault-tolerance assumes.
> So for anyone who believes this objection, I'd say that the ball is now firmly
> in their court."

Aaronson names the skeptic's position, states the specific experimental result
that bears on it, and only then assigns the burden of proof. The reader watches
him weigh a real objection on evidence instead of dismissing it, which is why the
final sentence reads as a fair call rather than a taunt.

## Holden Karnofsky, "The Track Record of Futurists Seems ... Fine"

Source: https://www.cold-takes.com/the-track-record-of-futurists-seems-fine/

> "I selected them by vaguely remembering that "the Big Three of science
> fiction" is a thing people say sometimes, googling it, and going with who came
> up - no hunting around for lots of sci-fi authors and picking the best or
> worst."

Karnofsky shows his method in full, including how casual it was, so the reader
can see he did not stack the sample to reach his conclusion. The self-deprecating
candor is the writer telling you exactly where his evidence came from before he
asks you to trust what he built on it.

> "They weren't infallible oracles, but they weren't blindly casting about
> either. (Well, maybe Heinlein was.) Collectively, I think you could call them
> "mediocre," but you can't call them "hopeless" or "clueless" or "a warning
> sign to all who dare predict the long-run future.""

He settles on a middle verdict and marks its edges by naming the extreme
positions he is refusing, each one a phrase someone might actually reach for. The
parenthetical about Heinlein is a real judgment about one of the three, dropped
in without breaking the measured tone.

> "(How do we determine what level of accuracy would be "fine" vs. "bad?"
> Unfortunately there's no clear quantitative benchmark - I think we just have to
> look at the predictions ourselves, how hard they seemed / how similar to
> today's predictions about AI, and make a judgment call...)"

Karnofsky admits there is no clean metric for the thing he is assessing and tells
the reader the verdict is a judgment they could reasonably reach differently. It
is a writer refusing to launder an interpretive call as a measurement.

## Scott Alexander, "Beware The Man Of One Study"

Source: https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/12/12/beware-the-man-of-one-study/

> "I think medical science is still pretty healthy, and that the consensus of
> doctors and researchers is more-or-less right on most controversial medical
> issues."
>
> "(it's the uncontroversial ones you have to worry about)"

Alexander gives a plain, qualified verdict and then undercuts his own reassurance
with a one-line aside that carries a real point about where error hides. The
casualness is deliberate, and the parenthetical works because it is about
controversial versus uncontroversial claims specifically.

> "We see it's centered around 0, which means there's some evidence that's the
> real signal among all this noise. The bell skews more to left than to the
> right, which means more studies have found negative effects of the minimum
> wage than positive effects of the minimum wage. But since the bell curve is
> asymmetrical, we intepret that as probably publication bias. So all in all, I
> think there's at least some evidence that the liberals are right on this one."

After pages showing how both sides marshal studies, he reaches a conclusion that
is committed but carefully sized, "at least some evidence," walking the reader
through the plot that took him there. The verdict is earned in front of you and
never inflated past what the one figure supports.

> "I don't have a good positive answer. I do have several good negative answers."
>
> "Decrease your confidence about most things if you're not sure that you've
> investigated every piece of evidence."

Alexander states outright that he cannot give the reader a clean rule, then gives
the honest partial guidance he does have. Admitting the gap is what makes the
advice that follows trustworthy, and the voice is a person who would rather be
useful than sound complete.
