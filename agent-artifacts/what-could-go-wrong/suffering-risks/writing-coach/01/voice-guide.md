## How this piece should sound

This lesson makes a speculative argument at full strength and then checks it
against what current systems actually do, so the writing has to hold both
modes without turning cold in the first and excited in the second. Piper puts
an unfamiliar risk next to a familiar one and then says exactly what is
different about it; this lesson can use the same move each time it crosses
from a documented fact about a real system to a claim about a system that
does not exist yet. Say what has actually been shown, then say plainly that
what follows is reasoning past it.

When the argument reaches for scale, follow Karnofsky's habit of stating the
skeptical view with real numbers attached, not as a gesture at "skeptics."
The reader meeting "astronomical" for the first time needs the term built
from a specific case, the way Karnofsky builds his own claim against a
stated alternative timeline, rather than from a synonym for "very large."

Singer's drowning child tests an abstract principle against one small,
physical scene instead of restating the principle more forcefully. This
lesson names several pathways to suffering — conflict between agents, an
indifferent optimizer, deliberate design — and at least one of them will land
better from a single worked case than from the category name alone.

State the piece's own confidence the way Karnofsky states his: at a specific
level, with the reason for that level given in the same sentence, rather than
as either conviction or caution on its own. Facing the argument's most
extreme conclusion, take Singer's approach: say what premise would have to
be false for a reader to reject it, rather than a general sense of how far the
argument goes.

The present-day section, where the desk's job is to teach how the field
reasons without joining it, can use Piper's economy for handling a live
debate: name the version of the disagreement a reader would reach for
first, then say in the next sentence why the actual disagreement is narrower
or different, rather than spending a paragraph on the correction.

## Peter Singer, "Famine, Affluence, and Morality"

Source: https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil308/Singer2.pdf (*Philosophy & Public Affairs* 1, no. 3, 1972)

> "As I write this, in November 1971, people are dying in East Bengal from
> lack of food, shelter, and medical care. The suffering and death that are
> occurring there now are not inevitable, not unavoidable in any fatalistic
> sense of the term."

The essay opens with a dated fact and a flat denial of inevitability, not a
preview of the thesis. Singer states the situation before he states his
argument, so the reader has something to check before being asked to agree
with anything, and there is no sentence of throat-clearing ahead of it.

> "if I am walking past a shallow pond and see a child drowning in it, I
> ought to wade in and pull the child out. This will mean getting my clothes
> muddy, but this is insignificant, while the death of the child would
> presumably be a very bad thing."

Singer tests an abstract moral principle by running it through one small,
physical scene rather than restating the principle more forcefully. The
comparison is between muddy clothes and a child's death, two things a reader
can actually weigh against each other, not two abstractions.

> "My conclusion follows from the principle which I advanced earlier, and
> unless that principle is rejected, or the arguments are shown to be
> unsound, I think the conclusion must stand, however strange it appears."

Singer names the strangeness of his own conclusion in the same sentence that
defends it, instead of hiding the strangeness or apologizing for it. He
states what would have to be false for the reader to reject the conclusion,
which puts the disagreement on a specific premise rather than on a general
feeling that the argument has gone too far.

## Kelsey Piper, "The Case for Taking AI Seriously as a Threat to Humanity"

Source: https://www.vox.com/future-perfect/2018/12/21/18126576/ai-artificial-intelligence-machine-learning-safety-alignment

> "The conversation about AI is full of confusion, misinformation, and people
> talking past each other — in large part because we use the word 'AI' to
> refer to so many things."

Piper diagnoses the confusion around the topic by naming its mechanical
cause, a single overloaded word, rather than by asserting that the topic is
confusing. The sentence gives the reader a specific thing to watch for in
everything that follows it.

> "It's immediately clear how nuclear bombs will kill us. No one working on
> mitigating nuclear risk has to start by explaining why it'd be a bad thing
> if we had a nuclear war."

Piper anchors the section in a comparison the reader already accepts before
she turns to the harder case it sets up. The two short sentences do
different jobs: the first states a fact, the second states what that fact
lets a certain kind of expert skip saying.

> "It's tempting to conclude that there's a pitched battle between AI-risk
> skeptics and AI-risk believers. In reality, they might not disagree as
> profoundly as you would think."

Piper names the easy, dramatic reading of a debate and declines it in the
very next sentence, without spending a paragraph on the correction. The word
"tempting" locates the oversimplification in the reader's own expectation
rather than in a strawman invented to knock down.

## Holden Karnofsky, "The Most Important Century (in a nutshell)"

Source: https://www.cold-takes.com/the-most-important-century-in-a-nutshell/

> "Many people find the 'most important century' claim too 'wild': a radical
> future with advanced AI and civilization spreading throughout our galaxy
> may happen eventually, but it'll be more like 500 years from now, or 1,000
> or 10,000. (Not this century.)"

Karnofsky states the objection to his own claim in the objector's own terms,
including the specific numbers a skeptic would offer, before he answers it.
Giving the objection real content, rather than a vague gesture at doubt, is
what lets the reply that follows mean something.

> "But part of the mindset I've developed through GiveWell and Open
> Philanthropy is being open to strange possibilities, while critically
> examining them with as much rigor as possible. And after a lot of
> investment in examining the above thesis, I think it's likely enough that
> the world urgently needs more attention on it."

Karnofsky states his confidence level plainly, "likely enough," rather than
either asserting the claim outright or hedging it into meaninglessness. He
also credits a specific process — years spent critically examining the
thesis — as the reason for that confidence, instead of asking the reader to
take it on faith.

> "When confronting the 'most important century' hypothesis, my attitude
> doesn't match the familiar ones of 'excitement and motion' or 'fear and
> avoidance.' Instead, I feel an odd mix of intensity, urgency, confusion and
> hesitance. I'm looking at something bigger than I ever expected to
> confront, feeling underqualified and ignorant about what to do next."

Karnofsky names the two easy postures toward a big claim, excitement and
fear, and states plainly that his own reaction is neither. He is visible in
the admission that he feels "underqualified and ignorant," a specific,
unflattering description of his own state rather than a general claim of
humility.
