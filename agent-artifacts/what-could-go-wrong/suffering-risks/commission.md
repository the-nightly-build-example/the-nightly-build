# Commission: what-could-go-wrong/suffering-risks

## The argument

The risk of astronomical suffering, "s-risk": the claim that the worst outcomes
from advanced AI are not human extinction but futures that contain suffering on a
scale dwarfing all of it so far, and that this class of outcome is neglected
relative to how bad it would be. The lesson teaches this argument on its merits.
It works from the original documents that made it, never the commentary. The
researcher confirms who made each move in the argument and every cited figure.

## Why this argument, now

The reader has met AI-risk talk that runs entirely on extinction. This is the
serious argument that extinction is not the floor, and it is made by a real,
small research community rather than by cranks. The moral-status question it
depends on is live in the labs right now. The reader should be able to state the
argument at full strength, see exactly how much of it rests on systems that do
not exist, and decide for themselves how much weight it carries. The desk's job
is to teach how the field reasons about a risk this speculative without joining
it or writing it off.

## The declared reader and the fit

The reader is smart, widely read, and new to this specific argument. Build up
every term the first time: what "astronomical" means here (a claim about scale
tied to how much computation a mature technology could run), what a moral patient
is, and the specific pathways the argument names. No hype and no doom: present
the case as its most careful defender would, then hold it to the evidence. A
grand word appears only after the argument earns it.

## What the lesson teaches

Follow the desk's structure for an argument.

1. Open with the argument at full strength. Name who first made it and what they
   had seen that worried them. Lay out the reasoning a careful defender would
   give: why a mature AI-enabled future could contain vastly more morally-relevant
   experience than history has, and why some fraction of it containing suffering
   is not obviously unlikely. Distinguish the pathways the literature names (for
   example suffering that arises from conflict between agents, suffering created
   incidentally by an indifferent optimizer, and suffering created deliberately),
   using the sources' own terms.
2. Draw the sharp line. Test the argument against what real systems actually do.
   Be honest that almost nothing here has been shown in a working system: the
   case is analogy and reasoning about systems that do not yet exist. Name the few
   present-day footholds for what they are and no more (the open question of
   whether any current system is a moral patient; multi-agent training as a thin
   analogy for conflict; the way other technologies already produce suffering at
   scale as the analogy the argument leans on).
3. Bring it to the present. Say who makes the argument today and what they want
   done, then check it against the most recent evidence, including the live
   debate over AI moral status in the labs. Name the gap between confidence and
   proof in both directions: the argument's own confidence about scale, and the
   dismissal that never engages it.

The original work: separating the argument's logical structure from its empirical
support, so the reader can see which of its steps are reasoning and which are
claims about the world that could in principle be checked.

## Boundaries against neighbors in the library

Two nearby lessons are already published and must not be re-argued: whether an AI
can be a moral patient at all, and the claim that we cannot specify what we value
so an optimizer loses it. This lesson uses the first as an input and links it in
Background; it is about the scale and disvalue of suffering specifically, not
about moral status in general or about value misspecification. Confirm the exact
slugs before linking. Keep the boundary visible in the prose.

## Sources plan

Template and series floor: eight sources, at least four primary and one
secondary. Primary: the essays and the peer-reviewed papers that made and
developed the argument, read for who makes which claim, plus the recent report or
lab statement documenting the present-day moral-status debate. Secondary
reporting is context only. Every figure or characterization carries its owner;
an accusation or a strong empirical claim needs the primary that owns it. Search
for the strongest objections to the argument and record them in full for the
skeptic read.

## Recent shapes to break

What Could Go Wrong has lately closed the body with a "who makes the case now"
or "who holds each side now" section before the takeaway. The present-day turn
is required content for this desk, but its heading and construction have started
to repeat; name this lesson's present-day section in its own terms and vary the
build. Avoid the two-clause comma-and heading mold flagged in the standard.
