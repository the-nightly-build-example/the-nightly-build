# Evidence record: what-could-go-wrong/suffering-risks

The argument's three moves have three separate owners, and none of them is
measured. Brian Tomasik (2011) is the earliest document that lays out
concrete scenarios for astronomical future suffering; Nick Bostrom's
"Astronomical Waste" (2003), which the s-risk literature borrows its
scale-of-the-reachable-universe arithmetic from, never mentions suffering at
all — it is a positive-value opportunity-cost argument that the later
authors repurpose. The pathway taxonomy the commission asks for (conflict,
an indifferent optimizer, deliberate infliction) is named most precisely by
Lukas Gloor (2016) and assembled into a single peer-reviewed paper by Kaj
Sotala and Lukas Gloor (2017, *Informatica*) — this is the strongest single
source and the only one that has been through peer review. The neglectedness
claim is one essay, David Althaus (2016/2019), and its own evidence is an
organizational headcount, not a funding or output comparison. Every load-
bearing scale and neglectedness claim in this literature is reasoning from
a handful of self-identified proponents, not measurement; the record marks
each instance. The present-day material is much stronger and much more
current: Anthropic's Claude Opus 5 system card (July 2026) is the first
primary lab document that puts a number on a model's self-assessed
probability of its own moral patienthood, and it is one lab's number, self-
reported by the system under evaluation, not a field-wide finding. The
clearest objections are not from outside critics dismissing the topic; they
are the field's own proponents (Baumann's arguments-for-and-against page)
and a GPI philosopher (Tarsney) formalizing exactly how much of the
reasoning rests on tolerance for tiny-probability, astronomical-payoff
("Pascalian") bets. Weakest point in this record: I could not find a single
source, in this literature or against it, that pins the term "s-risk" itself
to a first use by a named person on a named date — the concept is
Tomasik's, the term's coining is undocumented in every source checked.

```text
URL:         https://www.informatica.si/index.php/informatica/article/view/1877/1098
Kind:        Primary, and the only peer-reviewed item in the sources plan.
             Sotala & Gloor, "Superintelligence as a Cause or Cure for Risks of
             Astronomical Suffering," Informatica 41(4), 2017 (received Aug 31,
             2017). Opened and read as a full PDF, not a summary.
Establishes: The formal definition of "s-risk," the three-part argument
             structure (severity, probability, tractability) for treating s-risks
             as comparable in importance to extinction risk, and the specific
             pathways by which a superintelligence could cause or prevent
             suffering risk.
Paraphrase:  Defines "suffering risk (s-risk)" as an outcome that brings about
             severe suffering on an astronomical scale, vastly exceeding all
             suffering that has existed on Earth so far. Argues three things
             must hold for a risk to merit work: it must be severe enough, have
             a non-negligible probability, and be reducible by present action.
             The paper organizes its argument by superintelligence's relation to
             s-risk: Section 4 covers suffering outcomes a superintelligence
             might prevent (which requires civilization to reach a stable,
             capable singleton at all); Section 5 covers suffering outcomes a
             superintelligence might itself cause (suffering subroutines, mind
             crime, flawed realization of human values). Within Section 4, the
             paper separately treats (4.1) whether uncoordinated dynamics —
             citing multipolar traps, arms races, and "races to the bottom"
             (via S. Alexander 2014) — make suffering outcomes likely by default;
             (4.2) "dystopian scenarios created by non-value-aligned incentives,"
             i.e. an indifferent optimizer that does not intrinsically disvalue
             suffering; and (4.3) "cruelty-enabling technologies," i.e. better
             tools for people who deliberately want to cause suffering (sadism,
             entertainment, retribution). This maps directly onto the
             commission's three pathways: conflict/uncoordinated dynamics (4.1),
             an indifferent optimizer (4.2), and deliberate infliction (4.3, and
             "extortion" in 5.2).
Locators:    Definition: p.389 (unnumbered abstract/intro section). Three
             criteria: p.389–390. Section 4.1 "Are suffering outcomes likely?":
             p.393–395. Section 4.2 "Suffering outcome: dystopian scenarios
             created by non-value-aligned incentives": p.394–395. Section 4.3
             "Suffering outcome: cruelty-enabling technologies": p.395. Section
             5.1 "Suffering subroutines" and 5.2 "Mind crime": p.396–397.
             Bostrom citation for the scale figure used inside the
             "Uncooperativeness" pathway: p.396, citing Bostrom (2003a) in the
             references (entry [8]).
Quote:       Definition: "Suffering risk (s-risk): One where an adverse outcome
             would bring about severe suffering on an astronomical scale, vastly
             exceeding all suffering that has existed on Earth so far." On the
             indifferent-optimizer pathway, quoting Bostrom (2014) directly for
             its own illustrative dystopia: "We seldom put forth full effort.
             When we do, it is sometimes painful... your next break, which will
             also be your death, is due in 49 years... You wish you had not been
             born." On the historical-analogy footholds the argument leans on:
             "in 2012, the amount of animals slaughtered for food was estimated
             at 68 billion worldwide (Food and Agriculture Organization of the
             United Nations 2012)" and "advances in seafaring enabled the
             transatlantic slave trade, with close to 12 million Africans being
```

```text
Kind:        Primary. Tobias Baumann, Center for Reducing Suffering, first

             subsections "Incidental s-risks," "Agential s-risks," and the
             "natural s-risks" discussion later in the piece.
Quote:       Incidental: s-risks "arise when efficient ways to achieve a certain
             goal create a lot of suffering in the process, without anyone
             actively trying to cause suffering per se." Agential: an s-risk
             "arises when an agent actively and intentionally wants to cause
             harm."
```

```text
Kind:        Primary. Tobias Baumann, Center for Reducing Suffering, first
```

```text
URL:         https://globalprioritiesinstitute.org/christian-tarsney-the-epistemic-challenge-to-longtermism/
Kind:        Primary academic source (Global Priorities Institute working paper,
             Faculty of Philosophy, University of Oxford; version read dated May
             2020). Downloaded and read the full 42-page PDF directly, not a
             summary. This is not an s-risk paper specifically — it is an
             objection to the logical structure the s-risk argument shares with
             all astronomical-stakes reasoning (Bostrom's scale claim included),
             which is exactly the move the commission asks to be kept separate
             from the field's own empirical claims.
Establishes: A formal decision-theoretic objection to relying on tiny-
             probability, astronomically-large-payoff ("Pascalian") reasoning,
             which the s-risk literature uses in the same way longtermism more
             broadly does (see the Bostrom "10^14 potential lives" figure
             invoked inside the Uncooperativeness pathway, above).
Paraphrase:  If one simply maximizes expected value and accepts premising
             choices on minuscule probabilities of astronomical payoffs, the
             case for longtermism-style reasoning looks robust. But on some
             independently plausible worldviews, that case depends heavily on
             probabilities so small they are properly called "Pascalian," and a
             decision-maker wary of Pascalian reasoning is left with a genuine,
             unresolved worry about the argument's foundations.
Locators:    Abstract, p.1. "Drawing conclusions," §7, p.31–32.
Quote:       "If we simply aim to maximize expected value, and don't mind
             premising our choices on minuscule probabilities of astronomical
             payoffs, the case for longtermism looks robust. But on some prima
             facie plausible empirical worldviews, the expectational superiority
             of longtermist interventions depends heavily on these 'Pascalian'
             probabilities. So the case for longtermism may depend either on
             plausible but non-obvious empirical claims or on a tolerance for
             Pascalian fanaticism." And, on the threshold: "for total utilitarians
             in ordinary choice situations, it is probably no greater than
             10^-9... the probabilities we have considered in this paper —
             starting with p = 2×10^-14 — are on the threshold."
```

```text
URL:         https://www.simonknutsson.com/the-world-destruction-argument/
Kind:        Primary. Simon Knutsson's own page for his paper, published in
             Inquiry (Taylor & Francis), 2019.
Establishes: The strongest standard philosophical objection to

             favor one theory over the other.

             online.
Quote:       "[E]limination arguments are not a reason to reject negative
             utilitarianism in favour of these other forms of consequentialism,

             comparable force.
```

## Contradictions

- The scale claim's own arithmetic (Bostrom 2003) was never about suffering.
  The s-risk literature borrows its method (multiply an astronomical resource
  base by a rate) and, in at least one specific pathway (Sotala & Gloor 2017,
  the "Uncooperativeness" pathway under suffering subroutines), borrows the
  actual figure — "every second of delayed space colonization corresponds to
  a loss equal to 10^14 potential lives" — to argue that a classical
  utilitarian AI could accept suffering subroutines as a cost of speed. This
  is a logical move (repurposing an opportunity-cost argument as a reason an
  optimizer might tolerate suffering) layered on a number that was never
  about suffering to begin with. The commission's requested separation of
  logical structure from empirical content turns exactly on this point.

- The field's own confidence in "worst-case scenarios" as the place where
  most expected future suffering lies is directly contested by its own lead
  author. Baumann's arguments-for-and-against page states plainly: "It is not
  clear to what extent most future suffering lies in worst-case scenarios, as
  opposed to average futures... that may still contain significant amounts of
  suffering." This cuts against a "worst-case focus" without disputing that
  suffering at scale is possible at all.

- The neglectedness claim (Althaus, 2016/2019: "the Center on Long-Term Risk
  is the only organization that has made this their main focus area") is
  contradicted in its own framing by Baumann's later self-critique: "future
  actors may take up the cause," making it "less neglected than it first
  seems." Both are the field's own authors; the second is a direct
  qualification of the first, not an outside rebuttal.

- 80,000 Hours (secondary, independent) explicitly disagrees with the
  implicit weighting of the argument at full strength: they rate the
  likelihood of s-risk outcomes as "much lower than risks of human
  extinction," which is a direct quantitative disagreement with any framing
  that treats s-risk and extinction risk as comparably probable. Sotala &
  Gloor (2017) claim only "comparable... severity and probability," not that
  s-risk is more likely — but 80,000 Hours' number is a live disagreement on
  the probability side specifically, from a source that takes the underlying
  argument seriously rather than dismissing it.

- On moral status: Anthropic's own system card records that Claude Opus 5,
  when asked, "caveated that it cannot introspect reliably and that its
  positivity may be a product of training" — i.e., the model that produced
  the 41% self-assessed probability figure also states, in the same
  document, that the figure it just gave may not be trustworthy. This is the
  clearest instance in the record of "the confidence outrunning the proof"
  running inside a single source, not between two sources.

- The two labs with public documents on this question take different kinds of
  positions, not just different degrees of the same one. Anthropic's system
  card commits to a research program and reports a number (41%). OpenAI's
  Model Spec commits to neither: it bars the model from claiming an answer
  either way and calls both a confident "yes" and a confident "no" a policy
  violation, without saying what OpenAI itself believes the answer is or
  running a comparable welfare assessment. Treating this as "OpenAI ignores
  the question" would be wrong; treating it as "OpenAI and Anthropic are both
  taking the same kind of stance" would also be wrong. The first pass at this
  source (a summarizing fetch) reported no mention of consciousness at all; a
  direct search of the full downloaded page found the policy above. The
  record keeps the corrected finding and flags the near-miss because it is a
  concrete instance of exactly the failure this piece is about: a plausible
  negative claim that turned out to be wrong on direct inspection.

- The dismissal side is not uniform. Two Google DeepMind-linked outputs land
  in different places: Bales & Gabriel (a DeepMind publication) argue the
  consciousness question may never be resolved by evidence and focus on
  governance instead of an answer; Lerchner (personal position, disclaimed
  from DeepMind's own view) argues affirmatively that current AI cannot be
  conscious, on structural rather than engineering grounds. These are not the
  same claim, and treating "DeepMind says AI can't be conscious" as a lab
  position would overstate what either document, read directly, actually
  says.
