# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/autonomous-weapons (01)

## How this piece should sound

This lesson opens by stating the autonomous-weapons argument at full strength,
the way Kaplan opens "The Heated Debate Over America's Nuke Policy" by quoting
Biden's own 2017 speech rather than paraphrasing the "sole purpose" position,
and the way Piper opens "The case for taking AI seriously as a threat to
humanity" with Hawking's and Musk's own words before doing anything else with
them. Name who first raised the alarm about weapons that select and engage
targets without meaningful human control, and give their reasoning in a form
their own defenders would recognize, before the piece does anything to test it.

Once the claim is grounded, Piper's move after the Hawking-Musk opening is the
one to reach for: "That might have people asking: Wait, what? But these grand
worries are rooted in research." A strong claim gets a sentence that shows it
rests on something, not a sentence that either amplifies it or waves it off.
This lesson's version of that move is pointing to what a working autonomous
system actually does today, since that is the ground this argument has to
stand on or fall from.

When the piece turns to testing the claim against real deployed systems,
Kaplan's handling of Robert Gates' objection is the model: he names Gates, and
gives his three points as points, not as "critics worry" or "some argue."
Wherever this lesson stages the case that current weapon autonomy does not yet
resemble the killer-robot scenario, or the case that it is closer than the
comfortable line suggests, put a name and their actual reasoning behind it. The
series prompt already asks for this; Kaplan's paragraph is what it looks like
worked out at the sentence level.

Kaplan and Schneier both write about people dying — nuclear war, terrorism —
without the prose ever raising its voice. "Ike was horrified by the bomb's
destructiveness, but he also thought that any conflict with the USSR or Red
China would escalate to a nuclear conflict" states a grim calculation in the
same plain register as a sentence about budget line items, because for Kaplan
it is one. This lesson's subject is a weapon system selecting and engaging a
target without a person approving the strike, and that is exactly the kind of
fact that invites a sentence built to sound alarmed rather than a sentence
that states what the system does and lets that carry the weight. Reach for
the second kind.

The line this lesson has to hold — between narrow autonomy already flying,
already targeting, already in service, and the fully autonomous killing that
remains speculative — is a shown-versus-speculated line, and Piper's "It's
tempting to conclude that there's a pitched battle between AI-risk skeptics and
AI-risk believers. In reality, they might not disagree as profoundly as you
would think" is a model for correcting a reader's assumption about where a
debate actually sits, stated once, plainly, without a hedge-word doing the
work instead of a sentence. Kaplan's "It is likely that Biden will ultimately
be dissuaded as well" and "My guess is" show the other half of that
discipline: when the piece reaches its own judgment about where the argument
stands today, or how far current systems are from what the argument warns
against, mark it as a judgment in the sentence that makes it, the same plain
way Kaplan marks his.

This is a lesson, not a column: the declared reader is smart and widely read
but has never opened a targeting system's spec sheet, so a term like
"meaningful human control" or "select and engage" earns a plain-language
definition the sentence it first appears in, the way the template requires,
before the piece leans on it. And because the series names no company or state
as an authority, the sourcing move throughout should look like Kaplan's naming
of Gates or Piper's naming of Bostrom, Etzioni, and Russell: a person with a
position, cited and named, never an institution standing in for one.

## Bruce Schneier, "Beyond Security Theater"

Source: https://www.schneier.com/essays/archives/2009/11/beyond_security_thea.html

> "Terrorism is rare, far rarer than many people think. It's rare because very
> few people want to commit acts of terrorism, and executing a terrorist plot
> is much harder than television makes it appear. The best defences against
> terrorism are largely invisible: investigation, intelligence, and emergency
> response."

The opening states the central fact and immediately gives the reason behind it,
in two short sentences, before the piece has earned any drama. Schneier is
visible in the choice to open a piece about terrorism with a claim that
deflates the reader's fear rather than one that confirms it.

> "To be sure, reasonable arguments can be made that some terrorist targets are
> more attractive than others: aeroplanes because a small bomb can result in
> the death of everyone aboard, monuments because of their national
> significance, national events because of television coverage, and
> transportation because of the numbers of people who commute daily. But there
> are literally millions of potential targets in any large country (there are
> five million commercial buildings alone in the US), and hundreds of potential
> terrorist tactics."

He grants the opposing case its full reasoning first, with its own examples,
before he tests it against a number. The five million buildings is doing the
actual work of the paragraph; the concession before it exists so that number
lands as a rebuttal to something real, not a strawman.

> "Despite fearful rhetoric to the contrary, terrorism is not a transcendent
> threat. A terrorist attack cannot possibly destroy a country's way of life;
> it's only our reaction to that attack that can do that kind of damage."

A flat claim about a subject usually written up with more heat, stated in the
same register as the rest of the piece. Schneier is visible in refusing to let
the sentence get bigger than the claim it's making, even at the point in the
essay where a writer chasing a strong ending most wants to.

## Kelsey Piper, "The case for taking AI seriously as a threat to humanity"

Source: https://www.vox.com/future-perfect/2018/12/21/18126576/ai-artificial-intelligence-machine-learning-safety-alignment

> "Stephen Hawking has said, 'The development of full artificial intelligence
> could spell the end of the human race.' Elon Musk claims that AI is
> humanity's 'biggest existential threat.' That might have people asking: Wait,
> what? But these grand worries are rooted in research."

The piece opens with the strongest version of the claim, in the words of the
people who made it, before Piper has added anything of her own. The one line
of hers that follows does not argue with Hawking or Musk; it tells the reader
where to look for whether they're right.

> "Bostrom's book Superintelligence was compelling to many people, but there
> were skeptics. 'No, experts don't think superintelligent AI is a threat to
> humanity,' argued an op-ed by Oren Etzioni, a professor of computer science
> at the University of Washington and CEO of the Allan Institute for
> Artificial Intelligence. 'Yes, we are worried about the existential risk of
> artificial intelligence,' replied a dueling op-ed by Stuart Russell, an AI
> pioneer and UC Berkeley professor, and Allan DaFoe, a senior research fellow
> at Oxford and director of the Governance of AI program there."

Piper stages the disagreement as a named exchange between two actual op-eds
rather than summarizing "some experts disagree." Every person in the sentence
has a title attached, which is what lets the next paragraph's claim about how
the sides actually relate to each other stand on something.

> "It's tempting to conclude that there's a pitched battle between AI-risk
> skeptics and AI-risk believers. In reality, they might not disagree as
> profoundly as you would think."

Two short sentences correct a reader's assumption about the shape of a debate,
stated plainly and without qualification. Piper is visible in trusting the
flat statement to do the correcting, rather than building toward it with a
longer runway.

## Fred Kaplan, "The Heated Debate Over America's Nuke Policy"

Source: https://slate.com/news-and-politics/2021/11/nuclear-weapons-policy-no-first-use-complicated.html

> "To most people, the news may seem puzzling: Isn't this U.S. policy
> already—if an enemy nukes us, we'll nuke the enemy? In fact, it is not U.S.
> policy. Our war plans and our relations with key allies have long been
> premised on the pledge, and the rehearsed possibility, that we will use
> nuclear weapons first—not in a surprise attack but in response to
> conventional aggression against an ally or to a large-scale chemical,
> biological, or cyber attack that can't be halted without resorting to
> nukes."

Kaplan opens by naming the assumption a general reader is likely to hold and
correcting it in the same breath, with the actual scope of the policy laid out
in one sentence. He's visible in trusting a plain factual correction to be
interesting enough to open on, with no scene-setting ahead of it.

> "In the early phases of the atomic age, in the 1940s and '50s, our policy was
> to fire off nuclear weapons in response to any Soviet or Chinese aggression
> against the U.S. or its allies. President Dwight Eisenhower called this
> policy 'massive retaliation.' Ike was horrified by the bomb's destructiveness,
> but he also thought that any conflict with the USSR or Red China would
> escalate to a nuclear conflict and so, therefore, the only wise policy was to
> deter them from starting a conflict by threatening them with obliteration if
> they did so."

A paragraph about a president willing to threaten obliteration, written at the
same even pace as the rest of the piece. Kaplan is visible in the small human
detail — Eisenhower's own horror at the weapon he built policy around — sitting
next to the cold logic without either one being played up against the other.

> "However, during those internal debates, Defense Secretary Robert Gates
> raised three points. First, a shift in policy would frighten and alienate our
> allies, making them doubt our broad security commitments, and possibly compel
> them to build their own nuclear arsenals, which could spark new regional
> nuclear arms races. Second, the U.S. might someday face a large-scale
> biological attack; we stopped building bioweapons long ago, and so a nuclear
> response might be appropriate."

The counterargument arrives as one named official's numbered reasoning, not as
an unattributed pushback. Kaplan is visible in slowing down to give the second
point its own sentence rather than compressing both into a clause, so each
reason can be judged on its own.
