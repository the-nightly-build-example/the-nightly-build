# Commission: what-could-go-wrong/autonomous-weapons

## The argument
Lethal autonomous weapons systems (LAWS) — weapons that select and engage targets
without meaningful human control over the individual kill decision — are argued to
be a distinct danger: they could lower the threshold for going to war, open an
accountability gap (no human decides each strike, so who is responsible?), be
unable to reliably satisfy the laws of war (distinction between combatant and
civilian, proportionality), and enable cheap, scalable, deniable killing,
including by non-state actors. The strongest statements come from the 2015 open
letter on autonomous weapons (Future of Life Institute, signed by AI researchers),
Stuart Russell's advocacy (the "Slaughterbots" scenario), the International
Committee of the Red Cross, and the Campaign to Stop Killer Robots.

## Why this argument, now
The reader keeps meeting both "killer robots are coming" and "it's all science
fiction." What Could Go Wrong teaches the argument on its merits and then draws
the line the case turns on: between what has already been shown in a working
system and what is still analogy about systems that do not exist yet. Autonomous
weapons are unusually good for this because narrow autonomy in weapons is already
deployed, so the reader can see exactly where the real edge is and where the
science fiction begins — and because the international debate is live and stalled.

## The angle (full strength, then test, then present)
1. Open with the argument at full strength, steelmanned: name who makes it and
   what they saw, and give the reasoning as its most careful defender would — the
   accountability gap, the compliance-with-IHL problem, the lowered war threshold,
   and the scalable-anonymous-killing worry. Define "meaningful human control" in
   plain words. The reader should understand why serious people are alarmed before
   a word against it.
2. Test it against what real systems do, drawing the line:
   - What is already shown/deployed: defensive systems that engage without a
     per-shot human decision (e.g., close-in weapon systems, some air defenses),
     and loitering munitions with autonomous target-selection modes (e.g., the
     Harpy; the STM Kargu-2, which a 2021 UN Panel of Experts report on Libya
     described as possibly having engaged targets without an operator). Be exact
     about how autonomous each really is and how constrained the task.
   - What is still analogy/guesswork: cheap swarms performing selective mass
     assassination, and fully autonomous strategic systems deciding whom to kill
     at scale with no human in the loop. No such system has been fielded; that is
     the gap.
3. Bring it to the present: the UN CCW Group of Governmental Experts process and
   why it has stalled, the split between states seeking a binding treaty and those
   opposing one, and recent calls (e.g., from the UN Secretary-General) for
   regulation. Check confidence against evidence in both directions — where alarm
   outruns what is deployed, and where "just sci-fi" dismissal ignores that
   narrow autonomy already blurs the line.

## Boundaries and continuity
Work from the original documents (the 2015 open letter, the UN Libya report, ICRC
and CCW GGE documents, Human Rights Watch reports), never commentary. Name no
company or state as an authority; report positions. Differentiate from published
neighbors and link rather than overlap:
- what-could-go-wrong/cyber-uplift and bioweapon-uplift: those are about AI
  helping humans BUILD or find weapons (capability uplift). This is about autonomy
  in the WEAPON'S kill decision. Make the distinction explicit.
- what-could-go-wrong/racing-dynamics and concentration-of-power: link if a
  point genuinely depends on them; the spine here is the LAWS argument itself.
Leave the reader to decide how worried to be. No verdict block in the body; the
takeaway lands the judgment.
