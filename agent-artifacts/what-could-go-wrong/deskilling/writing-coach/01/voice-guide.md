# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/deskilling (01)

## Ed Yong, "Immunology Is Where Intuition Goes to Die"

Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/health/archive/2020/08/covid-19-immunity-is-the-pandemics-central-mystery/614956/

> "There's a joke about immunology, which Jessica Metcalf of Princeton recently told me. An immunologist and a cardiologist are kidnapped. The kidnappers threaten to shoot one of them, but promise to spare whoever has made the greater contribution to humanity. The cardiologist says, "Well, I've identified drugs that have saved the lives of millions of people." Impressed, the kidnappers turn to the immunologist. "What have you done?" they ask. The immunologist says, "The thing is, the immune system is very complicated …" And the cardiologist says, "Just shoot me now.""

The piece opens with a joke instead of a thesis sentence, and the joke does explanatory work: its punchline states the piece's central problem before the piece has argued for it. The scene is specific — a named source, a kidnapping, two professions in contrast — so it reads as an anecdote rather than a device for easing the reader in.

> "Amid all the fighting in your airways, messenger cells grab small fragments of virus and carry these to the lymph nodes, where highly specialized white blood cells—T-cells—are waiting. The T-cells are selective and preprogrammed defenders. Each is built a little differently, and comes ready-made to attack just a few of the zillion pathogens that could possibly exist. For any new virus, you probably have a T-cell somewhere that could theoretically fight it. Your body just has to find and mobilize that cell. Picture the lymph nodes as bars full of grizzled T-cell mercenaries, each of which has just one type of target they're prepared to fight. The messenger cell bursts in with a grainy photo, showing it to each mercenary in turn, asking: Is this your guy? When a match is found, the relevant merc arms up and clones itself into an entire battalion, which marches off to the airways."

Every part of the mechanism gets a role in the same small scene: the messenger cell, the T-cell, the search for a match. The writer doesn't name the technical term and move on — the picture comes first, and "T-cell" earns its place only once the reader has somewhere to put it.

> "But Farber cautions that having these cross-reactive T-cells "tells you absolutely nothing about protection." It's intuitive to think they would be protective, but immunology is where intuition goes to die. The T-cells might do nothing. There's an outside chance that they could predispose people to more severe disease. We can't know for sure without recruiting lots of volunteers, checking their T-cell levels, and following them over a long period of time to see who gets infected—and how badly."

This says exactly how far the evidence goes and stops there. The uncertainty isn't hedged in general terms — it's spelled out as a specific missing experiment (recruit volunteers, measure T-cells, follow them, see who gets infected), which is what makes the admission read as honest rather than as throat-clearing.

## Matthew Yglesias, "The case against crisis-mongering"

Source: https://www.slowboring.com/p/fake-crisis

> "What I think is going on here is that both American political coalitions have one foot planted firmly in the liberal camp and then various tendrils extending into various non-liberal ideologies. The readiness with which each camp accuses the other of having completely abandoned liberalism is in a way simply a sign of how firm liberalism's grasp over the American political tradition is. We don't have robust traditions of Christian Democracy or Social Democracy in the United States, so every deviation from a narrow debate of market liberals versus social liberals plays as a defection to Hitler or Mao. But this basic tension is not new; today's progressives are fighting Trumpian fascism in exactly the sense that the progressives of 15 years ago were fighting Bushian fascism. I think if you want to say we are perpetually teetering on the brink of toppling into right-wing authoritarianism but thanks to our heroic efforts we keep avoiding that outcome, that's fine. But I think a more enlightening interpretation of events would be to say that we have been living through strong directional progress toward more diversity and more cosmopolitanism but that when you push things forward, you end up with some overreach (both substantive and political) and then some blowback."

He states the view he's arguing against in language its holders would recognize as fair — each side genuinely experiences the other as the leading edge of something dangerous — and then concedes it outright ("that's fine") before offering a different reading. The concession is load-bearing: it's the moment the piece stops litigating who's right and starts asking which account explains more.

> "Since 1990, emissions in the U.S./Europe/Canada have fallen even as our economies and population have grown. If emissions had not skyrocketed during that period in China, India, and other Asian nations, we would easily be on track to contain warming at a very modest level."

The claim rests on a specific, checkable comparison — a start date, a set of regions, a counterfactual — rather than a general statement that emissions are a problem. A reader could look up the number and see whether it holds.

> "But during the GWOT Era, the worst thing that happened was the conceptual overreach itself. Instead of kicking the tires on everything to ask what cost-effective changes we could make to reduce the odds of a terrorist attack, we did everything from operating a secret global network of torture prisons to waging an obscure, years-long war in Somalia. Nobody knows why most people are taking their shoes off at airports or why, if that's important, you can just buy your way out of it getting TSA Precheck."

The mundane, checkable detail — shoes off, paying for Precheck — carries the claim that institutional overreach outlives its own justification, which a phrase like "excessive security theater" would only assert. It's also a joke, and the joke depends on the reader already knowing the airport ritual, which is why it isn't filler.

## Kelsey Piper, "Losing control of AI is actually the plan"

Source: https://www.theargumentmag.com/p/losing-control-of-ai-is-actually

> "This isn't some pessimistic projection of what might go wrong — it's actually the plan. Not the plan for the distant future; it's the plan for next spring."

The sentence draws a line by naming which side of it the claim sits on: not analogy, not a worry about the future, but a stated intention with a season attached. The second sentence narrows the date further, which is what keeps "the plan" from reading as a rhetorical flourish.

> "OpenAI reports that, over the past six months, the share of its research compute devoted to internal coding inference increased 100-fold (much faster than they've increased compute spending for customers). Tom Brown, an Anthropic co-founder, has said that "the vast majority of the work Anthropic does is internal software engineering.""

The figure is attributed to the company's own report, and the second sentence is a named person's own words, not the writer's paraphrase of what the company believes. Both sentences let the reader check the claim at its source rather than take the writer's summary of it on faith.

> "This is the context behind the AI crisis projections you may have heard of, such as AI 2027 and If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies: It's not that we will build GPT-7 next year, and GPT-8 the year after that, and GPT-9 the year after that, with each generation corresponding to noticeable but nonshocking productivity increases. If that happened, we'd have plenty of time to adapt, change course, make laws as needed, and gradually sift out which concerns are real and which haven't manifested."

Instead of asserting that the pace of change matters, she describes the slower counterfactual in enough concrete detail — three named generations, "noticeable but nonshocking" — that the reader can see exactly what the actual claim is being measured against, and why that difference is the thing worth arguing about.

## How this piece should sound

This is a lesson: its job is to leave the reader able to run Bainbridge's reasoning themselves. The steelman section can take the Yglesias move whole: state the case in language its own defenders would sign off on, and where a concession is genuinely owed, make it outright, the way he concedes the "teetering on authoritarianism" framing before offering a better-explaining account. A concession that arrives disguised as a transition ("of course, but...") reads as throat-clearing; one that's stated plainly reads as the writer actually having weighed it.

The shown-versus-forecast line this round is asking for doesn't have to live in a section heading — it can live in a single sentence the way Piper's does, naming outright which side of the line a specific claim sits on ("it's actually the plan," not "it might happen"). Wherever the piece turns from a measured finding (the aviation studies, the accident reports, a controlled trial of tool dependence) to something argued about present-day AI that hasn't been measured yet, that same kind of sentence can do the turning, rather than a transition that just changes subject and trusts the reader to notice the change in kind.

Bainbridge's argument itself — automating the easy part of a job strips the practice that made a person good at the hard part — is exactly the kind of mechanism Yong's T-cell passage is a model for: assign each part of it a role in one small, concrete scene (the pilot who used to hand-fly, the part of the job automation took, the part it left behind) before the term "ironies of automation" has to carry any weight on its own. The lesson template needs this piece to define that term in plain words at first use; a worked scene like Yong's is what makes a definition stick in a reader's head rather than just pass their eyes.

Where a figure shows up — how many pilots, what fraction of a diagnostic accuracy gap, how large a sample — Yglesias's 1990 emissions comparison shows one way to make a number mean something: pair it with a before-and-after or a side-by-side a reader can hold in their head. And where the evidence runs out before the argument does, Yong's Farber passage shows how to say so: name the specific study or measurement that doesn't exist yet. Both moves matter more here than usual, since the commission's own test for this piece is whether a reader can tell a measured finding from a forecast on the first read.

None of this asks the piece to sound clever about it. A plain, specific, even wry aside — Yglesias's shoes and TSA Precheck — is available where the material has one to offer; it isn't something a serious lesson has to avoid to stay serious. A line announcing its own carefulness, like "it's important to be clear-eyed here," names an intention. The sentence still has to do the work itself, the way Yong's or Piper's do.
