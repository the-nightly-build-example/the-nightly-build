# Evidence record: what-could-go-wrong/liars-dividend (01)

The evidence supports the argument at full strength and supports the desk's
shown/unshown line, with one sharp complication the writer must not smooth over.
The origin is firmly documented: Chesney and Citron coined "the liar's dividend"
in the California Law Review in 2019, and their own sentences carry the mechanism
and the paradox that the dividend grows as the public learns more about deep
fakes. Aviv Ovadya's earlier "infocalypse" warning is recoverable in his own
bylined writing (Harvard Business Review 2018, MIT Technology Review 2019) and,
for the specific coinage and its scenarios, through his direct quotes in the
BuzzFeed report that named it. The "flood of fakes swung an election" claim is
where the fear outran the proof: the two strongest 2024 studies I could read
(Kapoor and Narayanan's analysis of 78 election deepfakes, and, by summary only,
the Alan Turing Institute's CETaS work) find no measurable effect on any result
and find cheap fakes far more common than AI ones. The liar's dividend, by
contrast, is showing up in real disputes, including a court record: Tesla's
lawyers argued in a wrongful-death suit that genuine recorded statements by Elon
Musk might be deep fakes, and a judge rejected the move. The record is thin, and
in fact cuts against the strongest form of the worry, on one point: the only
controlled experiment on whether the liar's dividend actually works (Schiff,
Schiff, and Bueno, APSR) finds it pays off against text scandals but is largely
ineffective against video evidence, which is exactly where deep fakes are
feared. Nothing I found overturns the commission's angle, but the writer must
report that experimental gap. Ovadya's 2018 Washington Post op-ed, his Medium
essays, the live WIRED tracker page, and the CETaS report pages were all gated
(HTTP 403 or host-blocked) and could not be opened; see Discarded.

## Contradictions

- The core empirical split the commission expects holds. On the "flood of fakes"
  side, Kapoor and Narayanan find deepfakes were neither common nor decisive in
  2024 and that cheap fakes dominated; this cuts against the strong "synthetic
  media swings elections" fear. On the "liar's dividend" side, the Tesla court
  record and the Trump-Harris episode show the move is already being made in
  real disputes. These are two different worries and the evidence rates them
  differently, exactly as the desk brief predicts.

- The sharpest contradiction is internal to the liar's dividend itself. Chesney
  and Citron predict the dividend is strongest against genuine audio and video.
  Schiff, Schiff, and Bueno's experiment finds the opposite: crying
  "misinformation" helped politicians against TEXT scandals but was largely
  ineffective against VIDEO evidence. So the mechanism the theory fears most
  (dismissing real footage) is the one the only controlled test found weakest.
  The Tesla and Trump cases show the move being attempted, not that it works on
  audiences. The writer must keep "the move is being made" separate from "the
  move succeeds," and must not let the two court/political anecdotes stand in for
  audience effect that the experiment did not find for video.

- Kapoor and Narayanan are the serious skeptics of the whole alarm. Their claim
  is that misinformation is demand-driven, not a supply problem that cheap AI
  created, so "political misinformation is not an AI problem." Steelmanned: if
  audiences consume misinformation that flatters existing beliefs, making it
  cheaper to produce changes little. This is a direct challenge to the
  flood-of-fakes framing and belongs in the piece at full strength. Their own
  caveat cuts the other way: their dataset may undercount deepfakes.

- The Brennan Center's older examples (Dastis 2017, Fouts, Access Hollywood
  2017) show the "cry of fake" predates convincing deepfakes. This complicates
  Chesney and Citron's claim that deepfakes create the dividend; the honest
  reading is that deepfakes lower the cost and raise the plausibility of a move
  liars could already make, not that they invented it.

- The Trump-Harris case is a liar's-dividend move by a powerful figure against
  content that embarrassed him, but the "recording" was a still image of a rival's
  crowd, not evidence of his own wrongdoing. It fits the pattern (real content
  called AI to serve a narrative) but is not the Chesney-Citron paradigm of
  dodging accountability for one's own recorded acts. The Tesla case is the
  cleaner fit for that paradigm. Rank them accordingly.
