# Commission: what-could-go-wrong/liars-dividend

## Assignment

What Could Go Wrong teaches one argument, at full strength, then tests it against
what real systems do, then brings it to the present. Tonight's argument is that
cheap, convincing synthetic media corrodes the shared basis for trusting any
recording, and that the deeper harm is not the fake that fools people but the
"liar's dividend": once anyone can claim a real recording is fake, the powerful
can dismiss genuine evidence against them. Open with the argument as its most
careful defender makes it, name who first made it and what they had seen, then
test it against the record of what deepfakes have and have not done, then bring
it to the present.

The reader should leave able to state the argument precisely, distinguish the
"flood of fakes" worry from the distinct "liar's dividend" worry, and say what
the best evidence shows about each so far.

## The argument at full strength

The canonical statement is Chesney and Citron, "Deep Fakes: A Looming Challenge
for Privacy, Democracy, and National Security" (California Law Review, 2019),
which named the liar's dividend. Aviv Ovadya's earlier warnings about an
"infocalypse" are part of the origin. The researcher must recover what these
documents actually argued and the mechanism they proposed, so the argument is
presented at its real strength before any test.

## What the article must add: the shown/unshown line

Test the argument against the record. On the "flood of fakes" side: the 2024
global election year was the widely predicted stress test; recover what actually
happened from trackers and studies (documented deepfake incidents, and research
finding that measurable persuasive or electoral impact was limited and that
cheaper manipulations often mattered more). On the "liar's dividend" side:
recover concrete cases where a real recording was publicly dismissed as
AI-generated, including any where a court or a campaign made that move. Draw the
line the desk requires: the liar's dividend is showing up in real disputes, while
the strong "synthetic media swung an election" claim is so far weaker than the
fear. Where confidence outruns proof in either direction, name the gap.

## Boundaries

- The subject is the epistemic argument, not persuasion effect sizes (that is a
  separate published lesson, ai-persuasion; link it, do not re-run its trial) and
  not any single incident. The deepfake fraud and robocall incidents are their
  own published lessons in another desk; link if the reader needs one, do not
  retell them.
- Keep reported fact, study finding, and speculation about future capability in
  separate boxes. Name no company as an authority; work from the original
  documents and the tests behind headline numbers.
- Leave the reader to decide how worried to be.

## Sources to start from

Primary: Chesney and Citron 2019 (the law-review article); Ovadya's own writing
on the infocalypse; at least one empirical study or authoritative tracker of
deepfakes in the 2024 elections; and a documented liar's-dividend case (a real
recording publicly called a fake by the party it implicated, ideally with a court
or official record). At least one secondary source for how the argument is
invoked in current policy. Series policy requires at least eight sources, at
least four primary. Record contradictory evidence and the serious skeptics in
full; the empirical record is where this argument is contested.
