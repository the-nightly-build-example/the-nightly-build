# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/liars-dividend (01)

## How this piece should sound

This lesson explains one contested argument about AI risk, that cheap and
convincing synthetic media corrodes the shared basis for trusting any recording,
and the liar's dividend that follows once anyone can wave a real recording away
as a fake. The reader is smart and widely read and new to this particular
subject. The register is plain and concrete all the way through, in the manner
of the three writers below: claims stated flatly, stakes shown through specific
cases, no reaching for effect. Where a sentence could either sound impressive or
be understood, it should be understood.

State the argument at full strength before a word is raised against it, and state
it in language its own most careful defenders would accept. Cegłowski does this
when he lays out the premises of Bostrom's argument one plain sentence at a time,
and Luu does it when he states the common Microsoft narrative fully and even notes
how widely it is held. Cegłowski's "small box of thinking meat" shows that stating
another side's claim clearly does not require dressing it up, and Luu shows that
naming how many people hold a view can be part of putting it fairly. The reader
should be able to state the liar's-dividend argument, and the separate
flood-of-fakes worry, in the form the people who make them hold them.

When the lesson turns from the argument to what real systems have actually done,
the test works best on a concrete case rather than on the claim restated in bigger
words. This is Cegłowski's Hawking's-cat move and Alexander's weakly-effective
drug: a specific, checkable thing set in front of the abstraction. A named instance
of a recording dismissed as AI-generated, or of a predicted wave that did or did
not arrive, carries more than a general sentence about trust.

Where the record cuts both ways, the two readings belong side by side at their
real strength before either is weighed, the way Alexander sets five hundred
economists against six hundred. The conclusion then reaches only as far as the
evidence carries it, and it is hedged to that size the way Alexander hedges after
the funnel plot, with the thin places named as thin. The line this desk draws
between what a working system has already shown and what is still analogy about
systems that do not yet exist is where this restraint matters most.

The reader has to leave able to hold the flood-of-fakes worry apart from the
liar's-dividend worry, and to hold reported fact apart from study finding apart
from speculation about future capability. Alexander's care in separating questions
that look like one question, a drug that helps one form of an illness and not
another, shows how lumping distinct claims together produces a picture that is
false in both directions. Keeping the two worries named and separate, and keeping
the shown apart from the merely argued, is the main work of a lesson like this.

When confidence outruns proof, the honest move is to say so, whether the
confidence is alarm or dismissal. Luu's closing account of a bet he made against
Microsoft and lost, and Alexander's plain statements of where the evidence stops,
both show a writer reporting the limits of his own certainty rather than writing
past them.

## Maciej Cegłowski, "Superintelligence: The Idea That Eats Smart People"

Source: https://idlewords.com/talks/superintelligence.htm

> "We each carry on our shoulders a small box of thinking meat. I'm using mine to give this talk, you're using yours to listen. Sometimes, when the conditions are right, these minds are capable of rational thought."

This is the first premise of an argument Cegłowski means to dispute, and he states
it in the plainest possible terms, in words a defender of the argument would sign.
The image is physical and specific, and it carries a real claim under it, that
thinking is something ordinary matter already does. The reader meets the premise
squarely before meeting any objection to it.

> "The inside view requires you to engage with these arguments on their merits. You ask your visitors how they learned about the UFO, why they think it's coming to get us—all the normal questions a skeptic would ask in this situation."

Cegłowski explains a way of judging a strange argument by walking through an
ordinary scene, someone at your door and the questions you would put to them. The
abstract idea, weigh the argument on its merits, arrives already fixed to a concrete
situation, so the reader never has to hold it in the air. "All the normal questions
a skeptic would ask" keeps the sentence unhurried and unshowy.

> "Stephen Hawking is one of the most brilliant people alive, but say he wants to get his cat into the cat carrier. How's he going to do it?
>
> He can model the cat's behavior in his mind and figure out ways to persuade it. He knows a lot about feline behavior. But ultimately, if the cat doesn't want to get in the carrier, there's nothing Hawking can do about it despite his overpowering advantage in intelligence."

Cegłowski takes a large claim, that greater intelligence converts into getting
what you want, and tests it against a small case anyone can picture. He does not
argue the claim in the abstract; he puts a specific person and a specific cat in
front of it, and the example itself is the objection. The plain question "How's he
going to do it?" opens the whole example without any lead-in.

## Scott Alexander, "Beware The Man Of One Study"

Source: https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/12/12/beware-the-man-of-one-study/

> "For example, take medical research. Suppose a certain drug is weakly effective against a certain disease. After a few years, a bunch of different research groups have gotten their hands on it and done all sorts of different studies. In the best case scenario the average study will find the true result – that it's weakly effective."

Alexander builds an abstract statistical point, that any real question comes with
a spread of studies around the truth, out of one concrete case: a weakly effective
drug and the studies that accumulate around it. He hands the reader the specific
thing first and lets the general principle form from it. The sentences are short
and each one carries a single step.

> "Well, five hundred economists signed a letter to policy makers saying that the science of economics shows increasing the minimum wage would be a bad idea. That sounds like a promising consensus…
>
> ..except that six hundred economists signed a letter to policy makers saying that the science of economics shows increasing the minimum wage would be a good idea."

Alexander sets the evidence on both sides at equal strength and in matched wording,
five hundred against six hundred, so the reader sees the symmetry in the parallel
phrasing rather than being told it exists. He does not tilt the sentence toward
either answer; he reports each side plainly and lets the pairing make the point.
This is how he keeps from joining a camp while still carrying the argument forward.

> "The bell skews more to left than to the right, which means more studies have found negative effects of the minimum wage than positive effects of the minimum wage. But since the bell curve is asymmetrical, we intepret that as probably publication bias. So all in all, I think there's at least some evidence that the liberals are right on this one."

Alexander reasons out loud toward a conclusion and stops exactly where the evidence
stops, at "some evidence" and "probably publication bias." The verdict is scaled
to what the data will support, and the reader can follow each step that produced
it. The stray typo and the flat "all in all" keep it sounding like a person working
through a problem rather than handing down a result.

## Dan Luu, "Steve Ballmer was an underrated CEO"

Source: https://danluu.com/ballmer/

> "There's a common narrative that Microsoft was moribund under Steve Ballmer and then later saved by the miraculous leadership of Satya Nadella. This is the dominant narrative in every online discussion about the topic I've seen and it's a commonly expressed belief "in real life" as well."

Luu opens by stating the view he is about to argue against, in full and without
loading it, and he notes how widely it is held. A reader who believes the common
story recognizes it here, intact, before any rebuttal starts. The prose is flat
and direct, which keeps the fair statement from reading as a trap being set.

> "This is like pointing to a list of failed companies a VC has funded as evidence the VC doesn't know what they're doing. But that's silly in a hits based industry like venture capital. If you want to claim the VC is bad, you need to point out poor total return or a lack of big successes, which would imply poor total return."

Luu resets what would count as evidence by comparing the charge against Ballmer to
judging a venture capitalist, where a list of failures proves nothing because a few
hits pay for many misses. He swaps a vague accusation for a concrete standard the
reader can apply, and says plainly what a real criticism would have to show. The
reasoning is laid out one short sentence at a time.

> "That was wrong — Microsoft beat Google to $1T and is now worth $1T more than Google."

Luu reports a bet he made against Microsoft and lost, in plain words with the
figures attached. Putting his own wrong prediction on the record shows the reader
where his judgment failed, which is part of why the rest of his confidence reads
as credible. The admission is short and stated without any hedging.
