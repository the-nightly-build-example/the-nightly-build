# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/cot-monitorability (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a lesson on chain-of-thought monitorability for a reader who is sharp and
widely read but has never worked inside a model. That reader will not take a claim
on the strength of who made it, so the piece has to earn each step in plain words
rather than assert it, and explain what the field treats as obvious. Holden
Karnofsky's register is the closest of the three exemplars to what that asks:
level, unhurried, willing to put a serious concern in an ordinary sentence. The
paper's own touchstone, Matt Yglesias explaining something he knows cold, points
the same way.

The article takes an argument seriously and then tests it, so its hardest
sentences are the ones that say how much a given piece of evidence actually shows.
Karnofsky's COVID passage is worth studying for that: he states the worry and, in
the next sentence, says how far the evidence backs it and no further, and the
qualifier never softens into reassurance. When the lesson reaches the results it
can stand on and the futures it can only reason about, that is the calibration to
hold, the demonstrated results given their full weight and the projections kept
labeled as projections, in sentences a reader can weigh one at a time. The claims
that will need this most run in both directions: the confident ones that say
monitorability is already lost, and the confident ones that say it needs no
watching.

Dan Luu opens on Ballmer the way this piece can open on its argument: the
strongest version of the case, stated fairly, before a word against it, and with
his own counter-claim drawn no wider than the evidence. Where the lesson weighs
what a given result proves, his venture-fund move is the one to keep in view,
naming out loud the standard of proof a claim has to meet instead of arguing
around it. And when a strong claim has to be cut down to its real size, his "true,
but not much of a criticism" is the pattern to reach for: grant what is true, then
say plainly what is left of it.

The other demand is teaching a distinction a smart reader will not have met, and
the finest of them here is what researchers mean when they ask whether a
chain-of-thought is faithful. Evans' hidden-systems passage shows the instinct
that fits: name the parts of the system that stay out of view, since that is
usually where the confusion sits. Her line on clarity sets the bar, that the clear
version holds the same information as the hard one and only arranges it better, so
nothing the reader needs gets dropped to make the page easier.

## Holden Karnofsky, "AI Timelines: Where the Arguments, and the "Experts," Stand"

Source: https://www.cold-takes.com/where-ai-forecasting-stands-today/

> "I worry that the arrival of transformative AI could play out as a kind of slow-motion, higher-stakes version of the COVID-19 pandemic. The case for expecting something big to happen is there, if you look at the best information and analyses available today. But the situation is broadly unfamiliar; it doesn't fit into patterns that our institutions regularly handle. And every extra year of action is valuable."

Karnofsky states a real worry, then in the very next sentence says how far the
evidence actually backs it: the case is there if you look at the best analyses
available, and he stops exactly at that. He keeps the worry and the limit on it in
separate short sentences, so a reader can weigh each. The person shows in his
willingness to state the worry and qualify it in the same breath, without letting
the qualifier turn into reassurance.

> "I think the lack of a robust expert consensus - and concerns about self-delusion and insularity - provide good reason to dig hard on the "most important century" hypothesis, rather than accepting it immediately. To ask where there might be an undiscovered flaw, to look for some bias toward inflating our own importance, to research the most questionable-seeming parts of the argument, etc."

Here Karnofsky turns the same scrutiny on the view he holds, listing the specific
places it could be wrong: an undiscovered flaw, a bias toward inflating his own
importance, the weakest parts of the argument. The list is concrete rather than a
general show of humility, and each item names something a reader could go check.
His honesty is visible in that he does this to his own side of the question.

## Dan Luu, "Steve Ballmer was an underrated CEO"

Source: https://danluu.com/ballmer/

> "There's a common narrative that Microsoft was moribund under Steve Ballmer and then later saved by the miraculous leadership of Satya Nadella. This is the dominant narrative in every online discussion about the topic I've seen and it's a commonly expressed belief "in real life" as well. While I don't have anything negative to say about Nadella's leadership in this post, this narrative underrates Ballmer's role in Microsoft's success."

Luu states the view he is about to argue against in full, and fairly. He even
grants that Nadella's leadership was fine before he says where he thinks the
standard story goes wrong, so the reader meets the received opinion at its
strongest first, with nothing set up as a straw man. The care shows in how
narrowly he draws his own claim: the story underrates Ballmer, not that the whole
story is wrong.

> "When people point to a long list of failures like Bing, Zune, Windows Phone, and HoloLens as evidence that Ballmer was some kind of buffoon who was holding Microsoft back, this demonstrates a lack of understanding of the tech industry. This is like pointing to a list of failed companies a VC has funded as evidence the VC doesn't know what they're doing. But that's silly in a hits based industry like venture capital. If you want to claim the VC is bad, you need to point out poor total return or a lack of big successes, which would imply poor total return."

Luu takes the critics' evidence, the list of flops, and shows why it does not prove
what they use it to prove, by moving to a case the reader already understands: a
venture fund judged on its whole portfolio. Then he says plainly what evidence
would actually settle the question, poor total return or the absence of big wins.
The move is to name the standard of proof out loud rather than argue around it.

> "Of course it would be better if Ballmer was prescient and all of his bets succeeded, making Microsoft worth something like $10T instead of the lowly $3T market cap it has today, but the criticism of Ballmer that says that he had some failures and some $1T successes is a criticism that he wasn't the greatest CEO of all time by a gigantic margin. True, but not much of a criticism."

Luu grants the criticism in full and then says exactly how much it is worth: the
failures came alongside trillion-dollar successes, so the complaint reduces to "not
the greatest ever." The closing line gives the concession and the verdict in one
plain sentence. The judgment is visible in his willingness to agree with the
critic and still come out with a clear answer.

## Julia Evans, "Why is DNS still hard to learn?"

Source: https://jvns.ca/blog/2023/07/28/why-is-dns-still-hard-to-learn/

> "just teaching people what the hidden systems are makes a huge difference. For a long time I had no idea that my computer had many different DNS libraries that were used in different situations and I was confused about this for literally years. This is a big part of my approach."

Evans puts her whole method in the first sentence: the confusion clears once
someone names the parts of the system that stay out of sight. She backs it with
her own case, years of being stuck on something no one had told her existed. The
person shows in how plainly she admits to having been confused for so long, which
is what lets her write for someone in the same spot.

> "And it's not "dumbed down" or anything! It's the exact same information, just formatted in a more structured way. My biggest frustration with alternative DNS tools that they often remove information in the name of clarity. And though there's definitely a place for those tools, I want to see all the information! I just want it to be presented clearly."

Evans draws the line between making something clear and making it thinner: the
clearer version here holds the exact same information, only better arranged. She is
explicit that clarity earns its keep by ordering what is already there, not by
dropping the hard parts. Her voice comes through in the flat refusal to lose any
of the detail.
