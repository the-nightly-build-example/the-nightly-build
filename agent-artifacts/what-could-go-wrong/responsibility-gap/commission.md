# Commission: what-could-go-wrong/responsibility-gap

## The assignment
This desk teaches one argument about how AI could go wrong at full strength, then
tests it against what real systems do. Tonight's argument: the "responsibility
gap." When an autonomous, learning system causes harm, no human may be fairly
holdable for it — the machine is not a moral agent, and the humans in the loop
(designer, manufacturer, operator, user) each did nothing that ordinarily grounds
blame — so a gap opens where accountability used to sit.

Open with the argument as its careful defenders make it. Andreas Matthias, "The
responsibility gap: Ascribing responsibility for the actions of learning
automata" (*Ethics and Information Technology*, 2004), gave it its name and its
sharpest form: control and knowledge are the classical conditions for
responsibility, learning systems remove both from the operator, so nobody
qualifies. Robert Sparrow, "Killer Robots" (*Journal of Applied Philosophy*,
2007), pressed the same point for autonomous weapons: it would be wrong to hold
the programmer, the commander, or the machine responsible for a war crime it
commits, yet someone must be. Lay out why serious people find this compelling
before a word against it.

Then draw the sharp line the series demands: what has actually been shown in
deployed systems versus what is analogy about systems that do not exist. In real
cases, courts and regulators have NOT found an empty gap — they assigned
responsibility through existing doctrines. Test the argument against the record:
the 2018 Uber ATG self-driving fatality in Tempe (the safety driver, Rafaela
Vasquez, was charged; Uber was not criminally charged), Tesla Autopilot crash
litigation and NHTSA findings, product-liability and negligence law, and the EU's
approach (the withdrawn AI Liability Directive; the revised Product Liability
Directive covering software/AI). Show where the "gap" is a real doctrinal problem
(diffuse causation, opacity, the "many hands" problem) and where it is dissolved
by assigning strict or vicarious liability regardless of a blameworthy mind.

Bring it to the present: who makes the argument now (legal scholars, AI-ethics
researchers) and what they want (strict liability, mandatory insurance, logging,
a "liability by design" regime), and check it against the most recent evidence.
Name the gap between confidence and proof in both directions: those who say
accountability is impossible, and those who wave the problem away.

## The one-sentence contribution this article owes
Separate the responsibility gap as a genuine *design* problem (opacity + many
hands make fault hard to locate) from the stronger claim that no one *can* be held
responsible — and show, from real cases and liability law, that the stronger claim
has not held up where autonomous systems have actually caused harm.
