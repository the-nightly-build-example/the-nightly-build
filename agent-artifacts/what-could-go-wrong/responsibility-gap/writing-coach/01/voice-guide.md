# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/responsibility-gap

## How this piece should sound

This lesson teaches the responsibility gap: the claim that when an autonomous,
learning system causes harm, no human is fairly holdable, because control and
knowledge, the conditions blame usually needs, have left the operator's hands.
The reader is smart and widely read and has spent no time in a codebase, and
they keep meeting claims about AI they cannot check. Write for that reader, in
plain explanatory prose, as someone who understands both the philosophy and the
liability law and wants to be understood. Explain the hard parts in ordinary
sentences. Here is what the passages below ask of this particular lesson.

State the gap argument the way Matthias and Sparrow state it, at its full
strength, before a word against it. Chiang follows Elon Musk's strawberry-picking
scenario the whole way to human extinction before he begins to doubt it, and
Alexander credits Bem's meta-analysis as careful and better than average before
he disputes it. The reader should understand why a serious person concludes that
a learning system takes the control and the knowledge that responsibility rests
on away from the operator, and should reach the word "gap" believing it.

Define the terms the argument runs on the first time they appear, in plain words,
and carry each to a case before moving on. Control, knowledge, moral agency,
strict liability, vicarious liability, the many-hands problem: each is a term a
smart reader may not hold, and Chiang shows the move, defining "insight" in one
sentence and handing the reader a person who is aware of their own illness.

Draw the line the series turns on between what a deployed system has been shown to
do and what is analogy about systems that do not yet exist. Alexander grants that
Bem "definitely picked up a signal" and then asks the one question that decides
what the signal is; Chiang grants AlphaGo Zero and then says plainly what it still
cannot do. Where the Tempe crash or the Autopilot litigation settled who answered
for the harm, report what the record shows, and keep it apart from the claim that
no one could have been held.

State a view you are about to test in the words of someone who holds it, then give
the verdict without hedging. Kerr quotes Justice Brennan's property-to-privacy
account in full and only then calls it malarkey, with the history that backs the
call. Sparrow's claim that it would be wrong to hold the programmer, wrong to hold
the commander, and wrong to hold the machine deserves that treatment: his case
first, unhedged, before the cases and the doctrine test it.

Mark the common ground before the disagreement, so the reader can tell the part
everyone grants from the part in dispute. Kerr says what all sides agree the
Fourth Amendment must do, then names the single question that is actually hard.
The design problem the argument points to, that opacity and many hands make fault
hard to locate, is not the same as the claim that responsibility is therefore
impossible, and the reader should be able to hold the two separately.

Reach for the plain word and stay unimpressed by the material. Kerr writes
"property-ish" and "privacy-ish" and drops in "I think" where the grander register
was there for the taking. Liability doctrine and moral philosophy both invite an
inflated vocabulary; where the plain word is exact, prefer "the maker" or "the
operator" to "the responsible party" and "blame" to "moral culpability."

When you concede a difficulty, concede the real one and stop. Kerr calls finding
the modern equivalent of an old physical search an analogy problem and leaves it
undecided rather than dressing it up as solved. Where the doctrine genuinely does
not reach a case yet, say so; where it reached one without any finding of a
blameworthy mind, say that too.

## Ted Chiang, "Silicon Valley Is Turning Into Its Own Worst Fear"

Source: https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/tedchiang/the-real-danger-to-civilization-isnt-ai-its-runaway

> "It seems harmless enough, but as the AI redesigns itself to be more effective, it might decide that the best way to maximize its output would be to destroy civilization and convert the entire surface of the Earth into strawberry fields. Thus, in its pursuit of a seemingly innocuous goal, an AI could bring about the extinction of humanity purely as an unintended side effect."

Checked: https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/tedchiang/the-real-danger-to-civilization-isnt-ai-its-runaway, retrieved 2026-09-22

Chiang lays out someone else's alarming scenario at full strength and follows its
internal logic all the way to the extinction conclusion before he starts to push
back. The flat "It seems harmless enough" is Chiang setting the low starting point
himself so the escalation lands as the argument's own, not a straw version he
built to knock down.

> "In psychology, the term “insight” is used to describe a recognition of one’s own condition, such as when a person with mental illness is aware of their illness. More broadly, it describes the ability to recognize patterns in one’s own behavior."

Checked: https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/tedchiang/the-real-danger-to-civilization-isnt-ai-its-runaway, retrieved 2026-09-22

He defines the term he needs, "insight," in one plain sentence and gives an
instance in the same breath, a person aware of their own illness, so the abstract
word arrives already attached to something the reader can picture. The definition
appears where the word is first used and is not assumed earlier.

> "There have been some impressive advances in AI recently, like AlphaGo Zero, which became the world’s best Go player in a matter of days purely by playing against itself. But this doesn’t make me worry about the possibility of a superintelligent AI “waking up.” (For one thing, the techniques underlying AlphaGo Zero aren’t useful for tasks in the physical world; we are still a long way from a robot that can walk into your kitchen and cook you some scrambled eggs.)"

Checked: https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/tedchiang/the-real-danger-to-civilization-isnt-ai-its-runaway, retrieved 2026-09-22

Chiang grants the impressive result plainly and then separates what the system did
from what people fear it means, naming the smallest ordinary task it still cannot
do. The scrambled-eggs robot is Chiang choosing a concrete, checkable limit
instead of a general reassurance.

## Scott Alexander, "The Control Group Is Out Of Control"

Source: https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/04/28/the-control-group-is-out-of-control/

> "That is, in let’s say a drug testing experiment, you give some people the drug and they recover. That doesn’t tell you much until you give some other people a placebo drug you know doesn’t work – but which they themselves believe in – and see how many of them recover. That number tells you how many people will recover whether the drug works or not."

Checked: https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/04/28/the-control-group-is-out-of-control/, retrieved 2026-09-22

Alexander teaches the idea of a control by walking one concrete case step by step,
give the drug, give the placebo, compare, and only then states the general point
the case was carrying. The offhand "in let's say a drug testing experiment" is a
person explaining across a table, not a textbook laying down a definition.

> "This is far better than the average meta-analysis. Bem has always been pretty careful and this is no exception. Yet its conclusion is that psychic powers exist."

Checked: https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/04/28/the-control-group-is-out-of-control/, retrieved 2026-09-22

Before he disputes the study Alexander credits it, twice, as careful and better
than average, and states its conclusion flatly rather than mocking it. The credit
is real, which is what makes the disagreement that follows worth reading.

> "Bem definitely picked up a signal. The only question is whether it’s a signal of psi, or a signal of poor experimental technique."

Checked: https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/04/28/the-control-group-is-out-of-control/, retrieved 2026-09-22

Two short sentences do the whole separation: he grants that something real was
measured, then names the single question that decides what it was. Alexander
splits what the data shows from what it is taken to show without raising his
voice.

## Orin Kerr, "The Difficulty of the Search Question: More Thoughts on Chatrie"

Source: https://reason.com/volokh/2026/04/24/the-difficulty-of-the-search-question-more-thoughts-on-chatrie/

> "There is broad agreement that the Fourth Amendment needs to extend beyond actual physical intrusion: If it didn't, the role of the Fourth Amendment would diminish over time in a world of wiretapping, thermal imaging, and network-stored records. The hard question is, what's the test for how to make sure the Fourth Amendment maintains that role over time, preserving its protections as technology changes?"

Checked: https://reason.com/volokh/2026/04/24/the-difficulty-of-the-search-question-more-thoughts-on-chatrie/, retrieved 2026-09-22

Kerr states what everyone already agrees on and then names precisely where the
disagreement sits, so the reader knows exactly which question is the contested
one. He locates the hard part rather than gesturing at it, and the plain "The hard
question is" is a working lawyer telling you where to look.

> "As I see it, this was malarkey. Fourth Amendment law had long been based on a mix of appeals to property-ish notions and privacy-ish notions, going back to 18th Century English debates on general warrants, through 19th Century U.S. cases on the Fourth Amendment, and through the early 20th Century."

Checked: https://reason.com/volokh/2026/04/24/the-difficulty-of-the-search-question-more-thoughts-on-chatrie/, retrieved 2026-09-22

Having quoted Justice Brennan's view in full a paragraph earlier, Kerr gives his
verdict in one blunt word and immediately supplies the history that earns it, so
the judgment is not left hanging on his say-so. "Property-ish" and "privacy-ish"
are Kerr refusing an inflated register when a homely word is exact enough.

> "Identifying the modern technological equivalents can be a challenge, for all the reasons that identifying persuasive analogies in law can be a challenge. But that, I think, should be the goal."

Checked: https://reason.com/volokh/2026/04/24/the-difficulty-of-the-search-question-more-thoughts-on-chatrie/, retrieved 2026-09-22

Kerr names the real difficulty, that the whole task turns on finding a convincing
analogy, and leaves it there rather than pretending he has settled it. The "I
think" is Kerr marking the edge of what he is sure of instead of performing
certainty he does not have.
