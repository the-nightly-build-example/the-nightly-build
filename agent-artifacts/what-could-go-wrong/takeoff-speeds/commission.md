## The argument

Once AI can help build better AI, does capability jump fast and discontinuously,
leaving little time to react, or climb gradually and predictably? This is the
takeoff-speeds disagreement: hard or fast takeoff versus soft or slow takeoff. It
is a live crux for how much warning the world gets, and serious people hold both
positions.

## Angle

Open the fast-takeoff case at full strength: I. J. Good's 1965 intelligence
explosion, the Yudkowsky position in the 2008 Hanson-Yudkowsky debate, and
Bostrom's treatment of takeoff speed in Superintelligence, with the analogies its
defenders use, evolution and the AlphaGo jump. Then test it against what real
systems have done. The continuous case, argued most sharply by Paul Christiano in
"Takeoff speeds" (2018), is that progress has been incremental and roughly
forecastable, that scaling makes capability a smooth function of compute and data,
and that the economy would show large effects before any single system ran away.
Draw the sharp line the desk requires: what has been shown in working systems
(scaling has been continuous and predictable; AI is measurably better at aiding
AI work) versus what is still analogy or projection (a single system recursively
improving to superintelligence over days). Bring it to the present: who argues
fast and who argues slow now, what each wants done, and what the most recent
measured evidence says, naming the gap wherever confidence outruns proof, whether
the confidence is alarm or dismissal.

Name no company as an authority. Work from the original documents, not commentary
about them.
