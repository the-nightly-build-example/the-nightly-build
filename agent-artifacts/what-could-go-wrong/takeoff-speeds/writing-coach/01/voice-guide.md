# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/takeoff-speeds

## How this piece should sound

This lesson teaches an open argument: whether AI progress arrives fast and
discontinuous or slow and continuous. The reader is smart and has read widely,
but has never had to weigh this particular disagreement. The register that fits
is plain and unhurried, closer to a good explainer than to an opinion column.
When the fast-takeoff case rests on a probabilistic or technical claim, the
reader can only hold it if it is put in ordinary words with the uncertainty
stated in both directions at once, the way Hannah Ritchie turns a bare "more
likely than not" into a two-thirds chance it happens and a one-third chance it
does not. A figure the reader cannot place, or a confidence stated without its
other side, leaves them nothing to judge.

The desk asks for the fast-takeoff case at full strength before anything is
said against it. Dan Luu spends his opening laying out the exact narrative he is
about to argue against, in the terms its holders use, so that his disagreement
has a real position under it rather than a weak one he built to knock down. When
the strongest version of the discontinuous case is given the reasoning its most
careful defender would give it, the reader believes serious people hold it, and
the test that follows means something. Where the fast case turns on a pattern in
what past systems have done, saying what would actually count for it and what
would count against it keeps the argument off impression. Luu does this by
converting a list of failures into a checkable standard: whether a portfolio of
bets is bad, he writes, is a question of total return, not of how many
individual bets failed. The equivalent move here is to name what a run of real
systems would have to show before it settled the question either way.

The case usually turns on the line between what a working system has already
demonstrated and what is still projection about systems that do not exist yet.
That line holds only if it is drawn precisely. Ritchie separates a single hot
year from a long-term climate trend, and separates "risks increase with warming"
from "1.5°C is a threshold," without letting either half blur into the other.
The same care distinguishes a capability shown on the bench from an
extrapolation about where the curve goes next. Keep alarm and dismissal at the
same distance while you do it: Scott Alexander sets five hundred economists on
one side against six hundred on the other and lets the symmetry stand, and
Ritchie corrects an overstated alarm while holding on to the real concern
underneath it. A correction that slides into dismissal, or a caution that slides
into alarm, fails the same way.

Where a point against the lesson's reading is genuinely right, granting it and
then stating exactly how far it reaches costs the argument nothing. Luu concedes
his subject was not the greatest of all time and marks that as a thin
complaint. And where the evidence does not settle the question, the piece is
stronger for saying what it cannot show than for reaching past it, as Alexander
does when he admits he has no positive answer, only several negative ones. In
the body, that honesty is carried by the prose rather than by a narrator, since
the body speaks to no one; the two bookend cards are where the lesson may
address the reader directly. Throughout, an abstract claim about how progress
behaves lands when a concrete case carries it, the way Alexander builds his
whole point about noise from one imagined drug and its spread of studies.

## Dan Luu, "Steve Ballmer was an underrated CEO"

Source: https://danluu.com/ballmer/

> "There's a common narrative that Microsoft was moribund under Steve Ballmer and then later saved by the miraculous leadership of Satya Nadella. This is the dominant narrative in every online discussion about the topic I've seen and it's a commonly expressed belief "in real life" as well. While I don't have anything negative to say about Nadella's leadership in this post, this narrative underrates Ballmer's role in Microsoft's success."

Luu states the view he is about to contest before he contests it, and states it
as its holders would, not as a version thinned out to be easy. His own claim
arrives bounded: he underrates, he says, not that the opposing narrative is
worthless, and he sets aside Nadella rather than knocking him to raise Ballmer.
The reader can see the disagreement is with a real position.

> "When people point to a long list of failures like Bing, Zune, Windows Phone, and HoloLens as evidence that Ballmer was some kind of buffoon who was holding Microsoft back, this demonstrates a lack of understanding of the tech industry. This is like pointing to a list of failed companies a VC has funded as evidence the VC doesn't know what they're doing. But that's silly in a hits based industry like venture capital."

Here Luu changes what the evidence is allowed to prove. A list of failures feels
like a verdict until he supplies the standard it should be measured against,
total return in a business where a few large wins pay for many losses. The
analogy is doing analytic work, not decoration: it names the specific reason the
failure list does not carry the weight put on it.

> "Of course it would be better if Ballmer was prescient and all of his bets succeeded, making Microsoft worth something like $10T instead of the lowly $3T market cap it has today, but the criticism of Ballmer that says that he had some failures and some $1T successes is a criticism that he wasn't the greatest CEO of all time by a gigantic margin. True, but not much of a criticism."

Luu grants the criticism outright, then measures exactly how much it amounts to.
The concession is real, not a feint, and the two flat words that follow it do
the weighing. This is the habit of a writer who is willing to give the other
side its point and confident that stating the point's size is enough.

## Scott Alexander, "Beware The Man Of One Study"

Source: https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/12/12/beware-the-man-of-one-study/

> "For example, take medical research. Suppose a certain drug is weakly effective against a certain disease. After a few years, a bunch of different research groups have gotten their hands on it and done all sorts of different studies. In the best case scenario the average study will find the true result – that it's weakly effective."

Alexander teaches an abstract point, the spread of findings around a true value,
by building one concrete case and walking through it a step at a time. Nothing
is asserted before it is shown on the example. The plainness is deliberate: he
picks the simplest possible drug and disease so the reader is following the
reasoning, not the specifics.

> "Well, five hundred economists signed a letter to policy makers saying that the science of economics shows increasing the minimum wage would be a bad idea. That sounds like a promising consensus…"
>
> "..except that six hundred economists signed a letter to policy makers saying that the science of economics shows increasing the minimum wage would be a good idea."

He lets one side raise the reader's expectation, then sets the mirror-image fact
beside it and leaves the symmetry to make the point. Neither number is
editorialized. The restraint is the craft: Alexander trusts the matched pair to
land harder than any adjective he could attach, and it keeps both sides at an
equal distance.

> "I don't have a good positive answer. I do have several good negative answers."

After pages of argument, Alexander says plainly where his method runs out. He
does not manufacture a resolution to close on. Admitting the gap, and marking
what he can offer against what he cannot, is presented as the honest end of the
inquiry rather than a failure of it.

## Hannah Ritchie, "No, the world is not going to pass its 1.5°C warming target in the next few years"

Source: https://hannahritchie.substack.com/p/15c-warming-temporary

> "What this means is that between now and 2027, it's more likely than not that for one or two years the average temperature will go past 1.5°C. Now, this is not guaranteed: there's around a two-thirds chance it does, but also a one-third chance that it doesn't. So "more likely than not" is a reasonable summary."

Ritchie takes a phrase a reader would skim past and turns it into two numbers
they can actually hold, then states the chance it does not happen with the same
weight as the chance it does. She does not round the uncertainty away to make the
sentence cleaner. The reader is left able to judge the claim rather than trust
it.

> "When you refer to 1.5°C as a threshold, you tell people that something dramatic and substantial changes. People imagine it's a tipping point into oblivion. In reality, there is nothing particularly special about '1.5'. It's not that as soon as we move from 1.49°C to 1.5°C, we immediately set off a bunch of tipping points. Climate risks increase with warming (and increase more once we get into the world of 1.5°C, 2°C or more). But 1.5°C is not a threshold or a tipping point."

She corrects an overstatement and keeps the real concern in the same breath.
The precise pair of numbers, 1.49 against 1.50, does the dismantling, and the
line about risks rising with warming makes sure the correction is not read as
telling anyone to relax. Ritchie holds both truths at once instead of trading
one for the other.

> "The Paris Agreement was not about limiting the temperature increase in any given year to 1.5°C. That's 'weather', not climate. And this confusion (or deliberate miscommunication) of weather and climate is what we criticise deniers for (they use one cold snap to claim that global warming is a hoax)."

Ritchie draws a clean line between two things people run together, a single
year and a decades-long average, and then applies that line to both sides
evenhandedly, noting that the same confusion is what her own side objects to
when opponents use it. The standard does not bend to whoever is invoking it.
