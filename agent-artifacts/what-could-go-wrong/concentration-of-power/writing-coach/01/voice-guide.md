# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong / concentration-of-power (01)

## How this piece should sound

The argument this lesson tests — that AI could let a small group seize and
hold power — has a strongest version and a weakest version, and the reader
should be able to tell which one they're getting at every point. Mounk's
list of what a second Trump term would likely do is the model for that: four
separate claims, each carrying its own hedge word rather than one sweeping
verdict on "danger." The AI-enabled-power-concentration argument breaks the
same way — a loyal military AI, a captured surveillance apparatus, an
information advantage in electoral persuasion, a hollowed-out civil service
— and each mechanism can be stated at its own confidence level and checked
against what a real system has or hasn't done, rather than folded into one
up-or-down claim about whether "AI could enable authoritarianism."

Precision about the claim's own terms matters as much as precision about its
confidence. Mounk doesn't call Trump a fascist; he calls him an
authoritarian populist and defines the term in the same sentence, then
credits the real grievance under the label before separating it from the
part that actually threatens institutions. This argument needs the same
discipline applied to its own vocabulary — which small group, which
capability, which existing check it claims AI evades — instead of resting
on an undifferentiated "AI risk" or "power grab."

Let the strong version of the argument stand on its own before it gets
tested, the way Goldsmith lets the constitutional-crisis narrative stand
unhedged for a full paragraph before he complicates it. The series prompt
already asks for this: name who first made the argument and lay out their
reasoning the way its most careful defender would. What the Goldsmith and
Mounk passages below add is how the turn away from that version should be
made — the pivot's reason belongs in the same sentence as the pivot itself
("one can disagree... but the fact is"; "that's the good news... but it
would be a mistake"), not deferred to a section break doing the arguing by
implication.

Where the test can be grounded in a specific, named case rather than a
general claim, take it — Lee's move of settling an abstract capability
question with Waymo's actual timeline is the model. This article has real
material for that: documented instances (or their absence) of AI deployed
inside a government, existing institutional checks and whether they've
already been tested, any working system that resembles a piece of the
mechanism. Reach for the named case over the abstraction wherever the
sourcing supports it, and say plainly when no such case exists yet and the
argument is still analogy.

One calibration the exemplars can't supply, because all three are bylined
columns with a first-person voice free to hedge in the first person
("I am not arguing... though I agree with most of them"): this lesson's
body has no "I" to hedge from. The discipline Goldsmith performs by naming
what he isn't claiming has to be done in the third person here — stated as
what the evidence does and doesn't show, not as what the writer does and
doesn't believe. The two bookends are the one place in the lesson template
that does address the reader directly; the calm, unhedged-by-persona voice
these three passages model belongs to the body between them.

## Yascha Mounk, "The Danger to American Democracy"

Source: https://www.persuasion.community/p/the-danger-to-american-democracy

> "Trump's re-election would have enormous consequences in a wide variety of
> fields. He would likely weaken NATO and undermine Western support for
> Ukraine. He would likely offer tax breaks to billionaires and rich
> corporations. He would likely institute the largest program of mass
> deportations in American history. He may try to fire tens of thousands of
> civil servants and instruct the FBI to prosecute his adversaries."

Four claims, each with its own hedge rather than one blanket prediction. The
specificity — NATO, tax breaks, deportations, the FBI — makes every claim
checkable on its own, and the hedge words aren't a repeated tic: the surer
claims get "likely," the more speculative one gets "may try."

> "Trump is not a fascist but rather an authoritarian populist: somebody who
> assails the governing elite as corrupt or self-serving, and claims that he
> alone truly represents the people. It is populists' rejection of
> pluralism, not their lambasting of an elite that, in many cases, really is
> deeply flawed, which sets them on a collision course with democratic
> institutions."

Mounk needs a word for what Trump is, picks a more precise one over the more
sensational one available to him, and defines it in the same sentence so
the reader isn't left to guess what he means by it. The definition also
credits the complaint driving the movement — "an elite that, in many cases,
really is deeply flawed" — before drawing the line between that complaint
and the part that threatens institutions.

> "There are structural reasons to think that, compared to most other
> countries, America's institutions are rather resilient to autocratic
> takeover. That's the good news. But it would be a mistake to conclude
> that they are sure to hold up over the next four years just because they
> did so when Trump was first in office—especially since Trump is likely to
> attack them in a more capable and concerted manner the second time
> around."

This is the piece's hinge, where the steelmanned case for institutional
resilience turns into its complication. The reason for the turn is stated
in the same sentence as the turn itself — "especially since" — rather than
left for the next paragraph to explain.

## Jack Goldsmith, "The Powerful, Resilient, Independent Supreme Court"

Source: https://www.execfunctions.org/p/the-powerful-resilient-independent

> "From the beginning, Trump 2.0 engaged in an avalanche of controversial
> actions, many unlawful, all fueled by the elimination of internal lawyerly
> friction. Congress, controlled by the president's party, was silent or
> enabling, even when Trump was steamrolling its prerogatives. Some private
> institutions that might have been a counterweight to Trump—including law
> firms and media outlets—were on their heels due to Trump's weaponization
> of the presidency against them."

Goldsmith is restating the case against the institution he's about to
defend, and he restates it the way its strongest holders would have put it
— "many unlawful," "silent or enabling," "on their heels" — without a hedge
or a rebuttal folded in yet. The claim gets to stand on its own before he
starts testing it two sections later.

> "One can disagree with many of the Supreme Court decisions since Jan. 20,
> 2025. And one can disagree about which lines are important not to cross.
> But the fact is that the Court's strategy has preserved and possibly
> enhanced its authority vis-à-vis the president and has resulted in the
> successful enforcement of significant legal constraints on the presidency,
> all in the face of a nearly unprecedented set of challenges."

The claim under test — that the Court vindicated its authority — arrives
only after "one can disagree" is said twice about the very decisions the
claim rests on. Crediting the disagreement first is what keeps the sentence
that follows from reading as a verdict for one side.

> "I am not arguing in support of any particular Supreme Court decision,
> though I agree with most of them. I am not arguing that the Court has
> pushed back adequately, or inadequately, on the president. I am not
> denying that the Court might have (depending on one's view of the law)
> done more to push back against the president, though I believe that every
> serious pushback ran the risk of presidential defiance, especially early
> on."

Three sentences, each stating what the piece is not claiming before it
states what it is. This is a writer visibly refusing the stronger, easier
version of his own argument in print, where a reader can hold him to it
later.

## Timothy B. Lee, "Six principles for thinking about AI risk"

Source: https://www.understandingai.org/p/six-principles-for-thinking-about

> "The pair point to self-driving cars as an example. In the early years,
> these vehicles seemed to make rapid progress. By the late 2010s, many
> companies had built prototype vehicles with basic self-driving abilities.
> But the authors write that more recently, progress "has been far slower
> than experts originally anticipated because they underestimated the
> difficulty of collecting and learning from real-world interaction data."
> Self-driving cars have to deal with a long list of "edge cases" that must
> be discovered through trial and error on real public streets. It took
> more than a decade for Waymo to make enough progress to launch its first
> driverless taxi service in Phoenix. And even Waymo's cars still rely on
> occasional assistance from remote operators."

An abstract claim about why AI capability gains might slow down gets
settled by one case, named and dated: Waymo, Phoenix, "more than a
decade," and the caveat that even now its cars "rely on occasional
assistance from remote operators." The example carries the argument instead
of decorating it after the fact.

> "This isn't to say we shouldn't worry about possible harms from new AI.
> Most new technologies enable new harms and AI isn't an exception. For
> example, we've already started to see people commit fraud using deepfakes
> created using generative AI. AI systems have the potential to be very
> powerful—and hence to enable even more significant harms in the future.
> So maybe it would be wise to press pause? However, AI systems could also
> have tremendous benefits."

The worry is stated plainly and left to stand as a real question — "so
maybe it would be wise to press pause?" — rather than set up to be
dismissed in the same breath. The case for the other side only starts in
the sentence after.

> "And yet the world hasn't ended. Why is that? For the simple reason that
> the defenders have access to the same tools. Most critical software is
> extensively tested for vulnerabilities by developers and researchers
> before it is deployed. In fact, the development of bug-finding tools is
> primarily carried out not by hackers, but by a multibillion-dollar
> information security industry. On balance, the availability of AI for
> finding software flaws has improved security, not worsened it."

The reason a frightening-sounding claim doesn't settle the argument is given
as a mechanism, not an assurance: defenders have the same tools, the tools
are mostly built by a named industry, and that industry tests critical
software before it ships. Each clause narrows the claim instead of just
restating it more confidently.
