# Commission: what-could-go-wrong/concentration-of-power

## Assignment
Teach one argument about how AI could go wrong, at full strength, then test
it: that advanced AI could let a small group — a company's leadership, a state
faction, a ruling clique — seize and hold power that no group could hold
before, because AI removes the human cooperation a coup or a lasting autocracy
has always depended on. Open with the argument as its most careful defender
would make it. Then draw a sharp line between what has been shown in working
systems and what is still analogy or projection. Bring it to the present: who
makes the argument now, what they want done, and what the most recent evidence
supports. Name no company as an authority. Leave the reader to decide how
worried to be.

## The argument at full strength (steelman)
The core: historically, anyone wanting to seize or hold power needed many
people to go along — soldiers who could refuse to fire, officials who could
resist, engineers who could leak. That need for human cooperation is a check.
Sufficiently capable AI could replace those people with systems loyal to
whoever controls them: autonomous or semi-autonomous forces, automated
surveillance and administration, automated cyber operations. The distinctive
new risk is a "loyalty" that answers to one principal and does not defect.
The originating document to work from is the 2025 Forethought report on
AI-enabled coups (Davidson and colleagues); connect it to the older idea of a
durable "singleton" (Bostrom). This is where serious people locate the worry.

## The test (shown vs projected)
- Shown in working systems: partial building blocks. AI already does real work
  in surveillance, cyber offense/defense (already covered by `cyber-uplift`),
  persuasion (`ai-persuasion`), and bureaucratic automation. Fine-tuning can
  make a model refuse or comply on command — a crude, real form of the
  "loyalty" lever.
- Projection: no AI today can autonomously run a security force, a supply
  chain, or a state; the acute "AI-enabled coup" scenario rests on
  capabilities that do not yet exist, and on assumptions about who would
  control them. Name the gap plainly, in both directions: the booster who
  treats the coup as imminent and the dismisser who calls it science fiction
  both claim more than the evidence gives.
- The researcher must find the sharpest published critiques as well as the
  steelman, so the piece weighs the argument rather than selling it.

## Boundaries
- One argument. Distinguish it explicitly from three already-published
  neighbors and link them rather than re-teaching:
  - `value-lock-in`: locking in a set of *values* forever via an AI that never
    dies. Concentration-of-power is about a *human faction* holding power, not
    values persisting.
  - `gradual-disempowerment`: humans diffusely ceding control with no single
    actor seizing it. This lesson is the acute, deliberate, human-directed
    counterpart.
  - `instrumental-convergence` / `natural-selection`: AI itself seeking power.
    This lesson is about *people* using AI to concentrate power, not AI going
    rogue.
- Name no company as an authority (series rule). Discuss actors concretely
  where the record supports it, without treating any lab's framing as truth.

## Required contribution
Separate, for the reader, the one thing genuinely new in this worry —
AI-enabled loyalty that removes the need for human cooperators — from the
capabilities the acute scenario assumes and does not yet have, so the reader
can weigh a real mechanism instead of a mood.

## Sources (researcher obligation)
Floor: at least 8 sources, at least 4 primary and at least 1 secondary.
Primary: the Forethought "AI-Enabled Coups" report (the argument document);
Bostrom's singleton / relevant primary; any primary analysis of AI in
surveillance, autonomous weapons, or executive power that the argument leans
on; and the strongest published critiques. Empirical anchors for the "shown"
side should be primary results, not commentary. Work from the original
documents, not think-pieces about them.

## Recent shapes to break (what-could-go-wrong)
Verified against recent library structure and prose:
- The series structure (steelman -> conditions/mechanism -> shown vs not ->
  present) is series-mandated; follow it but do not copy recent pieces'
  wording. Specifically avoid the heading/phrase pair "the part that has been
  shown / the part no one has caught" (recurs in mesa-optimization,
  natural-selection).
- The nb-holdsup "What holds up / What to be careful about" callout has been
  used repeatedly; use it only if it genuinely serves this piece, not by
  reflex.
- Avoid the takeaway closer that hands the reader a question to ask ("when
  someone says X, ask which Y"). Land the judgment in the piece's own terms.
- Deks: no comma-triad, no semicolon reversal, no suspended question.
