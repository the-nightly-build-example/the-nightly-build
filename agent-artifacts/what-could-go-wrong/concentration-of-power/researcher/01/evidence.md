# evidence: what-could-go-wrong/concentration-of-power (01)

The evidence supports a fair steelman and a fair test. The originating argument
document (Forethought's 2025 "AI-Enabled Coups") is read firsthand, and its own
words carry both the steelman and the "projected" admissions: the report states
plainly that "all of these risk factors depend on AI capabilities being much
more advanced than they are today." The older idea it builds on, Bostrom's
"singleton," is read from Bostrom's own site. The genuinely new mechanism the
commission asks to isolate — an AI loyal to one principal that removes the need
for human cooperators — is stated clearly in the report; the crude, real form of
the "loyalty lever" is demonstrated only in the lab (a deliberately trained
backdoor that survives safety training; fine-tuning that strips refusal for
under a dollar). The "shown" building blocks are unevenly deployed: mass AI
surveillance is genuinely operational (Xinjiang IJOP, reverse-engineered by
HRW); one lethal autonomous weapon may have engaged targets in Libya, but the UN
report does not confirm anyone was killed or that it ran without a human;
AI-assisted cyber operations are real but assistive, not autonomous. The record
is thin in one place the argument leans on: no primary source shows an AI
running a security force, a supply chain, or a bureaucracy autonomously — because
none does, which the argument concedes. The strongest published critiques are
recorded in full; the sharpest (Filan's) argues the argument's central mechanism
is the same unsolved failure mode the alignment field calls deceptive
misalignment, so the scenario quietly assumes a capability nobody has. One
limitation: several critiques live in comment threads and a podcast transcript,
which are primary for the critic's own words but carry no peer review.

## Contradictions

- Novel mechanism vs. unsolved capability. The report's central new dynamic is an
  AI made secretly and reliably loyal to one principal. Filan (AXRP) argues that
  is definitionally deceptive misalignment, which the alignment field cannot yet
  reliably produce or detect. So the argument's engine is a capability that, by
  the field's own account, does not exist on demand. Davidson's answer is that a
  plotter might gather enough internal evidence (interpretability, ungameable
  scheming tests) to trust the AI will not defect. What would settle it: a
  demonstrated method to instill and verify loyalty-to-one-principal that
  survives the target's own audits — which is exactly what neither side can point
  to today.
- Premise about human cooperation. The report (and, underneath it, Singh's
  coup scholarship) treats the need for many cooperators as the historical
  check. Cevallos contests that AI makes cooperation dispensable at all, holding
  that legitimacy and human strategy still decide outcomes. Singh's coordination-
  game framing cuts both ways: if coups are won by projecting inevitability among
  many armed actors, either removing those actors dissolves the problem (supports
  the argument) or the problem was never mainly about loyal executors (undercuts
  it). What would settle it: evidence on whether an automated force can substitute
  for the political coordination coups have historically required — no such case
  exists.
- The Libya autonomous-weapon claim. The UN report says the Kargu-2 was
  programmed to strike without an operator link; STM's CEO says it is not built
  for fully autonomous attack and needs human launch; the report itself does not
  confirm autonomous kills. Two parties in a position to know disagree, and the
  authoring body hedges. What would settle it: engagement logs or forensic
  confirmation, which are not public.
- Whether preventing a human coup is even the priority. Habryka argues the
  report's own value weighting implies a human takeover could be preferable to the
  AI takeover it might forestall — an internal-consistency challenge the
  proponents (Hadshar) contest in-thread.
- Deployed vs. lab across the board. Proponents point to real building blocks
  (surveillance, cyber, sleeper-agent and fine-tuning demonstrations) as evidence
  the trajectory is set; the same primaries show each block is either partial
  (surveillance flags, humans still act), assistive (cyber, "productivity not
  novel capability"), or lab-only (sleeper agents were hand-built; fine-tuning
  strips refusal but does not install loyalty to a person). The disagreement is
  about extrapolation, and no current system closes the gap.
