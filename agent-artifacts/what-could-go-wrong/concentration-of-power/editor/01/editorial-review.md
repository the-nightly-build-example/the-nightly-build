# Editorial review: what-could-go-wrong/concentration-of-power (editor/01)

## Skeptic

Thesis: the AI-enabled-coup argument has one genuinely new part, an AI made
reliably and secretly loyal to a single principal so human cooperators become
unnecessary, and that part reduces to deceptive misalignment, a failure the
alignment field cannot produce or verify on demand. Everything else in the
argument is either an old tool in new form or a capability no working system
yet has. The headline states exactly this and the piece defends it.

The claims it stands on, and how each held:

1. Every coup has depended on many cooperators who could refuse, and that
   dependence is a check. Grounded in Singh's coordination-game account (s11,
   secondary) and stated as historical fact. Holds. Singh is cited only for the
   coordination framing; the 471-coups and ~300-hours figures were correctly
   left out per the evidence caution, and the article does not reintroduce them.

2. The report argues advanced AI removes that check by replacing the refusers
   with singularly loyal systems. Verified against the Forethought report
   firsthand (s1): the quoted passages ("naturally distributes power throughout
   society"; "removes this constraint, making it technologically feasible to
   replace human workers with AI systems that are singularly loyal to just one
   person") are present and used in context. The three named mechanisms
   (singularly loyal workforce, secret loyalties, exclusive access) match the
   source. Holds.

3. The hinge: secret loyalty reduces to deceptive misalignment, which the field
   cannot yet build or confirm. Verified against AXRP Ep. 46 (s8): Filan's line
   ("basically just the same thing (as far as I can tell) as deceptive
   misalignment") and Davidson's interpretability/scheming-test rebuttal are
   both on record and reported accurately, including that Davidson's answer is a
   bet on tools not yet built. The reduction is the article's own synthesis, and
   it holds against the sources rather than resting on one.

4. Shown vs projected. Each building block checks out against its primary:
   surveillance is real but ends in a human decision (HRW/IJOP, s3, ~13M Turkic
   Muslims, VPN/travel flags, human investigates and detains); cyber is real but
   assistive (Google GTIG, s4, "current LLMs on their own are unlikely to enable
   breakthrough capabilities"); the loyalty/compliance lever exists only in the
   lab (Sleeper Agents, s5, 2023/2024 code backdoor surviving safety training;
   Qi et al., s6, refusals stripped for under $0.20 on ~10 examples), and the
   article is careful that neither shows loyalty to a named person. Holds.

I pushed hardest on the Libya claim, the place the brief flagged. The article
reports it as the UN Panel stated it (s7, para. 63): retreating fighters
"hunted down and remotely engaged" by systems "programmed to attack targets
without requiring data connectivity between the operator and the munition,"
then states plainly that the panel does not say the weapon killed anyone or
acted without a human and that the manufacturer says a person verifies and
launches. It is not sold as "an autonomous weapon has killed." The contest is
kept in view. This is the correct handling and it survives a hostile read.

Even-handedness holds in both directions. "The line cuts both ways" charges the
booster (assumes a loyalty mechanism no one has demonstrated) and the dismisser
(ignores the cheap lab lever, live surveillance, and the authors' own uncertain
timeline), closing on "Each claims more than the evidence gives." The
who-makes-the-case section pulls critiques from three vantage points (Cevallos,
a democracy scholar; Singh, a political scientist; Habryka and Tracy, AI-safety
practitioners) and states honestly that the sharpest ones live in a comment
thread and a podcast rather than reviewed papers.

Display text, descriptor by descriptor: headline and dek make claims about the
world, not about the article's method or selection; the dek carries no
comma-triad, semicolon-reversal, or suspended-question mold. Every section
subhead is a step of the argument in the piece's own nouns, and none uses the
banned "shown / no one has caught" pair. Named people and affiliations check
out against the evidence record: Filan (alignment researcher, hosts AXRP);
Hadshar (report co-author, 80,000 Hours profile); Cevallos (CANVAS activist-
intelligence program, Journal of Democracy); Habryka and Tracy (Redwood
Research) on LessWrong. Attributing the co-authored report to "Tom Davidson's
2025 report" in the dek is lead-author shorthand, and the body names all three
authors in full, so it is accurate rather than misleading.

data-nb-kind audit: every label matches the evidence record's own classification
(s1-s8, s10, s12 primary; s9 and s11 secondary). The two secondaries are
correctly marked: the 80,000 Hours profile is a proponent synthesis by a report
co-author, and Singh is publisher/review access to a monograph the researcher
did not read whole. Google's GTIG report is used only as a record of observed
misuse on its own product, with its own limiting conclusion quoted, so no
company is treated as an authority on the coup thesis.

Citations: I opened all twelve hrefs as printed. Eleven land directly on the
cited source. The UN report (s7) resolves to securitycouncilreport.org's
document page for S/2021/229, which identifies the report and offers the
download; it is the document's hosting page, not a raw endpoint or a wrong
target, so it passes. Every quoted or numeric claim I could reach was confirmed
at the source.

No break found. No miscitation, no unsupported central claim, nothing routed to
the researcher.

## Cut

One sentence-by-sentence and two edge passes against `spec/slop.md`, plus the
dangling-referent, delete, prompt-leak, and recent-pattern checks.

Direct cut: the trailing appositive on the Hadshar paragraph, "candor that
measures how much of the case is still projection." It was decorative analysis
grafted onto three quoted hedges, and the projection point it made is already
carried by "The line cuts both ways" and the takeaway. The paragraph now lands
on the proponents' own words ("could backfire").

Edges held up better than the length would suggest. The negative-parallelism
constructions I checked are each earned against a misconception the piece names
(the "autonomous weapon killed people" reading of Libya; the steelman as a claim
about the present; AI running the world). The section openers in
who-makes-the-case ("The doubts come from more than one direction") do light
structural work rather than empty signposting, and the shown/projected opener
frames a real analytic move. The takeaway closer, "How worried to be tracks that
one gap, and nothing has closed it yet," lands the judgment in the piece's own
terms and does not hand the reader a question to ask, which the recent-pattern
notes flag. One sentence failed the slop test and was cut; the rest carry a
fact or a reasoning step.

Punctuation and counts: the body carries zero em-dashes (the single em-dash in
the file sits in a source title, outside the counted body), well under the
budget; no use of "leverage" or "load-bearing." Prose is grammatical throughout,
including display text and bookends.

Prompt leakage: the booster/dismisser framing echoes the commission's language,
but the article fills each side with its own reported specifics (the booster's
undemonstrated loyalty mechanism, the dismisser's cheap lab lever and live
surveillance) rather than lifting the instruction, so the point underneath is
the piece's own. No planning labels, selection rules, or "assignment fulfilled"
claims survive in the prose. The neighbor-distinction paragraph is required
boundary-drawing with live links, not leakage.

Furniture: the two bookends are the template's mandated apparatus, carry no
citations, and speak to the reader only where the template allows it. Both are
written to this lesson's particulars, and the takeaway resolves what the opener
sets up (surveillance here, compliance lever here, secret loyal force not). No
retired component (nb-holdsup, the legacy rs- blocks) appears. No component is
doing no work, and none is missing that the material needs.

## Reader

Read straight through, what the reader gets that the sources alone would not:
a way to take the coup argument apart and see exactly where it stops being
demonstrated, with the one new mechanism named and located inside a failure the
field already treats as unsolved. No single source performs that reduction or
grades the building blocks against each other; the report concedes its
capabilities are future, Filan raises the misalignment point in passing, and the
lab papers each show one lever, but assembling them into "the argument turns on a
loyalty no one can build or verify" is the article's own work. That matches the
draft-handoff's original-work sentence, and both answers survive. The prose sits
closer to the voice-guide exemplars than to a median summary: plain claims,
named and dated cases (Xinjiang's IJOP, the Kargu-2, $0.20 fine-tuning), and the
turn's reason placed in the same sentence as the turn, the way Mounk and
Goldsmith model it. The headline reads as the largest claim and the piece pays
it off.

## Decision

Approve. The argument is accurate, cited, even-handed in both directions, and
teaches the subject; my verification found no break, and the one slop sentence
is cut. The standing W-LENGTH-HIGH warning (2349 words, band 1200-2200) is not
publication-blocking, and it stands by design: the stamp counts the twelve-entry
sources block (~158 words) toward the total, so reaching under 2200 would take
roughly 150 words off the prose. Even removing both elements the writer marked
deferrable, the cyber building block and Tracy's critique, plus the fat, leaves
the count near 2260, so clearing the band would mean dropping required coverage
(a building block, a contradiction, or a critique direction) that the commission
stacked onto one lesson. Every remaining element is load-bearing, so the warning
stands rather than the piece being gutted to chase an unreachable number. My cut
lowered the count by about eleven words; the orchestrator will re-stamp before
preparing the PR.
