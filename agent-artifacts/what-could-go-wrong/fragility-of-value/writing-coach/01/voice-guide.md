# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/fragility-of-value (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a lesson for a reader who is sharp and widely read but new to how
alignment researchers reason, and it should sound like someone explaining the
fragility-of-value argument to them across a table, not performing it. Keep the
claims plain and the sentences short enough to follow on one read, the way
Karnofsky reports what the malaria trials did and did not show. When a line that
sounds impressive and a line that is understood pull apart, the piece is better
understood. Every term the argument needs, the complexity of value, a proxy, an
optimizer pushed to the extreme of its objective, is worth building from a
concrete case before the piece leans on it.

Open the argument the way Luu opens on Ballmer: state it at full strength, in
the terms its own defenders use, and let the reader feel why serious people hold
it before a word is said against it. The claim that a near-miss on values yields
a catastrophe rather than a near-miss reads best laid out as its authors meant
it, not pre-loaded with the doubt that comes later. The mild illustration, a
system optimizing for smiles, carries the mechanism when it is shown as a proof
rather than played as a punchline.

The turn where a proxy and the thing it stands for agree in the ordinary range
and part company at the extreme is the hinge of the lesson, and it can take the
treatment Alexander gives the bell curve: a specific case built one step at a
time until the shape is visible, each sentence a single move. Naming it "the
tails come apart" gives the reader a phrase for the idea; walking them through an
optimizer driven to the maximum of a slightly wrong objective, until they see
where the proxy and the goal separate, gives them the idea itself.

The series turns on the line between what real systems have done and what is
still analogy about systems that do not yet exist, and Karnofsky's separation of
what the trials proved from what they cannot say about present-day use is a model
for holding it. Where the evidence is a caught, bounded near-miss, the piece can
say exactly that; where the step to catastrophe rests on an optimizer more
single-minded than today's trained systems, it can mark that as projection. A
reader should be able to tell, at any point, which side of that line a claim sits
on.

The verdict is earned and cool, and it can afford to be short. Alexander's close
is the register for it: no inflated positive answer, a plain statement of what
the evidence will and will not support, and no drift toward doom or dismissal.
Where confidence outruns proof in either direction, the gap is worth naming in
the flat, specific way Karnofsky names the doubt he still carries, rather than
smoothing it over with a memorable line.

## Scott Alexander, "Beware The Man Of One Study"

Source: https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/12/12/beware-the-man-of-one-study/

> "For example, take medical research. Suppose a certain drug is weakly effective against a certain disease. After a few years, a bunch of different research groups have gotten their hands on it and done all sorts of different studies. In the best case scenario the average study will find the true result – that it’s weakly effective.
>
> But there will also be random noise caused by inevitable variation and by some of the experiments being better quality than others. In the end, we might expect something looking kind of like a bell curve. The peak will be at “weakly effective”, but there will be a few studies to either side."

Alexander teaches the point by building the example before he states it: a
weakly effective drug, many research groups, and only then the bell curve the
studies fall into. Each sentence carries one step, so a reader meeting the idea
for the first time follows the reasoning instead of taking the conclusion on
trust. He is present in the plain first-person "we might expect", which walks the
reader forward rather than handing down a result.

> "At some point in their education, most smart people usually learn not to credit arguments from authority. If someone says “Believe me about the minimum wage because I seem like a trustworthy guy,” most of them will have at least one neuron in their head that says “I should ask for some evidence”. If they’re really smart, they’ll use the magic words “peer-reviewed experimental studies.”"

Alexander speaks straight to a smart reader and credits them with the skepticism
they already have, then shows where it stops. The short mock-quotes make the
point without a diagram, and the register stays conversational without going
slack. The wry "at least one neuron" and "the magic words" are his humor, and it
comes from the reader's own habits, not from mocking the subject.

> "But the question remains: what happens when (like in most cases) you don’t have a funnel plot?
>
> I don’t have a good positive answer. I do have several good negative answers.
>
> Decrease your confidence about most things if you’re not sure that you’ve investigated every piece of evidence."

This is the verdict, and Alexander refuses to inflate it. He says outright that
he has no positive answer, only negative ones, and offers the one he can defend:
lower your confidence when you have not seen all the evidence. The honesty about
the limit of his own conclusion is where he is most himself, and it is what keeps
the close from tipping into either certainty or despair.

## Holden Karnofsky, "Minimal-trust investigations"

Source: https://www.cold-takes.com/minimal-trust-investigations/

> "The original studies involved very intense measures to make sure people were using their nets properly. In some cases these included daily or weekly visits to check usage. Modern-day LLIN distributions don't do anything like this. This made me realize that we can't assume a charity's LLIN distributions are resulting in proper usage of nets; we need to investigate modern-day LLIN usage separately."

Karnofsky draws the line his reader needs: what the original trials actually
established, and what they cannot tell you about how nets are used today. He does
not let the strength of the old studies stand in for the thing they never
measured, and he names the gap in flat, specific terms. He is visible in the
move from what the studies showed to "this made me realize that we can't assume",
where a proven result and an inherited assumption are pulled apart.

> "Because of this, I think it's simultaneously the case that (a) a lot of LLINs go unused or misused; (b) LLINs are still probably having roughly the effects we estimate. But I remain nervous that real LLIN usage could be much lower than the data indicates."

Karnofsky holds two true things at once without collapsing them into a slogan:
the nets are often unused, and they probably still work about as estimated. Then
he states the doubt he keeps rather than burying it. The plain "But I remain
nervous" is the whole register of the piece, a writer who tells you exactly how
sure he is and no surer.

> "But all in all, the case for LLINs holds up pretty well. It's reasonably close to the simpler case I gave at the top of this section.
>
> For GiveWell, this end result is the exception, not the rule."

The verdict is calm and proportionate: the case holds, and it is close to the
simple version he started with. He then adds that this was the easy case, rare
among his investigations, so the reader does not over-generalize from it.
Karnofsky is visible in that second sentence, the reflex to qualify a conclusion
the moment he has reached it.

## Dan Luu, "Steve Ballmer was an underrated CEO"

Source: https://danluu.com/ballmer/

> "There's a common narrative that Microsoft was moribund under Steve Ballmer and then later saved by the miraculous leadership of Satya Nadella. This is the dominant narrative in every online discussion about the topic I've seen and it's a commonly expressed belief "in real life" as well. While I don't have anything negative to say about Nadella's leadership in this post, this narrative underrates Ballmer's role in Microsoft's success."

Luu states the view he means to argue against in its strongest and most common
form before he says anything against it, and calls it the dominant view rather
than a weakened version. His own thesis follows in one plain sentence. The
fairness of the setup is where he is visible: he grants that he has nothing
negative to say about the rival and still intends to overturn the received story.

> "To the extent that Ballmer defended himself, it was by pointing out that the market appeared to be undervaluing Microsoft. Ballmer noted that Microsoft's market cap at the time was extremely low relative to its fundamentals/financials relative to Amazon, Google, Apple, Oracle, IBM, and Salesforce. This seems to have been a fair assessment by Ballmer as Microsoft has outperformed all of those companies since then."

Luu lets the record deliver the judgment. He reports what Ballmer claimed, checks
it against what the market later did, and the verdict rides on the evidence
rather than on the phrasing. The flat "This seems to have been a fair assessment"
is characteristic: an understated conclusion that points at the data instead of
raising its voice.
