# Editorial review: what-could-go-wrong/ai-moral-status (editor/01)

## Skeptic

Thesis: the serious case for AI welfare does not claim machines feel; it claims
we cannot yet rule out that some systems could come to matter morally, and that
both errors — spending concern on an empty system, or ignoring a system that
does have an inner life — carry real costs. The article's own contribution is
the spine: the strongest proponents and the sharpest opponent agree on what
current systems have actually been shown to do, and the live disagreement is
over which error is worse.

The claims it stands on, and how each held:

- **The case argues from uncertainty, not from a claim of consciousness.**
  Supported by the 2024 report's own words, quoted in the core-claim note ("our
  argument is not that AI systems definitely are, or will be, conscious ...
  Instead, our argument is that there is substantial uncertainty"). I confirmed
  the report's author list includes Chalmers and Birch, so the body's "a group
  including David Chalmers and Jonathan Birch" holds. Claim survives.
- **The indicator method finds no current system conscious, and no barrier to a
  future one.** I opened arxiv 2308.08708 directly: the abstract reads "no
  current AI systems are conscious, but also suggests that there are no obvious
  technical barriers to building AI systems which satisfy these indicators,"
  matching the article verbatim. The "would not mean ... definitely be
  conscious" qualifier is also present. The six table rows (RPT-2, GWT-2, GWT-3,
  HOT-2, AST-1, AE-1) match the evidence record's indicator list. Claim
  survives.
- **The pain/pleasure trade-off shows a behavior, not a feeling.** The model
  list (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4o, Command R+ switch above threshold; two others
  avoid at every level) matches the record. The authors' own caveat is quoted
  exactly ("is not integrating distinct sensory stimuli ... this inference does
  not translate to the LLM case straightforwardly"). The sharp line the desk
  wants — demonstrated output-behavior vs. unestablished experience — is drawn
  cleanly. Claim survives.
- **Both camps agree the current evidence is thin; they disagree on which error
  is worse.** This is the load-bearing synthesis, so I pushed hardest here. It
  holds, and the article is honest about its one seam: Suleyman agrees the
  outputs are weak evidence but goes further to assert systems are "internally
  blank," and the article flags precisely that overreach ("That there is
  definitely nothing behind them is a claim the science does not support").
  Steelmanned both sides; neither is summarized-then-dismissed. Claim survives.
- **Confidence outruns proof at both ends.** Chalmers' "under 10 percent"
  (current) and "25 percent or more" (within a decade), and his "extremely
  rough," are in the record; Suleyman "rounds the same question to none." The
  gap the desk asked for is named on both ends without the article taking a
  side. Claim survives.

Display text, checked descriptor by descriptor:
- Headline "The serious case for AI welfare never claims the machines feel" is a
  claim about the world the piece defends, not a grade of its own method.
- Dek attributes the 2024 report to "Long, Sebo, Chalmers and colleagues"
  (Chalmers is a verified co-author) and attributes the "leading scientific
  test" to no one — the earlier error (implying those three built the 2023
  indicator report) is gone. Chalmers is listed last among the ten 2024 authors,
  so naming him third is a curatorial pick, not a factual error; he is the name
  the body leans on. Dek carries two claims about the world, restates nothing in
  the headline, and matches none of the banned molds (no semicolon reversal, no
  suspended question, no comma-triad).
- Subheads are each a step of this argument in its own nouns and reconstruct the
  case when skimmed: the argument is a maybe → a checklist no model passes →
  what a working system has been shown to do → which error is worse.

`data-nb-kind` audit: s1–s6, s8 primary; s7 (Brookings) secondary — correct, it
reports others' positions before adding its own. Suleyman (s8) is primary for a
position held, not cited as an authority on whether the position is true; the
article identifies him as running Microsoft's AI division (speaker ID), which is
not a company-as-authority failure. "Anthropic's Claude Opus 4" names the system
studied, a reported fact. No author affiliations are printed for the flagged
papers. The Kyle Fish ~15–20% figure is cut (grep confirms no trace). Every one
of the eight hrefs returns 2xx as the article prints it, and both Background
links resolve to real library articles whose titles match the anchor text.

One break, routed: **the gaming-problem citation to s6 (Birch, *The Edge of
Sentience*) fails "cite only what you have read."** The researcher never opened
the book (publisher 403); the concept and its attribution to Birch were read in
the 2026 report (s1), and the s6 href resolves only to an OUP product page, not
to the chapter that owns the claim. Citing a source no one opened, for a passage
no one reached, is the impermissible case. The prose attribution "Birch calls it
the gaming problem" is fine (s1 attributes it to him); the numbered citation is
not. The honest fix moves that citation to s1 — which drops the count from 8 to
7, under the series' 8-source floor. Supplying the eighth non-taught primary (or
opening and verifying Birch ch. 16 directly, which is open access, so that s6
becomes legitimately citable) is the researcher's, and it is publication-
blocking. Routed below; I did not recite in place, because doing so would leave
the live file under the floor.

## Cut

Two sentences failed the slop test; both fixed directly.

- "The method is the honest part." — an empty grade of the method (idea + copula
  + assessment), losing no fact on deletion. Cut. The surviving contrast
  (checklist to inspect vs. verdict to trust) carries the real point and now has
  a clear antecedent: recast to "The list hands the reader something to inspect
  rather than a verdict to trust." The "rather than" contrast is earned — it
  corrects the natural expectation that a consciousness "test" delivers a verdict
  and it ties to the piece's own thesis about locating claims.
- One body semicolon patched a spot where the house default is a period:
  "The disagreement is not about what today's systems do; everyone agrees that is
  thin." → two sentences.

Edges walked on their own. The bookend opener's preview ("This lesson lays out
... Then it draws the line ... and finds where the loudest voices outrun the
proof") is the template's sanctioned self-reference and is not the banned "by the
end you will know" syllabus-closer; it previews specific content and resolves in
the takeaway. The body closes on a specific fact ("The test that could tell the
two apart has not been built"), not a moral, as the voice guide directs. The
several negative-parallelism constructions ("not the claim that machines feel /
it is the claim that ...", "not the machine's suffering but ours", "not
intelligence, and not a system reporting that it is conscious") each correct a
real, named misconception central to the piece — earned, not reflex, so left in
place. No em-dashes (proof-confirmed zero), no decorative-analysis tells, no
vague attribution, no prompt leakage: the reader-situation sentences in the
takeaway report the commission's stated reader rather than lifting its framing.

Furniture: note (core claim), table (indicators), holds-up grid, position card —
four pieces across the body plus the two bookends, each earning its place, the
page still reading as continuous prose. The absence of a Verdict note after the
holds-up grid is correct: the press editorial direction forbids closing the body
with a Verdict block. Headings vary in construction and none matches the recent
what-could-go-wrong shapes the brief flagged ("[Person] predicted X. None has
yet.", "What would count as the real thing", "The gap runs both ways").

## Reader

Read straight through as the paper's declared reader, what I have that the
sources alone would not give me: a way to hear "the AI said it was suffering" and
locate which kind of claim it is — a demonstrated behavior or an unfalsified
analogy — and to see that the field's loudest proponent and its sharpest skeptic
are arguing about which error to fear, not about what today's systems have been
shown to do. That is exactly the original-work sentence in the draft handoff, and
it survives the read: no single one of the eight documents draws that line; the
article threads them into it. The prose sits closer to the voice-guide exemplars
than to a median summary — it opens the body on one concrete interview, tests the
claim named-model by named-model, hands the uncertainty to named people rather
than to an authorial hedge, and ends on a fact. The headline, read as the largest
claim, is one the body defends.

## Edits

  list hands the reader something to inspect rather than a verdict to trust."
  (clear antecedent, grade removed).
- Changed the body semicolon in "The disagreement is not about what today's
  systems do; everyone agrees that is thin." to a period.

- **writer** — After the researcher resolves the source: either leave the

## Decision

revise — the article is sound in argument, sourcing labels, evenhandedness, and
prose, but its eighth source is cited without having been read; the honest fix
drops it under the series' 8-source floor, which only the researcher can restore.
