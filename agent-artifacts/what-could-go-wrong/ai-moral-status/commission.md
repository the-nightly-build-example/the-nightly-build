# Commission: what-could-go-wrong/ai-moral-status

## Assignment
Teach one argument about how AI could go wrong: that AI systems may come to
deserve moral consideration, and that treating morally relevant systems as mere
tools (or, conversely, over-attributing feelings to systems that have none) is a
way things go wrong. Open the argument at full strength, test it against what
real systems actually do, and bring it to the present. Leave the reader to decide
how worried to be.

## Why this argument, why now
The desk has covered thirty-six risk arguments, nearly all about misalignment and
catastrophe; this one — the moral status of AI systems, "model welfare" — is a
distinct, currently live strand of the same field's reasoning and appears
nowhere in the library. It has a 2023-2024 primary literature and an active
counter-argument, which makes it a clean fit for the desk's steelman-then-test
structure.

## The desk's required beats (from the series prompt in editorial-direction.md)
- Open with the argument at full strength. Name who made it and what they saw
  that worried them; lay out the reasoning as its most careful defender would
  (moral status tracks capacities like sentience or agency; functionalism makes
  the substrate not obviously disqualifying; act under uncertainty).
- Test it against what real systems do. Draw a sharp line between what has been
  shown in a working system (models produce text claiming feelings; the
  indicator-based assessments of current systems) and what is analogy or
  guesswork about systems that do not exist yet (any experience behind the text).
- Bring it to the present: who makes the argument today and what they want done,
  checked against the most recent evidence; and the strongest counter-argument
  (over-attribution / the risk of being manipulated by seemingly-conscious
  systems). Where confidence outruns proof — the certainty that AIs suffer, and
  the certainty that they never could — name the gap.
- Work from the original documents, not commentary. Study how the field reasons
  about this without joining it or writing it off. Name no company as an
  authority; cite the academic works and named individuals.

## Boundaries
- This is not a consciousness explainer and not an alignment lesson. Link
  adjacent published lessons at first use rather than re-teaching them (e.g.
  what-could-go-wrong/situational-awareness for what models model about
  themselves; the-mechanics/sycophancy for why models say what is rewarded).
- Keep three things distinct throughout: reported fact, estimate, and synthesis.
  A verdict is welcome only if earned from the cited evidence.
- Steelman both the case for moral consideration and the case against before
  weighing them.

## Required contribution (the original work the writer must name)
The article gives the reader a way to tell the parts of the model-welfare
argument that rest on demonstrated behavior from the parts that rest on
unfalsified analogy, so they can read the next "the AI said it was suffering"
headline and locate exactly which kind of claim it is.
