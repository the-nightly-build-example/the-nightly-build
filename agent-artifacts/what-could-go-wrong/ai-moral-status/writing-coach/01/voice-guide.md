# Voice guide: What Could Go Wrong — AI moral status

## How this piece should sound

Open on something a reader can picture, not on the abstraction "the moral status
of AI systems." Zimmer opens his piece on rival theories of consciousness with a
packed auditorium and a twenty-five-year-old wine bet between two named men;
Morell opens hers on a barking collie. This lesson has an equivalent: whoever
first worried out loud that a model might be owed something, in some specific
place, about some specific system. Start there before the argument gets stated in
general terms.

When the piece gives the argument that AI systems might already warrant moral
concern its full hearing, give it the shape Rothman gives Chalmers: the sharpest
formulation its own defenders use, delivered before any answer to it appears.
Chalmers gets "How does the water of the brain turn into the wine of
consciousness?" on the page whole, and Dennett's opposing account gets the same
treatment a few pages later — neither is summarized and then dismissed in the
same breath. A position stated at that strength is what makes the later testing
of it feel earned rather than staged.

Test the argument the way Morell tests the mirror test: species by species, named
researcher by named researcher, what was actually done and what a rival
researcher concluded from the identical result. This lesson's own job — separating
what a working AI system has actually been shown to do from what is still analogy
to a mind that might or might not resemble it — will read as demonstrated rather
than asserted if it is done at that level of specificity: which behavior, in which
system, reported by whom, disputed by whom. A blowfish scraping a dye mark off
its throat carries more than a sentence about "some species passing."

Where the evidence gives out, a named person may be the one who says so, the way
Zimmer hands that line to Anil Seth and Morell hands it to Lori Marino, rather
than the writer supplying the hedge in his own voice. Model welfare is a subject
where confident people sit on both sides of what is still unknown; quoting one of
them saying plainly that the evidence does not yet settle it can do more honest
work than an authorial "of course, much remains uncertain."

None of the three pieces closes on a summarizing moral. Zimmer ends on Chalmers
taking a new bet he says he hopes to lose. Rothman ends on Dennett at the
shoreline, still turning the question over. Morell ends on a scientist calling the
whole test "fun." The lesson template's takeaway card is where this piece is
allowed to state what it found; the body can afford to end on a specific fact or a
specific voice instead, the way these three do, and let the takeaway do the
summarizing.

The reader has not met this subject before, which none of these three exemplars
had to assume of theirs. Moral status, sentience, and moral patienthood each earn
a plain-word definition at first use, in the sentence where the word first
appears, without the pieces losing the unhurried, even-handed register they were
chosen to show.

## Carl Zimmer, "2 Leading Theories of Consciousness Square Off"

Source: https://carlzimmer.com/2-leading-theories-of-consciousness-square-off/ (originally The New York Times, July 1, 2023)

> "On a muggy June night in Greenwich Village, more than 800 neuroscientists, philosophers and curious members of the public packed into an auditorium. They came for the first results of an ambitious investigation into a profound question: What is consciousness?"

The scale is a specific number, not "a large crowd," and the question waits until
the second sentence instead of opening the piece. Zimmer is visible in the choice
to let a room full of people stand in for the stakes before he tells the reader
what those stakes are.

> "In 2017, Dr. Koch, by then working at the Allen Institute in Seattle, invited a dozen experts to the institute to plan out experiments that would test the two theories against each other. Dr. Chalmers also came from New York University to provide philosophical rigor. They agreed in advance on what the results of each experiment would mean for each theory. And the experiments would be conducted by independent scientists who had not pushed for either theory."

This is how a test gets described so that its result means something: who set the
terms, when, and that the terms were fixed before anyone knew which theory would
win. Zimmer never uses the phrase "rigorous methodology" — the paragraph just
lists what made it rigorous and lets the reader do the classifying.

> "'The current experiment is enough to show that neither theory is presently sufficient,' said Anil Seth, a neuroscientist at the University of Sussex in England. But the 25-year bet, at least, has been resolved: No one has found a clear neural correlate of consciousness."

The uncertainty is stated by a named scientist, not by Zimmer, and it is stated as
a finding rather than a caveat. The sentence after it doesn't soften what Seth
said or add "however" — it moves straight to the one thing that was in fact
settled.

## Joshua Rothman, "Daniel Dennett's Science of the Soul"

Source: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2017/03/27/daniel-dennetts-science-of-the-soul (The New Yorker, March 27, 2017)

> "At sunset on the last day of the conference, the experts found themselves circling a familiar puzzle known as the 'zombie problem.' Suppose that you're a scientist studying octopuses. How would you know whether an octopus is conscious? It interacts with you, responds to its environment, and evidently pursues goals, but a nonconscious robot could also do those things. The problem is that there's no way to observe consciousness directly."

Rothman explains a technical puzzle by handing the reader a single animal and a
single question to ask about it, rather than defining the puzzle first and
illustrating it second. The comparison to a robot arrives inside the explanation,
doing work, instead of announcing itself as an analogy.

> "Chalmers accused Dennett and the physicalists of focussing on the 'easy problems' of consciousness — questions about the workings of neurons or other cognitive systems — while ignoring the 'hard problem.' In a formulation he likes: 'How does the water of the brain turn into the wine of consciousness?' Since then, the 'hard problem' has been a rallying cry for those philosophers who think that Dennett's view of the mind is incomplete."

Chalmers's own line is quoted rather than paraphrased, and Rothman tells the
reader it is a formulation Chalmers "likes" — a small admission that this is the
opposing camp's chosen phrasing, not Rothman's summary of it. The sentence that
follows states what the phrase did in the field, which is a fact, not an opinion
about whether the phrase is right.

> "I still can't quite picture how neurons create consciousness. But, perhaps because I can take a stance toward my mother that I can't take toward myself, my belief in the 'hard problem' has dissolved. On an almost visceral level, I find it easier to accept the reality of the material mind. I have moved from agnosticism to calm conviction."

Rothman states his own change of mind as a fact about himself, hedged exactly
where his understanding is actually incomplete ("I still can't quite picture
how"). He is visible in the specific reason he gives — his mother, not the
argument alone — for a shift he doesn't claim the argument by itself produced.

## Virginia Morell, "What do mirror tests test?"

Source: https://aeon.co/essays/what-can-the-mirror-test-say-about-self-awareness-in-animals (Aeon, 23 October 2019)

> "When our collie, Buckaroo, was four months old, he began barking at his reflection in our glass patio door every evening, doing his best to alert us to this unknown, bold little pup outside. Of course, the reflected dog didn't back away, so Buck lunged at him and scratched the door jamb – actions that did get our attention."

Morell opens on one dog on one evening rather than on the mirror test as a
concept. The comic detail — that Buck's barking worked on the humans even though
it did nothing to the reflection — is Morell's own noticing, not something the
science required her to include.

> "And while some contend that certain species, such as dolphins, elephants and European magpies, have passed, Gallup disagrees. 'I do have an open mind, and am not against other species, but none has succeeded so far,' he says. To him, this is an indication that the test reveals a clear dividing line between animals with minds and those that are mindless. However, a growing number of scientists say that the fault lies not with the animals, but with the test."

Both sides of a real disagreement get a named holder — Gallup by his own quote,
the other side by what a "growing number of scientists" argue — and Morell
states what each side takes the same result to mean without saying which one she
thinks is right. The word "however" carries the whole pivot; nothing is added to
tell the reader how to feel about it.

> "Indeed, Marino breaks with Gallup when animals don't pass the test: she doesn't think it means they lack self-awareness. All animals, she argues, are self-aware: 'We just don't know what it means to be self-aware and not pass the mirror test.' And so until other tests of self-awareness that are appropriate for more species are devised, we are stuck with the one that works – and at least for us, adds de Waal, the mirror tests 'are fun.'"

The essay's last word is Marino's honest admission of a limit ("we just don't
know") followed immediately by de Waal calling the whole enterprise "fun" — Morell
lets the piece end on an unresolved question and a small joke rather than
resolving the tension herself in a closing line of her own.
