# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/situational-awareness

## How this piece should sound

This lesson has one hard job: state the situational-awareness argument the way
Cotra and Evans's collaborators would state it themselves, then draw a line
through the evidence with the same hand that just built the case up. The three
exemplars below show that move at three different scales — a paragraph, a
single sentence, and a whole essay's structure — and the piece can use all
three.

When the lesson names who first worried about this and why, name them the way
Piper names Yann LeCun and Stuart Russell rather than "some researchers":
Cotra's training-game framing, Evans's group turning it into something
measurable, Laine et al.'s SAD benchmark. A claim with a name attached is one a
reader can go check; "the field worries" is one they can't.

The turn from full-strength argument to measured evidence is the piece's
hinge, and it should read like Alexander's line about Bem: commit plainly to
what the data shows, and refuse just as plainly to commit to what it doesn't.
The SAD benchmark and the out-of-context reasoning results are a real signal —
say that outright. Whether that signal adds up to a model that would use its
own awareness to defeat a real eval unprompted is the open question, and the
piece should be as comfortable naming that gap as naming the result on the
other side of it.

Where the lesson has to say what it doesn't know — whether anyone has caught a
model using situational awareness against a live safety eval, say — say it the
way Grace says she doesn't know if a gap in her own argument has already been
closed elsewhere. That is a different, more honest sentence than "there is no
evidence of this," because it separates what the researcher has read from what
the field may have shown. This piece's original contribution is exactly that
separation — measured versus used — so the sentence that states it should
carry the argument's real weight.

The commission already rules out the recent habit of landing on a balance-beam
catchphrase. Grace's own conclusion is the model for what to replace it with:
after pages of steelmanning and testing, she names what she does and doesn't
see reason to believe, in one register, and stops. The takeaway can do the
same with its own two readings — "models already know and are hiding it"
against "it's pattern-matching, there's no real awareness" — by saying plainly
which one SAD and the out-of-context experiments support and which one they
don't reach, in wording built for this argument alone.

## Kelsey Piper, "The case for taking AI seriously as a threat to humanity"

Source: https://www.vox.com/future-perfect/2018/12/21/18126576/ai-artificial-intelligence-machine-learning-safety-alignment

> "It's tempting to conclude that there's a pitched battle between AI-risk skeptics and AI-risk believers. In reality, they might not disagree as profoundly as you would think."

The first sentence states the reading a reader would reach on their own; the
second overturns it in the same breath, with a plain claim. Piper earns the
correction in the very next paragraph by naming Yann LeCun's actual published
position.

> "There's intense disagreement in the field on timelines for critical advances in AI. While AI safety experts agree on many features of the safety problem, they're still making the case to research teams in their own field, and they disagree on some of the details. There's substantial disagreement on how badly it could go, and on how likely it is to go badly."

This breaks "no consensus" into three separate axes of disagreement — when,
how bad, how likely — each its own clause. A reader leaves knowing what kind
of disagreement it is.

> "No matter whether or not humanity should be afraid, we should definitely be doing our homework."

The piece's last line. It reroutes the fear question the whole explainer just
spent nine sections laying out both sides of, toward something both sides can
act on, and closes there without asking the reader to pick one.

## Scott Alexander, "The Control Group Is Out Of Control"

Source: https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/04/28/the-control-group-is-out-of-control/

> "This is *far* better than the average meta-analysis. Bem has always been pretty careful and this is no exception. Yet its conclusion is that psychic powers exist."

Three short sentences after several paragraphs crediting Bem's methodology
point by point. The third puts "good study" and "wrong conclusion" side by
side, plainly, and leaves them standing there.

> "Bem definitely picked up a signal. The only question is whether it's a signal of psi, or a signal of poor experimental technique."

The sentence the whole essay is built to earn. It commits fully to what the
data shows, a signal, and just as fully declines to say what caused it, in one
plain compound sentence.

> "This is not a criticism of Bem or a criticism of parapsychology. It's something that is inherent to the practice of meta-analysis, and even more, inherent to the practice of science. Other than a few very exceptional large medical trials, there is not a study in the world that would survive the level of criticism I am throwing at Bem right now. I think Bem is wrong. The level of criticism it would take to prove a wrong study wrong is higher than that almost any existing study can withstand. That is not encouraging for existing studies."

He separates his verdict on the paper's rigor from his verdict on its
conclusion, labels his own read as his own ("I think Bem is wrong"), and
follows it immediately with what that verdict costs everyone else's studies
too.

## Katja Grace, "Counterarguments to the basic AI x-risk case"

Source: https://aiimpacts.org/counterarguments-to-the-basic-ai-x-risk-case/

> "A 'gap' is not necessarily unfillable, and may have been filled in any of the countless writings on this topic that I haven't read. I might even think that a given one can probably be filled. I just don't know what goes in it."

She distinguishes "I haven't seen this answered" from "this hasn't been
answered" in three short sentences, refusing to claim more territory than her
own reading covers, right after promising a list of holes in an argument she
takes seriously.

> "Overall, I don't know of strong reason to expect very fast growth in AI capabilities at around human-level AI performance, though I hear such arguments might exist."

One flat sentence stating what she personally lacks evidence for, immediately
qualified by naming that evidence she hasn't seen might still exist. That
second clause is doing real work — it keeps the sentence from reading as "this
isn't true."

> "I think there are quite a few gaps in the argument, as I understand it. My current guess (prior to reviewing other arguments and integrating things carefully) is that enough uncertainties might resolve in the dangerous directions that existential risk from AI is a reasonable concern. I don't at present though see how one would come to think it was overwhelmingly likely."

The essay's conclusion, after thousands of words hunting for holes in the case
for AI extinction risk. It lands on neither the doom reading nor the dismissal
— "reasonable concern," not "overwhelmingly likely" — with each phrase doing
real evidential work.
