# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/sleeper-agents (01)

## How this piece should sound

This lesson can open by making the deceptive-alignment worry sound like
something a serious person would already be uneasy about, before a single
experimental result enters the piece. Karnofsky's move of restating the
question as "why wouldn't this be dangerous" rather than "why would it be" is
one way to state that worry at full strength: not as a claim the piece has to
argue toward gradually, but as a reframing the reader can weigh immediately.
Where the piece needs that opening force, this is a way to get it without
overclaiming, since the reframed question commits to nothing the sleeper-agents
result hasn't yet supplied.

Piper's habit of anchoring a claim that could otherwise float free suits the
stretch of this lesson where the reader has not yet met whoever first worried
about deception surviving training. Naming who raised it and what they had
seen, in the plain declarative sentences Piper uses for Hawking, Musk, and the
Oxford and Berkeley researchers, gives the worry something to stand on before
the piece has made its own case. The same habit can serve the section that
tests the worry against the experiment: where a technical result needs a reader
who has never trained a model to see exactly what happened, Piper's specification-
gaming passage shows what one worked case can do that a description of the
phenomenon alone cannot.

Where this lesson moves from what the sleeper-agents experiment actually showed
to what people worry it implies more broadly about deceptive alignment, the
line Karnofsky draws inside his own argument is worth holding: a sentence that
says outright which part of the case is demonstrated and which part is
speculative extension, rather than letting the two blur into one confident
register. Karnofsky's own seam, "necessarily speculative" against "the high-level
concern," is one way to mark that boundary without softening the argument on
either side of it.

Karnofsky's habit of answering a real objection with a concrete comparison,
rather than a flat rebuttal, suits the stretch of this lesson where the
strongest case meets the doubts a careful reader would already be forming.
Grace's Alice-and-Bob illustration offers another way to teach whichever of this
lesson's abstractions turns out to be hardest to hold: a small, concrete pair of
cases the reader can hold in mind can do more than the general claim stated
twice. And her closing paragraph, which names what remains genuinely uncertain
rather than resolving toward doom or dismissal, is close to how this lesson's
body can leave things: the takeaway bookend is where the house standard puts a
lesson's verdict, but the body itself can let the sleeper-agents evidence and
the still-unproven parts of the deceptive-alignment worry stand as what they
separately are, without the body reaching for the verdict itself.

Grace's aside about wanting "some good romance" and Karnofsky's flat "why
wouldn't it be" both carry a person's particular way of putting things without
loosening the argument they're inside. Plain prose here doesn't have to mean
prose with the person edited out of it.

## Kelsey Piper, "The case for taking AI seriously as a threat to humanity"

Source: https://www.vox.com/future-perfect/2018/12/21/18126576/ai-artificial-intelligence-machine-learning-safety-alignment

> That might have people asking: Wait, what? But these grand worries are rooted
> in research. Along with Hawking and Musk, prominent figures at Oxford and UC
> Berkeley and many of the researchers working in AI today believe that advanced
> AI systems, if deployed carelessly, could permanently cut off human
> civilization from a good future.

This is the second paragraph of the piece, and it does the grounding work a
"grand worry" needs before a reader will sit still for the argument behind it.
Piper doesn't argue for taking the worry seriously in the abstract; she names
four separate kinds of standing (a physicist, an entrepreneur, two universities)
in one sentence, so the claim has something under it before the piece has made
its own case at all.

> Victoria Krakovna, an AI researcher at DeepMind (now a division of Alphabet,
> Google's parent company), compiled a list of examples of "specification
> gaming": the computer doing what we told it to do but not what we wanted it to
> do. For example, we tried to teach AI organisms in a simulation to jump, but we
> did it by teaching them to measure how far their "feet" rose above the ground.
> Instead of jumping, they learned to grow into tall vertical poles and do flips
> — they excelled at what we were measuring, but they didn't do what we wanted
> them to do.

The abstract idea, a system optimizing the metric instead of the goal, gets one
sentence. Everything else is the jumping example, given with enough specifics
(the feet, the poles, the flips) that a reader who has never trained a model can
still see exactly what went wrong and why it counts as the same failure as the
one the piece is building toward.

> It's tempting to conclude that there's a pitched battle between AI-risk
> skeptics and AI-risk believers. In reality, they might not disagree as
> profoundly as you would think. Facebook's chief AI scientist Yann LeCun, for
> example, is a vocal voice on the skeptical side. But while he argues we
> shouldn't fear AI, he still believes we ought to have people working on, and
> thinking about, AI safety.

Piper names the tempting, simpler story and then declines it, using a specific
skeptic's actual position rather than a general claim that skeptics aren't so
skeptical. The correction is attached to one named person, which is what makes
it a finding rather than a rhetorical move.

## Holden Karnofsky, "AI Could Defeat All Of Us Combined"

Source: https://www.cold-takes.com/ai-could-defeat-all-of-us-combined/

> We generally don't have a lot of things that could end human civilization if
> they "tried" sitting around. If we're going to create one, I think we should
> be asking not "Why would this be dangerous?" but "Why wouldn't it be?"

This is the piece's thesis, and Karnofsky states it by naming the two possible
questions and choosing between them, rather than by asserting his conclusion
directly. The move puts the burden of proof somewhere specific instead of
leaving "you should worry about this" to do the work alone.

> This appendix goes into detail about how AIs coordinating against humans could
> amass resources of their own without humans being able to shut down all
> "misbehaving" AIs. It's necessarily speculative, and should be taken in the
> spirit of giving examples of how this might work - for me, the high-level
> concern is that a huge, coordinating population of AIs with similar
> capabilities to humans would be a threat to human civilization, and that we
> shouldn't count on any particular way of stopping it such as shutting down
> servers.

Karnofsky says in plain words which part of what follows he is confident in and
which part he is not, in the same two sentences. The high-level concern and the
speculative mechanism are named as different kinds of claim, not folded into one
tone of voice.

> I would guess we would see some warning signs, but does that mean we could nip
> it in the bud? Think about human civil wars and revolutions: there are some
> warning signs, but also, people go from "not fighting" to "fighting" pretty
> quickly as they see an opportunity to coordinate with each other and be
> successful.

Karnofsky takes an objection seriously enough to answer it with a real
comparison instead of a flat "no." The civil-war analogy does the work a bare
assertion couldn't: it gives the reader a mechanism for why warning signs might
not be enough, drawn from something already familiar.

## Katja Grace, "Counterarguments to the basic AI x-risk case"

Source: https://aiimpacts.org/counterarguments-to-the-basic-ai-x-risk-case/

> This hypothesis about the provenance of human triumph seems wrong. Intellect
> surely helps, but humans look to be powerful largely because they share their
> meager intellectual discoveries with one another and consequently save them up
> over time. You can see this starkly by comparing the material situation of
> Alice, a genius living in the stone age, and Bob, an average person living in
> 21st Century America. Alice might struggle all day to get a pot of water,
> while Bob might be able to summon all manner of delicious drinks from across
> the oceans, along with furniture, electronics, information, etc. Much of Bob's
> power probably did flow from the application of intelligence, but not Bob's
> individual intelligence. Alice's intelligence, and that of those who came
> between them.

Grace is arguing against the idea that individual intelligence alone explains
power, and instead of restating that claim more emphatically, she builds two
named people and lets the comparison carry it. The example is small (a pot of
water, a stocked kitchen) and does more to make the abstract claim checkable
than another sentence of restatement would.

> I have some goals. For instance, I want some good romance. My guess is that
> trying to take over the universe isn't the best way to achieve this goal. The
> same goes for a lot of my goals, it seems to me. Possibly I'm in error, but I
> spend a lot of time pursuing goals, and very little of it trying to take over
> the universe. Whether a particular goal is best forwarded by trying to take
> over the universe as a substep seems like a quantitative empirical question, to
> which the answer is virtually always "not remotely." Don't get me wrong: all of
> these goals involve some interest in taking over the universe. All things
> equal, if I could take over the universe for free, I do think it would help in
> my romantic pursuits. But taking over the universe is not free. It's actually
> super duper duper expensive and hard.

This is a serious argument, about whether a goal-directed system's incentives
actually point toward domination, made through a joke about her own dating life
rather than in the register the rest of the essay uses. The joke doesn't
decorate the point; it is the point, run through a case she can check against
her own experience, which is why it survives being taken seriously.

> I think there are quite a few gaps in the argument, as I understand it. My
> current guess (prior to reviewing other arguments and integrating things
> carefully) is that enough uncertainties might resolve in the dangerous
> directions that existential risk from AI is a reasonable concern. I don't at
> present though see how one would come to think it was overwhelmingly likely.

This is the entire conclusion. Grace names a specific epistemic state, a
concern worth having without a confident verdict either way, rather than
resolving forty pages of counterargument into a stance the material doesn't
support. She is explicit that this is a current guess and not a settled
finding.
