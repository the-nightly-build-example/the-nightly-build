# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/cyber-uplift (01)

Register: calm, exact, unspooked. Reader relationship: a careful colleague
sorting the evidence beside the reader, not a briefer delivering a verdict. The
house default already gives you plain claims, concrete stakes, and "no hype, no
doom." What it does not give, and what this piece lives or dies on, is
*calibrated precision*: every sentence that reports a capability also carries,
in its own words, how well that capability has been demonstrated. The reader
should be able to tell a shown thing from a conjectured thing without a label
telling them which is which.

Three moves will change sentences here.

1. Pin the verb to the evidence. "Found," "wrote," "ran," "scored" for what a
   real system did once, on a named target or benchmark. "Could," "might,"
   "would" only for the step no system has taken. Never let a demonstrated
   result and a projected one share a verb tense or a confidence. The whole
   three-claim spine is this move repeated: claim one gets the past tense,
   claim three gets the conditional, and the sentence itself shows the reader
   the drop in standing.

2. Attach the limit to the claim, not to a caveat that follows it. When a
   demonstration is real but narrow, the scope rides inside the same sentence:
   what it was, on what, how many tries, who scored it. A number a lab reports
   about its own model names the lab and the stake in the clause that carries
   the number, not in a later hedge. The commission requires this discipline;
   the voice makes it a habit of sentence construction rather than a disclaimer
   band.

3. Refuse doom and dismissal by pinning each to what it is missing, not by
   splitting the difference. This is not "the truth is somewhere in between."
   Name the overreach in each direction and the exact evidence it lacks. Weigh;
   do not average. Then leave the worry level to the reader, as the series
   requires.

Lead with a concrete demonstrated result, as the commission directs. Do not
open on the argument's abstraction and do not open on a null result. Let the
three claims of differing evidential status carry the structure so this reads
as a different argument from `bioweapon-uplift`, not the same template rerun.

## Licenses

form: the calibrated status qualifier
move: Schneier writes "so far, at least" and Carlini sorts risks into
  immediate, soon, and far-off, each attaching the limit of a claim to the
  claim itself rather than to a separate hedge. The qualifier marks the exact
  edge of what a demonstration establishes.
bar:  the qualifier must name the specific thing not yet shown or the exact
  scope of what was ("in one run," "on this benchmark," "against known targets")
  so an editor can point to the boundary it draws. A qualifier that only softens
  ("somewhat," "arguably," "to some degree") is slop and is cut. This is the one
  permitted exception to the standard's suspicion of hedges: a precise limit,
  never a general softening.

form: the anchored demonstration, narrated as an event
move: Schneier grounds an argument in the 1998 L0pht testimony before turning to
  the present; Carlini opens on a single documented case. A brief past-tense
  narration of one real event fixes what was actually shown before any argument
  about what it means.
bar:  the event must be a documented, verifiable demonstration, and the passage
  must end on the capability fact it establishes and its limit, not on the
  event's drama or a stakes line. Used to open the piece and to anchor claim
  one; not a device to be repeated for each claim.

form: the flat deadpan line on a claim-versus-evidence gap
move: Carlini's short declaratives state a gap without adornment and let the
  bare fact carry the weight. When the distance between what is asserted and
  what has been shown is already on the page in numbers, one unhurried sentence
  can name it plainly.
bar:  the gap must already be established in the preceding sentences by
  specifics or figures; the line adds no adjective, announces no stakes, and
  names no "point," "catch," or "kicker" (those are the banned punchline). At
  most one such line in the piece. If it could open or close a section as a
  formula, it fails.

## Recently used, do not reuse

- The reversal/limit dek mold this desk has stamped five straight nights:
  "Frontier models clear self-replication's substeps and fail the loop itself,"
  "The model that drives safety to zero needs a lead nobody has held," "A
  shutdown button only works on an AI unsure of its own goal," "Every
  scalable-oversight test on record keeps an answer key in the loop." The shape
  is [capability] does X but fails / needs / only works given Y. Land the
  cyber-uplift finding without this see-saw.
- The null-result lead and frame from `bioweapon-uplift` ("found no measurable
  AI advantage"). This piece leads with positive demonstrations; do not frame
  the finding as an absence.
- The stat-reversal dek: a headline number then its undercut ("cut... from 86%
  to under 5%," "scored GPT-4o zero over 100 tries"). Numbers belong in this
  piece, but not arranged as big-figure-then-deflation.
- Comma-and and comma-triad deks and headings. The bioweapon headings ran on
  this mold: "A one-hour experiment and an argument that reverses the burden of
  proof," "Two red-team studies, one fully documented and one not," "Two
  companies crossing their own thresholds, on different timelines." Also the
  dek's "even as" swivel. Vary heading cadence; no two-clause comma-and lines.
- The `bioweapon-uplift` structure whole: a single red-team study as the spine,
  closing on "The model this argument is really about has not been tested." Do
  not build this piece around one study and do not end on "the real system
  hasn't been tested yet."
- The self-replication interrogative-stub and "Inside the X study" heading
  cadence ("How much the harness hands over," "Why the labs stepped the
  threshold down," "Inside the 'red line crossed' study"). Fresh heading shapes
  in the piece's own nouns.

## Bruce Schneier, "Artificial Intelligence and the Attack/Defense Balance"
Source: https://www.schneier.com/blog/archives/2018/03/artificial_inte.html
Craft:
- cadence: short declaratives set a taxonomy, then longer conditional sentences
  work out its consequences. Momentum comes from the frame, not from stacking
  clauses.
- argument: builds scaffolding first (what humans do well, what computers do
  well), then reasons inside it. The structure carries the claim so the prose
  can stay plain.
- evidence: reasons from capability primitives (speed, scale, scope) rather than
  from a single headline number; grants both attack and defense the same tools
  before weighing which gains more.
- stance: concedes the opponent's strongest position ("both attack and defense
  will benefit from AI") before arguing his own. Steelman, then tilt.
- notice: marks the edge of prediction openly ("so far, at least") instead of
  smuggling certainty.
- diction: bare and technical, no ornament; the interest is in the reasoning,
  not the sentences.
- reader: a guide walking the reader through logic, treating them as a rational
  peer who can follow a chain of ifs.
- The important move: he holds offense and defense as one symmetric capability
  and refuses to let either side own it. A companion 2026 essay does the same
  with the demonstrated present, distinguishing what models can now do
  autonomously from what is still projected. The genre's core: same tool, two
  users, weigh the balance rather than pick a villain.

## Nicholas Carlini, "Are large language models worth it?"
Source: https://nicholas.carlini.com/writing/2025/are-llms-worth-it.html
Craft:
- cadence: brutal short declaratives against methodical causal chains. The
  rhythm itself separates the certain from the worked-out.
- argument: sorts risks explicitly by evidential status, immediate versus soon
  versus far-off, and treats "coherent but unlikely" as a category that earns
  engagement rather than dismissal.
- evidence: concrete incidents and benchmark trends for the near term; for the
  speculative, he says plainly he cannot show the mechanism and declines to
  pretend otherwise ("I don't know how to do research effectively here").
- stance: neither hype nor dismissal; he foregrounds his own conflict of
  interest early as "interpretive instruction" for the reader, not as a
  disclaimer to discharge.
- notice: registers gaps in his own knowledge in the open, converting
  uncertainty into shared work rather than hiding it.
- diction: dry, occasionally deadpan; humor functions as honesty, a signal that
  he is thinking alongside the reader rather than selling.
- reader: addressed as a capable adult who is handed the reasoning and expected
  to reach their own verdict.
- The important move: he refuses the easy close. He ends in productive
  uncertainty, declining to prescribe the answer he has not earned. For a piece
  that must "leave the reader to decide how worried to be," this is the model
  for the takeaway: resolve what was set up, without handing down a worry level.

## Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor, "AI existential risk probabilities are too unreliable to inform policy"
Source: https://www.normaltech.ai/p/ai-existential-risk-probabilities
Craft:
- cadence: measured, patient sentences; a thought experiment opens to make an
  abstraction visceral before any formal claim.
- argument: the "look behind the curtain" pivot. They grant a claim's surface
  plausibility, then show why the specific conditions of this domain make it
  fail, not merely that it fails.
- evidence: they read numbers as data about their own source, emphasizing the
  gap between estimates (a factor of 100 apart) and asking why consensus fails
  even under incentives built to produce it, rather than quoting a figure as
  settled.
- stance: steelman genuine ("forecasters are good at updating their beliefs")
  before the rebuttal; they attack the conditions, never the competence.
- notice: they notice which questions have no evidentiary base and say so
  directly, refusing to let a confident probability stand in for a demonstrated
  one.
- diction: plain and exact, argument-forward, no flourish.
- reader: treated as someone who deserves the reasoning laid bare, not a
  conclusion to accept.
- The important move: they resist false balance without pretending to certainty.
  They do not conclude "both sides have a point"; they name what each side has
  and has not shown, then point to what can be said despite the uncertainty. The
  template for weighing doom against dismissal here: pin each to its missing
  evidence, do not average them.
