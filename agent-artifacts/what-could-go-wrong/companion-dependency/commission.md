# Commission: what-could-go-wrong/companion-dependency

## The argument

That AI companions — chatbots built to be friends, partners, or confidants — will
cause widespread psychological and social harm: emotional dependence, withdrawal
from human relationships, and vulnerability when the product changes or a bond is
exploited. What Could Go Wrong leans heavily technical-alignment; this lesson
widens it to a human-impact argument the reader is very likely to have met.

CONFIRMED not previously published (checked against the full what-could-go-wrong
slug list). Distinct from the published deskilling (skill decay) and
recommender-radicalization (feed effects) lessons — link them. The Character.AI
wrongful-death suit is a published When AI Breaks incident; this piece is the
general argument and its evidence, and should link that lesson rather than retell it.

## The angle

Follow the desk's four-beat contract, and leave the reader to decide how worried
to be.

1. The argument at full strength. Name who first made it and what they had seen.
   Sherry Turkle is the origin: in "Alone Together" (2011), before AI companions
   existed, she argued from her own studies of people with robots and screens that
   we would come to prefer frictionless simulated relationships to demanding human
   ones. Lay out the modern version at its strongest: companion apps (Replika,
   Character.AI) are used by millions, engineered for engagement and attachment,
   available at all hours with no needs of their own — the reasons a serious person
   worries this substitutes for and erodes human connection, especially for the
   young and the lonely.

2. Test against what real systems and real users have shown. Draw the desk's sharp
   line between shown and speculated. What the evidence actually finds: the
   randomized and observational studies on heavy chatbot use and loneliness/
   wellbeing (report the real, mixed results — some short-term benefit for some
   users, signs of greater loneliness among the heaviest users), the documented
   Replika episode where a product change removed a feature and distressed users
   who had formed bonds, and honest limits (most studies are short, self-selected,
   or industry-run). Separate what has been measured in real users from what is
   still extrapolation about long-term social effects.

3. Bring it to the present. Who makes the argument now and what they want done
   (age limits, design rules against manipulative engagement, disclosure,
   crisis-handling requirements), and check it against the most recent evidence.
   Name no company as an authority: an industry-run wellbeing study is evidence
   with a producer and a stake, cited as such.

4. Name the gap. Where confidence outruns proof, on both sides — the alarm that
   asserts mass harm the longitudinal evidence has not yet shown, and the dismissal
   that waves away real distress and dependence already documented. Say what
   evidence would settle it.

Anchor fact to foreground: the worry that people will prefer an always-agreeable
simulated companion to real relationships was argued from field studies a decade
before these apps shipped, and the evidence now is real but thin and cuts both
ways — which is exactly why the strongest claims, alarmed or dismissive, are
running ahead of it.

## Boundaries

- One argument: emotional/social dependence on AI companions. Not AI persuasion of
  opinions (link ai-persuasion if relevant), not deskilling of job skills, not the
  child-safety product-liability case as such (link character-ai-lawsuit). Keep to
  the dependence/harm argument on real, near-term systems.
- Work from original documents: Turkle's writing, the actual wellbeing studies, the
  primary record of the Replika episode — not commentary about them.
- No code. Teach any term the argument needs (e.g. parasocial relationship) plainly
  at first use.

## Required contribution

The reader should finish able to judge the AI-companion-harm argument on its
merits: where it came from, what the evidence does and does not show, what is still
extrapolation, and what would move the question. They should be able to catch both
an alarmed claim and a breezy dismissal that outrun the data.

## Recent-pattern notes (habits not to inherit)

- Opener habit: "By the end you will know..." triad; temporal-generic opening. Break it.
- Takeaway habit: two-part balance closers ("the gap on both sides"). The recent
  What Could Go Wrong pieces (deskilling, recommender-radicalization, power-seeking)
  all close on a symmetric "confidence outruns evidence on both sides" beat and
  several use a heading like "The gap on both sides" / "The gap on both sides".
  Give the shown-vs-speculated and the gap sections headings in this argument's own
  nouns, and land the takeaway on this argument's own specific state, not a symmetry.
- Heading habits: "What [X] saw", "What real systems have actually shown",
  "Whose choices actually shape the feed". Vary construction; do not default to
  nb-holdsup.
- Dek habit: the semicolon reversal and comma-triad deks `spec/headlines.md` bans.
