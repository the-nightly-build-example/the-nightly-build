# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/companion-dependency

## How this piece should sound

This lesson lays out the strongest version of the AI-companion-harm argument,
then checks it against what companion apps and their users actually do.
Thompson's restaurant paragraph is the model for opening on a concrete case
instead of a claim: name the place, the count of bags on the counter, the
people who came and went, and let a reader arrive at the scene before being
told what it means. The same move works for a specific person's use of a
companion app, or a specific exchange with one, ahead of any claim about what
it signifies.

Steelman the argument in language its own defenders would recognize, the way
Yong states the three objections skeptics raised against the replication
crisis before he tests any of them, in the same plain sentences a skeptic
would use. Give the AI-companion-harm case its full form: who first raised
it, what about these products worried them, before turning to what the usage
data and the psychological literature show.

Where the evidence could run in either direction, say so directly rather than
settling on the reading that fits the argument better. Julian does this with
sex and unhappiness: she states the causal direction her data support, then
asks the opposite question in her own voice: "mightn't a decline in sex and
intimacy also be leading to unhappiness?" A heavy user of a companion app
could be lonely because of the app or drawn to the app because already
lonely, and the piece can hold that open the way Yong lets Nosek and
Srivastava read the same non-replicating data two different ways, without
manufacturing a tie-breaker neither of them claims.

A reader who is smart but new to this specific subject doesn't need Julian's
full inventory of candidate causes. She names nineteen in one breath before
her reporting narrows to a handful worth following. This lesson has room for
the two or three explanations the record on companion apps can actually
support, chosen for what holds up rather than for completeness.

Anchor a number that carries the argument, whether a sample size, a rate of
use, or a percentage-point shift, the way Thompson anchors a five-point rise
in alone time to a ten-percent pay cut, so the reader can weigh it against
something they already have a feel for instead of being told only that it is
large or small.

## Derek Thompson, "The Anti-Social Century"

Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2025/02/american-loneliness-personal-technology/681091/

> "A short drive from my home in North Carolina is a small Mexican
> restaurant, with several tables and four stools at a bar facing the
> kitchen. On a sweltering afternoon last summer, I walked in with my wife
> and daughter. The place was empty. But looking closer, I realized that
> business was booming. The bar was covered with to-go food: nine large
> brown bags."

The piece opens on one restaurant, one afternoon, and a literal count of nine
bags, before it makes any claim about the country. Thompson's own presence is
registered only in the physical fact of walking in with his family, not in a
stated opinion about what the empty room means. The reader is given the scene
and reaches the point on their own, one paragraph later.

> "But solitude and loneliness are not one and the same. 'It is actually a
> very healthy emotional response to feel some loneliness,' the NYU
> sociologist Eric Klinenberg told me. 'That cue is the thing that pushes you
> off the couch and into face-to-face interaction.' The real problem here,
> the nature of America's social crisis, is that most Americans don't seem to
> be reacting to the biological cue to spend more time with other people.
> Their solitude levels are surging while many measures of loneliness are
> actually flat or dropping. A 2021 study of the widely used UCLA Loneliness
> Scale concluded that 'the frequently used term "loneliness epidemic" seems
> exaggerated.'"

Thompson separates two words a reader would otherwise treat as
interchangeable, then backs the distinction with a named study that runs
against the more dramatic term. He lets his own source complicate the
argument he is building, loneliness described as a healthy response rather
than a symptom, instead of quoting only the parts that support his frame.

> "But the dosage matters. A night alone away from a crying baby is one
> thing. A decade or more of chronic social disconnection is something else
> entirely. And people who spend more time alone, year after year, become
> meaningfully less happy. In his 2023 paper on the rise of 21st-century
> solitude, Atalay, at the Philadelphia Fed, calculated that by one measure,
> sociability means considerably more for happiness than money does: A
> five-percentage-point increase in alone time was associated with about the
> same decline in life satisfaction as was a 10 percent lower household
> income."

"People who spend more time alone become less happy" could be any sentence
in any piece on this subject. Thompson turns it into a specific trade a
reader can weigh: a five-point rise in alone time against a ten-percent pay
cut. He states plainly, too, that a single night alone and a decade of it are
not the same claim.

## Kate Julian, "Why Are Young People Having So Little Sex?"

Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2018/12/the-sex-recession/573949/

> "Some social scientists take issue with aspects of Twenge's analysis;
> others say that her data source, although highly regarded, is not ideally
> suited to sex research. And yet none of the many experts I interviewed for
> this piece seriously challenged the idea that the average young adult
> circa 2018 is having less sex than his or her counterparts of decades
> past. Nor did anyone doubt that this reality is out of step with public
> perception — most of us still think that other people are having a lot
> more sex than they actually are."

Julian states the specific objections to her central source's methodology in
the same paragraph as the finding itself, rather than burying the caveat in a
footnote or leaving it out. The finding survives the objection because she
shows exactly what the objection does and doesn't touch, which is the mark of
a writer who has actually weighed the two against each other.

> "Over the course of many conversations with sex researchers, psychologists,
> economists, sociologists, therapists, sex educators, and young adults, I
> heard many other theories about what I have come to think of as the sex
> recession. I was told it might be a consequence of the hookup culture, of
> crushing economic pressures, of surging anxiety rates, of psychological
> frailty, of widespread antidepressant use, of streaming television, of
> environmental estrogens leaked by plastics, of dropping testosterone
> levels, of digital porn, of the vibrator's golden age, of dating apps, of
> option paralysis, of helicopter parents, of careerism, of smartphones, of
> the news cycle, of information overload generally, of sleep deprivation,
> of obesity. Name a modern blight, and someone, somewhere, is ready to
> blame it for messing with the modern libido."

Julian lets the list run to nineteen items before she comments on it at all,
so the reader feels the sheer volume of blame on offer before she starts
sorting through it. The closing line earns its humor because it is built
directly out of the list she just gave, not dropped in as a separate joke.

> "The data show that having sex makes people happier (up to a point, at
> least; for those in relationships, more than once a week doesn't seem to
> bring an additional happiness bump). Yet unhappiness inhibits desire, in
> the process denying people who are starved of joy one of its potential
> sources. Are rising rates of unhappiness contributing to the sex
> recession? Almost certainly. But mightn't a decline in sex and intimacy
> also be leading to unhappiness?"

Julian names the causal direction her evidence supports and then, in her own
voice, asks whether the arrow also runs the other way. Both directions are
left standing at the end of the paragraph; she does not pick one to close on.

## Ed Yong, "Psychology's Replication Crisis Is Real, Many Labs 2 Says"

Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/science/archive/2018/11/psychologys-replication-crisis-real/576223/

> "But skeptics have argued that the misleadingly named 'crisis' has more
> mundane explanations. First, the replication attempts themselves might be
> too small. Second, the researchers involved might be incompetent, or lack
> the know-how to properly pull off the original experiments. Third, people
> vary, and two groups of scientists might end up with very different
> results if they do the same experiment on two different groups of
> volunteers."

Yong states three objections to the finding he has just reported, numbered
and in a skeptic's own terms, before he has told the reader which one he
thinks holds up. Nothing in the sentences signals doubt or agreement yet.
That comes later, once the evidence is on the table.

> "It's worth dwelling on this because it's a serious blow to one of the
> most frequently cited criticisms of the 'reproducibility crisis' rhetoric.
> Surely, skeptics argue, it's a fantasy to expect studies to replicate
> everywhere. 'There's a massive deference to the sample,' Nosek says. 'Your
> replication attempt failed? It must be because you did it in Ohio and I
> did it in Virginia, and people are different. But these results suggest
> that we can't just wave those failures away very easily.'"

Yong returns to one specific objection from his own earlier list and shows
exactly what the new data closes off: not the whole debate, just that one
escape hatch. He lets Nosek's own blunt phrasing carry the force of the
point instead of translating it into a more measured sentence of his own.

> "Sanjay Srivastava from the University of Oregon says the lack of
> variation in Many Labs 2 is actually a positive thing. Sure, it suggests
> that the large number of failed replications really might be due to
> sloppy science. But it also hints that the fundamental business of
> psychology — creating careful lab experiments to study the tricky,
> slippery, complicated world of the human mind — works pretty well.
> 'Outside the lab, real-world phenomena can and probably do vary by
> context,' he says. 'But within our carefully designed studies and
> experiments, the results are not chaotic or unpredictable. That means we
> can do valid social-science research.'"

The piece ends on a named researcher's own reading of the same numbers it
has just spent several paragraphs complicating, offered as one useful way to
see the result rather than as Yong's verdict on the field. He does not
resolve the tension between a damning reading and a hopeful one; he shows
both are available from the same data.
