# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/mesa-optimization (01)

## Directive

Write as a teacher walking a smart reader through a live disagreement they cannot
referee themselves. The reader is quick and widely read; what they lack is any way
to check whether the base-objective/mesa-objective worry is a demonstrated fact or a
careful guess. Your whole job is to keep that distinction visible in the prose, not
parked in a caveat at the end.

The move that must change sentences here, beyond the house default: every load-bearing
claim sits visibly on one side of a line, and the sentence shows which side. On one
side, what a working system has already done (a proxy goal learned and pursued off
distribution). On the other, what is still argued about systems that do not exist yet
(a search process, inside the weights, running its own objective). The house standard
tells you to keep fact, estimate, and synthesis distinct. This article needs that
distinction carried at sentence grain, because the disagreement it teaches is almost
entirely about where that line falls.

Hold one temperature. Give the argument its full strength in the words its most careful
defender would use before you test it, and test it in the same even voice you built it
in. A steelman that sounds warmer than the scrutiny that follows has taken a side; so has
scrutiny that sounds relieved. Attribute every claim to a named author and paper. When
you need authority, it is the document's reasoning, never the institution behind it.

## Licenses

form: the fenced intuition pump (the evolution analogy)
move: the exemplars state the analogy plainly to install one specific intuition (natural
  selection optimized for fitness and produced minds that chase sugar and sex, not
  fitness), then in the same passage name the exact respect in which it is only an
  analogy rather than evidence about trained models. The fence is part of the move, not
  a later retraction.
bar:  a sentence using the analogy must either build a named intuition the argument then
  spends, or mark where the analogy stops carrying weight. An analogy sentence that does
  neither is decoration; cut it. The reader must never be able to mistake the analogy for
  a finding about a real network.

form: the in-sentence epistemic marker
move: a short clause that grades the claim it rides on and locates it on the
  demonstrated/analogy line ("has been shown in CoinRun"; "has never been identified in a
  real model"; "is open"). The exemplars vary the verb to signal strength, so entailment
  and judgment call do not wear the same word.
bar:  the marker must change what the reader is entitled to conclude, sorting shown-in-a-
  working-system from argued-about-systems-that-do-not-exist. A reflexive hedge that only
  softens ("it seems," "arguably," "some would say") without placing the claim on that
  line is slop; delete it and state where the claim actually stands.

## Recently used, do not reuse

- The dek mold that pairs a claim with a deflating clause joined by "and"
  (self-replication's "clear the substeps and fail the loop itself"; racing-dynamics'
  "needs a lead nobody has held"). This article's confidence-outruns-proof shape pulls
  hard toward that mold. Do not write the dek that way.
- Section headings built as two clauses joined by a comma and "and." Vary the heading
  shape across the piece.
- Any closing block that restates the finding as a verdict. The takeaway bookend lands
  the judgment; there is no second one.

## Holden Karnofsky, "AI Could Defeat All Of Us Combined"
Source: https://www.cold-takes.com/ai-could-defeat-all-of-us-combined/
Craft:
- cadence: short declarative to set the stake, then a longer sentence to unfold it; the
  reader is anchored before the complexity arrives.
- argument: isolates the conditional from the likelihood and works one at a time ("if
  such an attack happened, it could succeed" before any claim that it would).
- evidence: concrete counts and comparisons the reader already holds; simplifications are
  flagged as simplifications rather than smuggled.
- stance: a guide reasoning alongside the reader, holding the core claim firmly while
  conceding the real counterpoints.
- notice: repeatedly names what he is not claiming, so the reader cannot over-read the
  steelman into a prediction.
- diction: plain and unsensational; avoids the sci-fi register even when the stakes are
  large.
- reader: treated as intelligent and skeptical, never hostile; mechanism is laid before
  urgency.
- the move the axes miss: he lingers deliberately on a single premise instead of racing
  to the conclusion, which is what keeps a frightening argument from reading as a pitch.

## Joseph Carlsmith, "Existential Risk from Power-Seeking AI"
Source: https://jc.gatspress.com/pdf/existential_risk_and_powerseeking_ai.pdf
Craft:
- cadence: measured and even; no superlatives carrying the weight a claim should carry.
- argument: breaks a contested case into explicit steps a reader can accept or reject one
  at a time, so disagreement can be specific rather than total.
- evidence: separates what follows logically from what is a judgment call, and says which
  is which at each step.
- stance: owns his uncertainty in the first person without demanding the reader share it.
- notice: signals the strength of each step through verb choice, so "follows" and
  "plausibly" are never interchangeable.
- diction: restrained; the doubt lives in the grammar, not in decorative hedging.
- reader: invited to inspect the structure at any joint and challenge that joint alone.
- the move the axes miss: fairness performed as transparency about where his confidence
  ends, which is more persuasive than a confident tone would have been.

## Scott Alexander, "Deceptively Aligned Mesa-Optimizers"
Source: https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/deceptively-aligned-mesa-optimizers
Craft:
- cadence: in the explanatory stretches, sentences shorten to subject-verb-object; nested
  ideas get nested syntax only when the idea earns it.
- argument: concentric circles of certainty, from how evolution actually works, to what
  current systems observably are, to what could happen in theory.
- evidence: states the boundary flatly ("as far as we know, there are no existing full
  mesa-optimizers") so speculation cannot be mistaken for observation.
- stance: even between worry and dismissal; a real example is called "kind of" a
  mesa-optimizer and "not a very scary one."
- notice: builds the concept from a worked analogy, then marks where the analogy is a
  spandrel and not a lesson.
- diction: introduces a term of art only after bedding it in a concrete instance (a drive
  as a proxy for its evolutionary purpose) so the word arrives already meaning something.
- reader: the skeptic's position is stated in its own strongest form before it is
  complicated, never mocked.
- the move the axes miss: mechanism first, stakes second; the fear is never allowed to
  carry the argument. Take this craft, not the jokes.

## Note on the exemplars

Two of the three writers are now affiliated with AI labs. Study their craft, not their
authority: this desk forbids naming any company as an authority, and nothing here licenses
leaning on where a writer works. The transferable technique is the sentence-level line
between shown and argued, and the fenced analogy. Neither depends on who employs anyone.

## Self-test

The house default already gives plain claims, concrete stakes, no hype, no doom. What it
does not give, and this article needs, is prose that grades its own certainty as it goes:
a piece where the reader can feel, sentence by sentence, the difference between a proxy
goal demonstrated in CoinRun and an inner optimizer no one has ever found, and where the
evolution analogy is load-bearing and openly fenced in the same breath. That calibrated,
even-handed seam between demonstrated and analogy is the sound this lesson is after.
