# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/automated-escalation

## How this piece should sound

This lesson argues that putting AI into military decision-making could
compress the time and judgment that has kept past crises from tipping into
war, and it has to hold that argument up against what deployed systems
actually do. The three writers below all handle a version of the same
problem: a claim about a catastrophe that has not happened, made by people
worth taking seriously, tested against what is actually known. None of them
resolve that tension by raising their voice or lowering it.

Open the way Kaplan opens. He states the contested claim itself, not a
promise to get to it: "he poses no threat to the United States—or at least no
sort of threat that we can't readily handle." It is a flat, checkable
sentence with no hedge in it. This lesson's own opening claim, that
compressing decision time could produce a war nobody chose, can take the same
directness before a single caveat qualifies it. Geist and Lohn's reasoning,
and the "flash war" framing built on it, can be given whole and straight
before anything pushes back on it.

Freedman's discipline is separating what he knows from what he is worried
about, sentence by sentence, in the same paragraph: "There is no evidence for
now that weapons are being moved into position or being prepared for such
strikes." The commission's central line does the same job. What a working
system has already done in a wargame is one kind of claim; what remains
analogy about a command structure no state has built is another. Freedman's
plainness is the model for holding those two apart: named study, named
result, named absence of the thing being feared.

Schlosser's restraint is in the arithmetic, not the adjectives: "a six-digit
code, with a million possible combinations." This lesson has real figures of
its own. How often, in the 2024 study, an agent escalated to nuclear use. The
exact dates of the 2023 Political Declaration and the November 2024
statement. Any of these can carry a sentence the way Schlosser's number does,
instead of leaving a word like "alarming" to carry it.

All three name who is speaking. Kaplan quotes McMaster by name and title.
Freedman quotes a named Duma member's own intercepted words. This argument
has real names on both sides of the section that brings it to the present:
the officials behind the Political Declaration, the two governments behind
the 2024 statement, the researchers behind the wargame result. The piece can
use them the same way, by name and title, with what they actually said or
found.

None of the three close on a bigger claim than the piece just earned.
Freedman's last move is to say plainly what is not yet known, not to resolve
it toward alarm or toward dismissal. The lesson template's own rule points
the same way: no Verdict block, and the takeaway teaches nothing new. The
takeaway can state what the RAND analysis and the 2024 result actually show,
and where the confidence on either side outruns it, without manufacturing a
verdict past that line.

Schlosser also shows how to carry a term of art. He expands "permissive
action links" into "PALs" in the sentence that first uses it, then never
re-explains it. This lesson holds its own vocabulary a reader new to it will
not already have: automation bias, use-it-or-lose-it, human-in-the-loop,
"flash war" itself. Each can be built up once, in plain words, at first use,
the same way Schlosser earns his abbreviation before he leans on it.

Kaplan explains a mechanism by describing what it takes to work: "Deterrence,
in a sense, is a confidence game. To be effective, you have to convince the
leaders you're trying to deter that, if they step over the line, you really
would do the things you say you'd do." The lesson's own mechanisms, automation
bias and the physics of coupled automated systems interacting faster than a
human can intervene, can be built the same way: a thing that works a
particular way, explained before the piece says what could go wrong with it.

## Fred Kaplan, "Don't Panic About North Korea"

Source: https://slate.com/news-and-politics/2017/09/why-nuclear-deterrence-will-still-work-on-north-korea.html

> "North Korean leader Kim Jong-un is doing a lot of reckless things these days, but he poses no threat to the United States—or at least no sort of threat that we can't readily handle. If he deploys a nuclear-tipped missile with the range to strike U.S. territory (as he'll likely be able to do soon), that will complicate national security policy but in a completely manageable way. It won't mean that he's about to attack some American city—or that we need to attack North Korea pre-emptively."

This is the piece's opening paragraph, and it states the whole argument before defending any of it. The claim is made immediately and it is falsifiable, and the qualifications ("or at least," "in a completely manageable way") sit inside the sentence itself instead of hedging in front of it.

> "Deterrence, in a sense, is a confidence game. To be effective, you have to convince the leaders you're trying to deter that, if they step over the line, you really would do the things you say you'd do. Moscow had to believe Washington would defend Berlin or London as it would defend Boston or New York. Pyongyang has to believe Washington would defend Tokyo or Seoul with the same commitment."

Kaplan defines a term of art by saying what it does, not what it is called. The Cold War comparison arrives as a worked pair of named cities, not as a general reference to history, which is what makes the abstraction ("confidence game") land as something concrete a reader can picture.

> "President Trump is doing a poor job at building up this necessary confidence. Repeatedly he has done what he and others accused President Obama of doing in Syria: He has drawn red lines, then done nothing when they were crossed. He threatened 'fire and fury' against Kim Jong-un if North Korea tested another missile—then did nothing when Kim tested another missile, then another after that. After this happens a couple of times, Kim—quite rationally—will get the idea that he can get away with anything."

The judgment here is pointed, and it is built entirely from named, dated actions: a threat, a missile test, another missile test. "Quite rationally" is doing real work in that sentence. It credits the adversary with reasoning, the harder and more honest read of his behavior.

## Lawrence Freedman, "Going Nuclear"

Source: https://samf.substack.com/p/going-nuclear

> "Supreme leaders achieve their positions and then hold them by controlling events to their advantage. It is natural, therefore, to assume that even when they appear to have lost control, they will find a way to regain it. This assumption is behind the common refrain these days, even heard from people who would dearly like Vladimir Putin to fail, that he will not allow it to happen, that even at this late stage he will find something to do that will turn the tide of the war."

Freedman opens by stating the assumption behind the fear he is about to test, and he credits it to people who want the opposite outcome, not to a strawman. Naming who holds a view and why, before testing it, is what lets the piece disagree later without having invented an opponent first.

> "The problem with a demonstration is that the message may be unclear. It will show that Russia is ready to ignore the strong normative prohibition on any nuclear use yet is still cautious on making the most of the explosive power. When a similar option was discussed in 1945 prior to the decision to target the city of Hiroshima one concern was that while this could show that the US had a new weapon of unprecedented power, and do so without killing large numbers of people, unless the Japanese could see its destructive effects directly it would make no impression on their leadership."

This is from the middle of the piece, where Freedman works through what a Russian nuclear strike would actually have to accomplish. He reaches for a documented historical decision, the 1945 debate over a demonstration shot, and the analogy does analytic work: it clarifies exactly what a demonstration would and would not communicate to an adversary watching it.

> "There is no evidence for now that weapons are being moved into position or being prepared for such strikes. US intelligence, which has been extraordinarily precise so far can be expected to pick up any details (or at least the Russian would need to assume that)."

The closing section states what is not known and why that absence is informative, without converting it into either reassurance or a new warning. The parenthetical is the tell: it flags exactly how much weight the claim can bear, holding the sentence to no more certainty than the evidence supports.

## Eric Schlosser, "Almost Everything in 'Dr. Strangelove' Was True"

Source: https://www.newyorker.com/news/news-desk/almost-everything-in-dr-strangelove-was-true

> "Its plot suggested that a mentally deranged American general could order a nuclear attack on the Soviet Union, without consulting the President. One reviewer described the film as 'dangerous … an evil thing about an evil thing.' Another compared it to Soviet propaganda."

Schlosser opens with the reception the film got, and lets two named reactions carry the reader's sense of how implausible the premise seemed at the time. His own assessment comes later, once the history has done the work of earning it.

> "With great reluctance, Eisenhower agreed to let American officers use their nuclear weapons, in an emergency, if there were no time or no means to contact the President. Air Force pilots were allowed to fire their nuclear anti-aircraft rockets to shoot down Soviet bombers heading toward the United States."

This is from the piece's historical middle section. "With great reluctance" is attributed to a documented state of mind, not asserted as color, and the sentence that follows immediately specifies which weapons, which crews, and which threat. The abstraction never floats free of the mechanism it describes.

> "Today's Category D PALs, installed in the Air Force's hydrogen bombs, are more sophisticated. They require a six-digit code, with a million possible combinations, and have a limited-try feature that disables a weapon when the wrong code is repeatedly entered."

The precision is the whole effect: a six-digit code, a million combinations, a named failure mode. The specificity of the numbers does the work that a word like "advanced" or "robust" would otherwise be asked to do, and this is also the sentence where Schlosser expands his abbreviation (PAL) the first time he needs it.
