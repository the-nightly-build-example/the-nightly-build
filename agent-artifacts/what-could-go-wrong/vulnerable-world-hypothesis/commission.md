## The argument

The Vulnerable World Hypothesis, argued by Nick Bostrom in a 2019 paper of that
name (Global Policy). Picture invention as drawing balls from an urn: most come
out white and useful, some grey and mixed. The hypothesis is that somewhere in
the urn there may be a black ball, a technology that by default devastates the
civilization that pulls it, and that we have not yet drawn one mostly by luck.
The desk's job is to lay the argument out at full strength, test it against what
AI systems have actually been shown to do, and bring it to the present, leaving
the reader to decide how worried to be.

## Angle

Follow the desk's arc. Open the case at full strength, its author named and its
reasoning given as its most careful defender would.

1. The argument, steelmanned. Bostrom's urn, and his worked example of a black
   ball: the counterfactual where building a nuclear weapon had turned out to be
   easy, so the capacity for mass destruction fell into many hands at once. His
   "semi-anarchic default condition," the three features of today's world (no
   reliable way to stop a determined actor, no capable global governance, actors
   with a wide range of motives) that make catastrophe the default if a black
   ball exists. His typology of vulnerabilities and his proposed stabilizations,
   including the drastic ones: preventive policing approaching ubiquitous
   surveillance, and stronger global governance. Give them as he does, downsides
   and all, so the reader sees why serious people take the argument seriously
   before reading a word against it.
2. The sharp line the desk requires, drawn against real systems. What has been
   shown: the exact mechanism the hypothesis fears, a technology lowering the cost
   of mass harm, is now something the field measures directly. AI uplift
   evaluations for bioweapons and for cyberattacks test whether a system lowers
   the barrier, and the measured uplift in current evaluations is limited. Those
   are the empirical anchor. What is still analogy or projection: that any actual
   black ball exists, that AI will be one, and that the drastic remedies would
   work or are warranted. The nuclear case is a thought experiment, not a
   discovered black ball.
3. The present, and the gap on both sides. Who invokes the hypothesis now and
   what they want done (it appears in AI-governance arguments for monitoring,
   licensing, and compute controls, and in global-catastrophic-risk writing), and
   the strongest objections (that the hypothesis cannot be falsified, that its
   surveillance remedy may be worse than the danger, that having drawn no black
   ball is not evidence one exists). Name the gap wherever confidence outruns
   proof, whether the confidence is alarm treating a possibility as near-certain,
   or dismissal treating the clean run so far as proof of safety.
