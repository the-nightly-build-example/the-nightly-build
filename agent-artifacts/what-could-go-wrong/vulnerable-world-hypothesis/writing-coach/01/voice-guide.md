# Voice guide: the Vulnerable World Hypothesis

## How this piece should sound

This is a lesson on the Vulnerable World Hypothesis for a reader who is sharp and widely read but has never met Bostrom's argument. The register is a serious paper explaining something clearly, in the plain, unfussy voice the press asks for: a claim is stated directly, and the stakes are made concrete. The reader should come out able to state the hypothesis, the black-ball thought experiment, and the semi-anarchic default condition on their own, and able to see where the argument is doing real work and where it is reaching.

The first movement asks you to put the argument at full strength while you plainly do not yet know whether it holds. Scott Alexander opening the ivermectin piece by calling its opponents' evidence "the most impressive act of science communication I have ever seen" is the move worth studying: he credits the case he is about to test, specifically and without hedging, and that is what earns the reader's trust when the testing starts. Bostrom's urn and his worked black ball can take that same unhedged presentation before a word of objection.

The urn, the black ball, and the semi-anarchic default condition are abstractions, and a reader meeting them for the first time will hold them only if they are anchored. Lanchester sinking the Yap stone to the bottom of the Pacific, with the ownership surviving the loss, is how an abstraction becomes something the reader can picture; Tufekci's crowded bar with one billionaire in it does the same for a claim about averages. Where the hypothesis turns on an abstraction, a concrete case may carry it further than another sentence of definition, and Bostrom's own worked examples give the piece cases to reach for.

When the lesson draws its line between what AI systems have been shown to do and what is still analogy or projection, the craft is fairness held under skepticism. Alexander crediting a source he has contempt for with a real point, then admitting he "can't prove that even to myself, let alone to you," models both halves at once: be fair to the side you doubt, and be honest about the limits of your own confidence. That holds whether the confidence on the page is alarm treating a possibility as settled or dismissal treating a clean run as proof. Lanchester noticing how bitcoin's own champion stores his keys is a second form of it, testing a claim against what its advocates actually do and letting one detail raise the doubt. The drastic remedies Bostrom proposes, the preventive policing that shades into ubiquitous surveillance, invite exactly that kind of test.

The desk's usual way in is closed to this piece, so it needs its own opening. Tufekci starting on named regions and real death tolls that refuse to line up shows a hook built from specifics rather than a promise about what the reader will learn. And the ending can decline a verdict the way Lanchester's does, setting out the possibilities that are genuinely still open and leaving the reader to decide how worried to be, without reaching for a line that grades the argument instead of ending it.

## Scott Alexander, "Ivermectin: Much More Than You Wanted To Know"

Source: https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/ivermectin-much-more-than-you-wanted

> "Putting aside the question of accuracy and grading only on presentation and scale, this is the most impressive act of science communication I have ever seen. The WHO and CDC get billions of dollars in funding and neither of them has been able to communicate their perspective anywhere near as effectively. Even an atheist can appreciate a cathedral, and even an ivermectin skeptic should be able to appreciate this website."

Alexander is about to spend ten thousand words dismantling the pro-ivermectin case, and he opens by praising its best exhibit without reservation, on its own terms. The admiration is specific and unhedged, and that is what makes his later skepticism read as fair rather than motivated. The person shows in the willingness to say a rival did something better than the institutions he otherwise trusts.

> "On the one hand, I have immense contempt for ivmmeta for letting all those other awful studies pass and then pulling out all the stops to try to nitpick this one. I have no idea if their proposed randomization failure really happened. And no doubt the reason they're even able to investigate this is that this study is really careful and transparent - most of them don't tell you anything about their randomization method."

Here Alexander credits a source he openly despises with a valid point, and grants in the same breath that the point tells against studies he would rather keep. He keeps his judgment of the source and his judgment of the evidence apart, and says so out loud. The person is visible in the refusal to let how he feels about a source decide what it got right.

> "Now it's p = 0.04, seemingly significant, but I had to make some unprincipled decisions to get there. I don't think I specifically replaced negative findings with positive ones, but I can't prove that even to myself, let alone to you."

After grinding through thirty studies, Alexander reports his own result and immediately tells the reader how much of it he manufactured by choosing one defensible method over another. The last clause hands over the exact reason to trust the number less, rather than burying it. The person shows in caring more about being caught out than about looking certain.

## John Lanchester, "When Bitcoin Grows Up"

Source: https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v38/n08/john-lanchester/when-bitcoin-grows-up

> "It has sometimes happened to the Yapese that their boats are hit by stormy weather on the way back from Palau, and to save their own lives, the men have to chuck the big stones overboard. But when they get back to Palau they report what happened, and everyone accepts it, and the ownership of the stone is assigned to whoever quarried it, and the stone can still be used as a valid form of money because ownership can be exchanged even though the actual stone is five miles down at the bottom of the Pacific."

Lanchester's real subject is that money is a register of who owns what, not the physical token, and rather than assert it he sinks the stone five miles into the Pacific and shows the ownership carrying on without it. The abstract point becomes something the reader can picture and check. The person is visible in how much specific detail he is willing to spend before collecting on it.

> "This is one of those times when the people advocating for something end up being unwitting advocates for the other side of the argument. Wences Casares, the Argentine investor described by Popper, is an impressive advocate for bitcoin. And yet this detail stuck with me: he and his co-investors store their bitcoin keys in an offline laptop stored in a safe deposit box. No other form of computer storage is sufficiently secure. If those are the lengths you have to go to in order to defend yourself from crooks, what does that say about the safety of the cryptocurrency?"

Casares is Lanchester's strongest witness for bitcoin, and Lanchester lets him make the case, then notices what the man actually does with his own keys. One concrete habit carries a doubt that a paragraph of argument could not. The person shows in the eye for the telling detail and the restraint to end on a question rather than a verdict.

> "It's time for the cryptocurrency to decide what it wants to be when it grows up. Blockchains could become merely a new technique to ensure the continuation of banking hegemony in its current form. That would be one of those final plot twists which leaves everybody thinking that although they enjoyed most of the show, the ending was so disappointing they now wish they hadn't bothered. Or, along with peer-to-peer lending and mobile payments, they could have an impact as great as the new kind of banking introduced in Renaissance Italy. That would be more fun."

The essay ends on two genuine futures for the technology and picks neither, and the wry aside keeps the refusal from sounding grand. Lanchester has tested bitcoin hard for ten thousand words and still declines to say how it comes out, because he does not know. The person is visible in treating his own uncertainty as the honest ending rather than a failure to conclude.

## Zeynep Tufekci, "This Overlooked Variable Is the Key to the Pandemic"

Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/health/archive/2020/09/k-overlooked-variable-driving-pandemic/616548/

> "If Amazon's CEO, Jeff Bezos, walks into a bar with 100 regular people in it, the average wealth in that bar suddenly exceeds $1 billion. If I also walk into that bar, not much will change. Clearly, the average is not that useful a number to understand the distribution of wealth in that bar, or how to change it. Sometimes, the mean is not the message."

Tufekci needs the reader to see why an average can mislead, and she gets there with a bar, a billionaire, and herself walking in after. The everyday scene does what a formal definition could not, and the idea sticks. The person shows in putting herself into the example rather than keeping the explanation at arm's length.

> "Your friends are, on average, going to have more friends than you. (Sorry!) It's straightforward once you take the network-level view. Friendships are not distributed equally; some people have a lot of friends, and your friend circle is more likely to include those social butterflies, because how could it not? They friended you and others."

She takes a genuinely counterintuitive fact and walks it out one small step at a time, built from pieces the reader already holds, so nothing is asserted that has not just been shown. The tone stays light without going slack. The person is visible in the warmth of that parenthetical apology dropped into the middle of a statistics lesson.

> "Why, for instance, was there such an enormous death toll in northern Italy, but not the rest of the country? Just three contiguous regions in northern Italy have 25,000 of the country's nearly 36,000 total deaths; just one region, Lombardy, has about 17,000 deaths."

Before naming any concept, Tufekci lists real places and real numbers that do not add up, so the reader feels the puzzle instead of being told there is one. The specificity is the hook: exact death tolls in named regions leave a question the reader wants answered. The person shows in trusting concrete facts to raise the curiosity, with no throat-clearing in front of them.
