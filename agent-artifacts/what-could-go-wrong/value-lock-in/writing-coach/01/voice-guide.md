# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/value-lock-in (01)

## How this piece should sound

Before the lesson tests the value lock-in argument, it has to make the reader feel why serious people hold it: the claim that advanced AI could let one set of values, or one power structure, persist indefinitely. Ball's opening shows how to give a large claim its full strength without adopting it. He states the assembly-complexity proposal as "nothing less than a new law of nature", follows its biggest consequence out loud, and lets one conditional, "If they're right", carry all his distance from it. Lay the argument out as strongly as that, with the writer's own verdict kept out of the sentences that present it, so the reader understands the worry before meeting anything against it.

Aaronson draws, for a neighboring technology, the exact line this lesson needs: between what real machines have already done and what is still hope about machines that do not exist yet. He explains in precise terms what a quantum computer can do, marks how few problems actually allow it, and when he reports his own optimism he ties it to a single measured quantity and its history, gate fidelity climbing from 50% to 99.9%, rather than to a mood. The parts about what a model has been trained to hold or defend, and about whether anything like a durable global lock has ever been shown, can hold to that standard: the concrete mechanism, the figure where the evidence supports one, and no confidence past what the shown case actually reaches.

Alexander shows how to weigh a candidate explanation fairly. He states the account he is about to reject, the Baumol story, so that it sounds reasonable first, then checks it against one plain fact and lets that fact settle it. His Netherlands passage handles the harder case, where a claim has to reason past its evidence: he calls his own calculation "completely irresponsible" and makes it anyway, so the estimate and its weakness reach the reader together. When the lesson argues from a historical case of values persisting, or from what a narrow lab result might imply about a permanent worldwide outcome, that is the honest form: flag the shaky step as you take it, and still give the judgment it yields. Where the confidence runs past the proof, toward doom or toward dismissal, saying so plainly is part of writing fairly for a reader new to the argument.

Bostrom's singleton and MacAskill's lock-in chapter invite grand language, and the register holds calm and precise against it. Ball's concession passage shows one way to keep it there under pressure: he lets the theory's own author admit its central weakness, then sets the defense right beside it. Name the people who make this argument and the people who doubt it, quote what they actually wrote, and let those stand for the reader to judge.

## Philip Ball, "Why Everything in the Universe Turns More Complex"

Source: https://www.quantamagazine.org/why-everything-in-the-universe-turns-more-complex-20250402/

> "A new proposal by an interdisciplinary team of researchers challenges that bleak conclusion. They have proposed nothing less than a new law of nature, according to which the complexity of entities in the universe increases over time with an inexorability comparable to the second law of thermodynamics — the law that dictates an inevitable rise in entropy, a measure of disorder. If they’re right, complex and intelligent life should be widespread."

Ball gives the proposal its most ambitious form, a new law of nature on the footing of the second law of thermodynamics, and draws the large consequence out of it, that intelligent life should then be common. The single conditional "If they're right" keeps his own commitment out while letting the reader feel the claim's full pull. He is visible in stating a grand idea at its grandest and marking, in three words, that he has not yet joined it.

> "Hazen acknowledges that, for most physical objects, it is impossible to calculate functional information even in principle. Even for a single living cell, he admits, there’s no way of quantifying it. But he argues that this is not a sticking point, because we can still understand it conceptually and get an approximate quantitative sense of it."

Ball puts the theory's central weakness in the mouth of its own author: Hazen admits the key quantity cannot be calculated even for a single cell. The proponent's reply follows immediately, so the concession and the defense sit side by side for the reader to weigh. Ball is visible in getting the damaging admission from the theory's champion rather than from a critic, which makes it harder to wave away.

## Scott Aaronson, "Quantum Computing: Between Hope and Hype"

Source: https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=8329

> "I think it’s important to tell people that the only hope of getting a speedup from a QC is to exploit the way that QM works differently from classical probability theory — in particular, that it involves these numbers called amplitudes, which can be positive, negative, or even complex. With every quantum algorithm, what you’re trying to do is choreograph a pattern of interference where for each wrong answer, the contributions to its amplitude cancel each other out, whereas the contributions to the amplitude of the right answer reinforce each other. The trouble is, it’s only for a few practical problems that we know how to do that in a way that vastly outperforms the best known classical algorithms."

Aaronson explains the single mechanism behind any quantum speedup, interference among amplitudes, in concrete terms, then closes on its limit: only a few practical problems are known to allow it. The precision is what makes the restraint credible, because he has said exactly what the machine does before saying how little of it carries over. He is visible in declining the sweeping claim his own field is often sold on and naming the narrow case instead.

> "If someone asks me why I’m now so optimistic, the core of the argument is 2-qubit gate fidelities. We’ve known for years that, at least on paper, quantum fault-tolerance becomes a net win (that is, you sustainably correct errors faster than you introduce new ones) once you have physical 2-qubit gates that are ~99.99% reliable. The problem has “merely” been how far we were from that. When I entered the field, in the late 1990s, it would’ve been like a Science or Nature paper to do a 2-qubit gate with 50% fidelity. But then at some point the 50% became 90%, became 95%, became 99%, and within the past year, multiple groups have reported 99.9%."

Asked why he is optimistic, Aaronson gives a single measurable quantity, two-qubit gate fidelity, and walks its value from 50% to 99.9% across the years. The judgment is tied to a number the reader can watch move, and the parenthesis stops to define "net win" in plain terms as he goes. He is visible in answering a question about confidence with one figure and its history.

## Scott Alexander, "Considerations On Cost Disease"

Source: https://slatestarcodex.com/2017/02/09/considerations-on-cost-disease/

> "We could tell a story like this to explain rising costs in education, health care, etc. If technology increases productivity for skilled laborers in other industries, then less susceptible industries might end up footing the bill since they have to pay their workers more.
>
> There’s only one problem: health care and education aren’t paying their workers more; in fact, quite the opposite."

Alexander states the received explanation plainly and grants it a fair hearing, "We could tell a story like this", before he tests it. The rebuttal is a single checkable fact, that these workers are not paid more, and he states it flatly, without turning it into a triumph. He is visible in the patience of setting an explanation up carefully in order to knock it down.

> "It’s going to be completely irresponsible to try to apply this to the increase in health spending in the US over the past 50 years, since this is probably different at every margin and the US is not the Netherlands and the 1950s are not the 2010s. But if we irresponsibly take their median estimate and apply it to the current question, we get that increasing health spending in the US has been worth about one extra year of life expectancy."

Alexander borrows an estimate from a study of the Netherlands, calls applying it to the US "completely irresponsible", and then applies it anyway to reach a figure of about one year of added life expectancy. The value of the passage is that the estimate and its unreliability stay in view together, so the reader can discount it themselves. He is visible in labeling his own inference "irresponsible" out loud instead of hiding the weakness in it.
