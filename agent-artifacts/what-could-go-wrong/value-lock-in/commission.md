# Commission: what-could-go-wrong/value-lock-in

## Assignment

One lesson on a single risk argument: value lock-in, the claim that advanced AI
could let one set of values or one power structure persist indefinitely, removing
the normal churn that has always eventually overturned bad regimes and bad ideas.
What Could Go Wrong opens an argument at full strength, tests it against what real
systems actually do, draws a sharp line between what has been shown and what is
analogy, and brings it to the present. Name no company as an authority; leave the
reader to decide how worried to be. The reader is smart, widely read, new to this
argument.

## Why this argument, now

Value lock-in is a load-bearing premise of longtermist AI-risk writing: if the
future can be locked, then steering the transition to advanced AI dominates other
concerns. It is argued in print by named people today, and it is the kind of claim
that turns entirely on the shown-versus-analogy line the beat exists to draw.

## Angle

Open with the argument as its most careful defender makes it. Trace the origin:
Bostrom's *Superintelligence* (2014) on a "singleton" and the persistence of
values, and William MacAskill's *What We Owe the Future* (2022), whose chapter
makes value lock-in explicit — the mechanism is that AI can remove the reasons
values have always drifted (rulers die, institutions decay, generations replace
their parents' beliefs), because an AI system can hold and enforce a goal without
aging, forgetting, or being persuaded. Give the reader the strongest version:
historical values have persisted for centuries (religions, constitutions) even
without AI, and AI removes the last limits on that persistence.

Then test it. Draw the sharp line: what has been *shown* in a working system is
narrow (models can be trained to hold and defend an objective in-distribution;
they can also be re-trained, jailbroken, and drift — link the paper's related
evidence). Everything about a global, permanent lock is analogy about systems that
do not exist: there is no demonstrated mechanism by which any actor achieves the
durable, worldwide, uncontested control the argument requires, and every historical
"locked" value the argument cites did in fact eventually change. Name the gap in
both directions: the confidence that lock-in is likely outruns the evidence, and
the dismissal that it is impossible also outruns what we can show, because the
enforcement technologies (persuasion, surveillance, autonomous control) are
improving.

Bring it to the present: who argues it now (longtermist researchers and
organizations, as individuals and named works, not "the AI community"), what they
want done (prioritizing the governance of the AI transition, preserving option
value / avoiding premature commitment), and what the most recent evidence supports.

## What to teach (short, complete)

1. The argument at full strength, with its origin named (Bostrom's singleton;
   MacAskill's lock-in chapter) and the mechanism stated plainly: AI removes the
   causes of value drift.
2. The historical premise the argument leans on (values persist a long time) and
   its honest reading (they persist, but they have always eventually changed).
3. The shown-vs-analogy line: what current systems demonstrate about holding an
   objective, and why none of it demonstrates a durable global lock. Link related
   published evidence (goal-misgeneralization, deceptive-alignment, reward-tampering,
   the-off-switch) instead of re-arguing it.
4. The present: named proponents and what they ask for, and where confidence
   (either direction) outruns proof.

## Boundaries and non-overlap

- Distinguish value lock-in sharply from what-could-go-wrong/gradual-disempowerment
  (humans incrementally losing influence as AI runs the economy, culture, and
  state, per Kulveit et al. 2025). Value lock-in is about the *permanence /
  irreversibility* of whatever values win, and the actor may be human. Require a
  Background link and one clear sentence of contrast; do not re-tell that argument.
- Also distinct from vulnerable-world-hypothesis (Bostrom's black-ball technology
  and the surveillance cure) and from intelligence-explosion / takeoff-speeds.
  Link where the reader would benefit; do not overlap.
- Work from the original documents (Bostrom, MacAskill, and the primary papers for
  the empirical points), never the commentary about them.

## Source policy

Lesson in What Could Go Wrong: at least 8 sources, at least 4 primary and at least
1 secondary. Primary: *Superintelligence* (relevant chapter), *What We Owe the
Future* (the lock-in chapter), the primary alignment papers cited for what systems
actually do, and any primary source for a named proponent's present-day position.
Secondary: careful critique of the argument (kept secondary), used to steelman the
opposing view before weighing it.

## Habits to avoid (break these, from the recent record)

- Recent What Could Go Wrong deks name the arguer and the claim ("Bostrom says only
  luck has kept us..."; "Soares warns that..."). That is the house shape and fine,
  but do not copy a neighbor's exact construction; and the vulnerable-world lesson
  already used "Bostrom says...", so do not open the dek the same way even though
  Bostrom is again in view. Lead with the concrete claim in this argument's terms.
- Note the banned term `load-bearing` (max 0): name the dependency plainly.
- Furniture: a position-card or holds-up grid contrasting shown vs analogy could
  help, but only if it changes understanding; do not stack blocks.
