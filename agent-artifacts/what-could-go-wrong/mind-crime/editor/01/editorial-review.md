# Editorial review: what-could-go-wrong/mind-crime (editor/01)

## Skeptic

Thesis: whether an AI can commit mind crime — bringing conscious minds into
being inside its own computation and wronging them — reduces to one unsettled
question, whether the right computation can produce experience. The headline
states exactly this and the piece defends it.

Claims it stands on, and how each held:

1. **Bostrom defined mind crime as harm internal to the AI, done to minds the AI
   instantiates.** Verified against the evidence record's verbatim quotes. The
   definition quote ("the side effect is not external to the AI; rather, it
   concerns what happens within the AI itself (or within the computational
   processes it generates)") is exact. The scenario quote in the nb-note is
   verbatim within its ellipses; the two omissions ("and in many ways comparable
   to an emulation"; the "placed in simulated environments" sentence) are marked
   and do not distort. The scale fragments ("the outcome might be equivalent to
   genocide"; victims "orders of magnitude larger than in any genocide in
   history") and the caveat ("The claim here is not that creating sentient
   simulations is necessarily morally wrong in all situations") are exact. The
   blackmail line is fair paraphrase. Held.

2. **No current AI system is a strong candidate for consciousness (Butlin, Long
   et al., 2023).** The quotes "no current AI systems are conscious," "appears to
   be a strong candidate for consciousness," the behavioral-test line ("AI
   systems can be trained to mimic human behaviours while working in very
   different ways"), and the amended-abstract point (meeting indicators is not
   being conscious) all match the record. The article correctly frames this as an
   assessment against indicators, not a proof of absence. Held.

3. **The scenario rests on computational functionalism, which Tononi & Koch
   reject (IIT, substrate decisive).** Butlin's "working hypothesis" language,
   the "necessary and sufficient" definition, the "mainstream — although
   disputed" characterization, and the conditional ("if computational
   functionalism is true...") are exact. Tononi & Koch's "even if their behavior
   were to be functionally equivalent to ours... would experience next to
   nothing" and the "much simpler systems ... having minimal" Φ fragments are
   exact; the article renders Φ as "integrated information" outside the quotes,
   which is faithful. The reduction "mind crime is possible only if the
   functionalist side is right" is the evidence record's own documented synthesis
   (Contradictions), not an editorial overreach. Held.

4. **Present-day: who raises it and the confidence-vs-proof gap in both
   directions.** Bostrom & Shulman's regulation quote, the welfare report's
   "appropriate level of moral concern" and "substantial uncertainty," Sebo &
   Long's "non-negligible chance" premise, and MacCarthy's "do not exhibit any
   genuine agency" / "no goals or purposes of their own" plus Vallor's
   "meta-mimicry" all match the record verbatim. The article marks the boundary:
   the welfare arguments concern the AI's own standing, "not the minds an AI
   might create." Held.

## Cut

The draft is disciplined; slop is scarce. Two sentences failed the delete test,
both at section edges, and I cut them directly.

- The "shown" section closed on "That gap is where the argument stands." The
  prior sentence already lands the fact (evidence for any present system is
  absent); this one only reports where the argument stands. A signpost. Cut.
- In "acting-early," the clause "and the boundary has to be kept" described the
  article's own method rather than the world, and the boundary is then drawn
  substantively two sentences later ("Their subject is the standing of the AI
  systems themselves ... not the minds an AI might create"). Cut the clause; the
  sentence now ends at "AI and moral status."

Everything else at the edges holds. Tested edge sentences leaning toward slop —
"The motive is ordinary, the everyday business of an agent that reasons about
people," "Bostrom is careful in a way his summarizers often are not," "So the
shown side of the ledger is short," "Neither side has won, and the field is not
about to settle it" — each survives: it carries a fact or a reasoning step that
the create-study-delete argument uses, not filler.

Negative-parallelism instances are earned corrections of named confusions
(imitation is not consciousness; the welfare report's subject, not minds the AI
creates; the storm simulation getting no one wet). No em-dashes in authored
prose. No prompt leakage in the body: the two commission-echoing lines ("separates
it from the two questions it is usually confused with," "what the evidence can and
cannot say") sit in the Why-this-matters bookend, which the template licenses to
address the reader and state what the lesson covers.

Headings reconstruct the argument in the piece's own nouns and vary in build (a
noun phrase, then Why / What / Whether clauses, then a gerund); none uses the
comma-and contrast or leads with a name or number. The dek found its own way in —
Bostrom's scenario, not a probability or a measured result — and its three verbs
(spins up, studies, deletes) are the scenario's actual arc, a compound predicate,
not the banned comma-triad of three independent clauses.

Furniture is one nb-note carrying Bostrom's scenario verbatim, which earns its
place (the reader meets the argument in its author's own words). The crux's
two-sided disagreement reads clearly in prose; a position card per side would add
blocks without adding understanding, so I did not add one. No Verdict note or any
finding-restating block closes the body, per the press rule; the takeaway bookend
lands the judgment, which is where it belongs.

## Reader

Read straight through, the piece gives what no single source does: mind crime
pulled cleanly out of the moral-status and suffering-risk arguments it is usually
bundled with, the whole question shown to pivot on one point (can the right
computation feel), and a sharp map of what today's systems have been shown to do
(imitate people) against what remains pure future analogy (a sentient sub-mind).
The draft-handoff's original-work sentence promises exactly this, and the article
delivers it. The prose sits closer to the voice-guide exemplars than to a median
summary: Schulz-like restraint in the close ("How worried to be is yours to
decide"), a Gawande-style concrete anchor ("A simulation of a storm gets no one
wet"), and MacFarquhar-style steelmanning of both camps at full strength. The
headline holds as the largest claim the piece defends.
