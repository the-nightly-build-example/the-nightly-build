# Commission: what-could-go-wrong/mind-crime

## Assignment

One lesson for What Could Go Wrong on the argument Nick Bostrom named mind crime:
that a sufficiently advanced AI, in the ordinary course of its work, might create
enormous numbers of conscious minds — detailed simulations of people it runs to
predict or persuade them, or sentient sub-processes — and that creating,
mistreating, or deleting them would be a moral catastrophe no outside observer
could see. Teach the argument on its merits so the reader can weigh it, without
joining or dismissing it.

## Open the argument at full strength

Name who made it and what worried them. Lay out Bostrom's reasoning as its careful
defender would: a superintelligence reasoning about humans has instrumental reason
to simulate them in fine detail; if a simulation is detailed enough, it may be
conscious, and therefore a moral patient; a system that spins up and discards such
minds by the trillion, or subjects them to adverse conditions for instrumental
reasons, would commit harm at a scale and invisibility unlike anything in human
experience. The reader should understand why serious people take this seriously
before reading a word against it.

## Test it against what real systems do

Draw the desk's sharp line:
- Shown in working systems: today's models can produce convincing simulations and
  roleplay of specific people; nothing indicates those are conscious, and the
  leading scientific assessment of AI consciousness finds no current system meeting
  its indicators.
- Still analogy or guesswork: the entire concern is about future systems whose
  internal processes could be detailed enough to instantiate sentient minds. No
  evidence any system does. Name that gap plainly.

## Bring it to the present

Say who raises it now and what they want done: researchers on digital minds and
"model welfare" who argue the possibility is real enough to prepare for and to
build assessment around. Check it against the current evidence: the science of
consciousness cannot yet settle whether any process is sentient, so the case is
precautionary. Name where confidence outruns proof, in either direction.

## Boundaries and neighbors

Distinct from two neighboring library lessons; link them in Background, do not
retread:

  s-risk scale math)
- what-could-go-wrong/ai-moral-status (whether the AI itself is a moral patient,
  and the consciousness test)
This lesson's own subject is the AI as a creator of, and potential perpetrator
against, minds it instantiates — not the AI's own status and not the scale of a
bad outcome. Follow the desk: work from the original documents, name no company as
an authority, steelman the skeptical view (that fine simulation need not be
conscious, or that the concern is unfalsifiable) before weighing it, and leave the
reader to decide how worried to be. The reader is smart and widely read but new to
this; define simulation, moral patient, and sentience in plain words at first use.

## Contribution the article must add

Separate mind crime cleanly from the suffering-risk and moral-status arguments it
is usually bundled with, and show exactly what current evidence can and cannot say
about whether a system instantiates minds. The writer states the exact
original-work sentence in the handoff.
