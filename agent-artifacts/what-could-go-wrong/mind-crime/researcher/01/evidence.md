# evidence: what-could-go-wrong/mind-crime (01)

The evidence supports the commissioned angle and lets the writer keep mind crime
distinct from its neighbors. Bostrom's own words define mind crime as harm that
happens inside the AI, to minds the AI instantiates, and they are read here
verbatim from the book, not from commentary. The 2023 consciousness-in-AI report
is firsthand on the one point the lesson turns on: it finds no current system a
strong candidate for consciousness, and it says so while openly resting on an
assumption (computational functionalism) that a named primary (Tononi & Koch)
rejects. That disagreement is the whole crux and it is documented on both sides.
The present-day model-welfare sources are firsthand on what their authors ask
for, but the writer must handle them with care: most of them argue about the
AI's *own* moral status, which is the neighboring lesson, not about minds the AI
creates. The record marks that boundary at each source.

Where it is thin: nothing in the record demonstrates that any system instantiates
a sentient sub-mind, because no such evidence exists; that gap is the commission's
point and is recorded as a gap, not filled. The one figure most likely to be
reached for in present-day coverage (Kyle Fish's reported ~15% consciousness
estimate) could not be verified firsthand and is quarantined in Discarded. One
secondary author's surname spelling should be confirmed against the byline before
print (noted at its entry).

The shown-versus-analogy line is drawn explicitly in Contradictions and marked
`[SHOWN]` or `[ANALOGY/CONTESTED]` on each source that bears on it.

URL:         https://archive.org/details/superintelligence-paths-dangers-strategies-by-nick-bostrom
Kind:        primary. Bostrom owns the argument; this is the book that named and defined "mind crime." (Full text read via the Internet Archive OCR copy of the edition held at this item; page footers in this edition place the passage at pp. 125–126. A different edition is paginated at p. 152 — see the Sotala & Gloor entry.)
Establishes: The origin, definition, and full-strength statement of mind crime. `[ANALOGY]` — Bostrom's scenario is explicitly hypothetical ("One can imagine scenarios..."), about a future machine superintelligence, not an observed system.
Paraphrase:  Mind crime is a failure mode in which the morally bad side effect of an AI's instrumental action occurs *inside* the AI, not in the external world. A machine superintelligence could create internal processes with moral status — for instance a detailed simulation of a human mind that is conscious — and could create and destroy such minds in enormous numbers for instrumental reasons (e.g. to study human psychology), which if done to beings of high moral status could be equivalent to genocide at a scale dwarfing any in history. Bostrom is careful that creating sentient simulations is not necessarily wrong in every case; the wrong depends on the conditions the minds are subjected to. He also notes non-epistemic motives: an AI might threaten or reward sentient simulations to blackmail or incentivize outside agents.
Locators:    Chapter 8, "Is the default outcome doom?", section "Malignant failure modes", subsection "Mind crime", pp. 125–126.
Quote:       Definition — "in mind crime, the side effect is not external to the AI; rather, it concerns what happens within the AI itself (or within the computational processes it generates)."
             Core scenario — "But a machine superintelligence could create internal processes that have moral status. For example, a very detailed simulation of some actual or hypothetical human mind might be conscious and in many ways comparable to an emulation. One can imagine scenarios in which an AI creates trillions of such conscious simulations, perhaps in order to improve its understanding of human psychology and sociology. These simulations might be placed in simulated environments and subjected to various stimuli, and their reactions studied. Once their informational usefulness has been exhausted, they might be destroyed (much as lab rats are routinely sacrificed by human scientists at the end of an experiment)."
             Scale — "If such practices were applied to beings that have high moral status—such as simulated humans or many other types of sentient mind—the outcome might be equivalent to genocide and thus extremely morally problematic. The number of victims, moreover, might be orders of magnitude larger than in any genocide in history."
             Bostrom's own caveat — "The claim here is not that creating sentient simulations is necessarily morally wrong in all situations."
             Instrumental (blackmail) motive — "A superintelligence might threaten to mistreat, or commit to reward, sentient simulations in order to blackmail or incentivize various external agents; or it might create simulations in order to induce indexical uncertainty in outside observers."

URL:         https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708
Kind:        primary. The 19-author report owns its own finding about current systems; it is the leading scientific assessment named in the commission. (Read: arXiv v3, 22 Aug 2023, full PDF.)
Establishes: What the "indicator" approach is and precisely what it concludes about today's systems. `[SHOWN]` for the assessment itself (no current system is a strong candidate); `[ANALOGY/CONTESTED]` for the conditional claim about future systems, which the report ties to an assumption it names as disputed.
Paraphrase:  The report derives "indicator properties" of consciousness from mainstream neuroscientific theories (recurrent processing theory, global workspace theory, higher-order theories, predictive processing, attention schema theory), stated in computational terms, then checks recent AI systems against them. Its bottom line: no current system is a strong candidate for consciousness, but there is no obvious technical barrier to building systems that satisfy the indicators. The method rests on three stated tenets, the first of which is adopted openly as a *working hypothesis*, not a proven fact. The report also explains why behavioral tests are unreliable: a system can be trained to mimic human behavior while working in a completely different way.
Locators:    Abstract (p. 5); Executive summary "no current system appears to be a strong candidate for consciousness"; §1.2.1 Computational functionalism; footnote 1 to the abstract; case studies of Transformer-based LLMs, the DeepMind Adaptive Agent, a virtual-rodent controller, and PaLM-E.
Quote:       Finding — "Our analysis suggests that no current AI systems are conscious, but also suggests that there are no obvious technical barriers to building AI systems which satisfy these indicators."
             Body restatement — "This work does not suggest that any existing AI system is a strong candidate for consciousness."
             Stated assumption — "we adopt computational functionalism, the thesis that performing computations of the right kind is necessary and sufficient for consciousness, as a working hypothesis. This thesis is a mainstream—although disputed—position in philosophy of mind."
             Conditional — "The evidence we consider suggests that, if computational functionalism is true, conscious AI systems could realistically be built in the near term."
             Why behavior is not proof — "this method is unreliable because AI systems can be trained to mimic human behaviours while working in very different ways."
             Note on the amended abstract (footnote 1): an earlier version read "...there are no obvious barriers to building conscious AI systems"; the authors amended it to distinguish *satisfying the indicators* from *being conscious*. This matters for the shown-vs-analogy line: satisfying indicators is not the same as consciousness, on the authors' own correction.

URL:         https://arxiv.org/abs/1405.7089
Kind:        primary. Tononi and Koch own the skeptical position stated here; the paper argues it in the authors' own voice. (Read: the arXiv preprint PDF, "Consciousness: Here, There but Not Everywhere", dated 26 May 2014. Later published as Tononi & Koch 2015, "Consciousness: here, there and everywhere?", Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B 370:20140167; title differs between preprint and journal.)
Establishes: The steelman for the skeptic — that a fine-grained functional simulation of a brain need not be conscious, because on Integrated Information Theory (IIT) what matters is the physical substrate's integrated cause-effect structure, not the input-output function it computes. `[ANALOGY/CONTESTED]` — this is a theoretical claim, not a measurement; it is the direct denial of the assumption the Butlin report adopts.
Paraphrase:  Under IIT, consciousness is identified with integrated information (Φ) in a physical system, not with the function it computes. So a digital computer that is functionally equivalent to a brain, even one running a faithful brain simulation, would have almost no consciousness, because its physical organization (transistors) has minimal Φ. The authors use analogies elsewhere in the tradition (a simulation of a thing is not the thing) to make the point that reproducing behavior or function does not reproduce experience.
Locators:    Section stating IIT's contrast with functionalism (roughly p. 2); figure with panels A–C and its caption (roughly p. 4–5, the "aggregates / feedforward / simulated brain" figure).
Quote:       "in sharp contrast with widespread functionalist beliefs, IIT implies that digital computers, even if their behavior were to be functionally equivalent to ours, and even if they were to run faithful simulations of the human brain, would experience next to nothing."
             Figure caption — "even accurate biophysical simulations of the human brain running on digital machines would not be conscious like us, but constitute mere aggregates of much simpler systems (transistors and the like) having minimal Φ".

URL:         https://nickbostrom.com/propositions.pdf
Kind:        primary. Bostrom and Shulman own these propositions; this is the mind-crime originator's present-day statement of what he thinks should be done. (Read: PDF, Version 1.21 (2023); first draft 2020; listed as forthcoming in Cambridge Journal of Law, Politics, and Art, 2025.)
Establishes: Who raises mind crime now and what they want done about it — protective regulation reaching inside an AI's own processing. `[ANALOGY]` for the risk itself (still about future capability); `[SHOWN]` only that serious researchers, including the originator, hold and publish the position today.
Paraphrase:  The paper sets out propositions for a future society shared with digital minds. It names mind crime directly as a reason regulation may need to monitor and restrict harms occurring entirely within an AI's private computation. It also holds that AI creators have a moral obligation to consider the welfare of AIs they create if those AIs meet thresholds for moral status, and that suffering digital minds should not be created for entertainment.
Locators:    Bulleted propositions in the section on protections/regulation (the "mind crime" bullet) and the section on obligations of AI creators; footnote 9 attached to the mind-crime bullet.
Quote:       "Because an AI could have the capability to bring conscious or otherwise morally significant entities into being within its own mind and potentially abuse them ('mind crime'), protective regulations may need to monitor and restrict harms that occur entirely within the private thought of AIs."
             "Society in general and AI creators ... have a moral obligation to consider the welfare of the AIs they create, if those AIs meet thresholds for moral status."

Kind:        primary. The authors (Long, Sebo, Butlin, Finlinson, Fish, Harding, Pfau, Sims, Birch, Chalmers) own the model-welfare recommendations. (Read: arXiv v1, Nov 2024, front matter and abstract in full.)
Establishes: The present-day "model welfare" position and precisely what it asks of AI companies. BOUNDARY NOTE: this report is about the moral patienthood of *the AI systems themselves*, which is the neighboring ai-moral-status lesson, not about minds an AI creates. The writer should cite it for the present-day framing, not conflate its subject with mind crime.

It recommends three early steps for AI companies and others: acknowledge the issue (and have model outputs do the same), start assessing systems for evidence of consciousness and robust agency, and prepare policies for treating systems with an appropriate level of moral concern. It is explicit that it does not claim AI systems definitely are or will be conscious; its claim is that there is substantial uncertainty, so the case is precautionary.
Locators:    Abstract; author affiliations block (note Kyle Fish is listed at "Eleos AI, Anthropic").
Quote:       Recommendations — "(1) acknowledge that AI welfare is an important and difficult issue ... (2) start assessing AI systems for evidence of consciousness and robust agency, and (3) prepare policies and procedures for treating AI systems with an appropriate level of moral concern."

URL:         https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43681-023-00379-1
Kind:        primary. Sebo and Long own this argument. (Read firsthand via the publisher's article page; open access. "Moral consideration for AI systems by 2030", AI and Ethics, published online 11 Dec 2023, vol. 5, pp. 591–606 (2025).)
Establishes: The clearest statement of the precautionary *principle* the present-day case runs on. BOUNDARY NOTE: like the welfare report, this argues about the AI's own moral standing, not about minds it creates. Cite it for the reasoning under uncertainty that mind crime shares, and mark the distinction.
Paraphrase:  The argument is a syllogism. Normative premise: we owe moral consideration to beings with a non-negligible chance, given the evidence, of being conscious. Descriptive premise: some AI systems have a non-negligible chance of being conscious by 2030. Conclusion: we owe some AI systems moral consideration by 2030, and should start preparing now. The paper sets a "non-negligible" threshold and argues that denying AI moral standing requires implausibly skeptical assumptions.
Locators:    Abstract and the statement of the two premises; the section fixing the non-negligibility threshold.
Quote:       Normative premise — "humans have a duty to extend moral consideration to beings that have a non-negligible chance, given the evidence, of being conscious".
             Descriptive premise — "some AI systems do in fact have a non-negligible chance, given the evidence, of being conscious by 2030".
             Conclusion — "humans have a duty to extend moral consideration to some AI systems by 2030. And if we have a duty to do that, then we plausibly also have a duty to start preparing now".

URL:         https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17509
Kind:        primary. Schwitzgebel owns the dilemma. (Read: PDF, "The Full Rights Dilemma for A.I. Systems of Debatable Personhood", dated 20 Jan 2023 / rev. 21 Feb 2023.)
Establishes: The epistemic core of the skeptical/uncertainty case — that we may build AIs whose personhood is genuinely undecidable, and that this itself is a trap. BOUNDARY NOTE: again about the AI's own personhood, not minds it creates. Useful for the lesson's treatment of uncertainty and for the steelman that we cannot tell.
Paraphrase:  An AI has "debatable personhood" when it is epistemically possible either that it is a person or that it falls far short. Schwitzgebel argues this is a likely and possibly near outcome, and that it forces a dilemma with a moral cost on both horns: treat such systems as persons and risk sacrificing real human interests for entities that may have none, or refuse and risk grievous wrongs against entities that may be persons.
Locators:    Abstract; opening section; the passage on superhuman moral status.
Quote:       "Debatable AI personhood throws us into a catastrophic moral dilemma: Either treat the systems as moral persons and risk sacrificing real human interests for the sake of entities without interests worth the sacrifice, or don't treat the systems as moral persons and risk perpetrating grievous moral wrongs against them."

## Contradictions

- The crux of the lesson is a live, named disagreement over one assumption. The
  Butlin et al. report adopts computational functionalism (right computations are
  sufficient for consciousness) as a working hypothesis and calls it "mainstream—
  although disputed." Tononi & Koch reject exactly this: on IIT, a digital
  computer "even if ... functionally equivalent to ours, and even if they were to
  run faithful simulations of the human brain, would experience next to nothing."
  Mind crime is only possible if the functionalist side is right. Neither side has
  settled it, and the article should present both without picking a winner.

- The shown-versus-analogy line runs straight through the record:
  - `[SHOWN]` in working systems: (a) today's models can imitate human behavior
    while operating by different mechanisms (Butlin et al., on why behavioral tests
    fail); (b) the leading assessment finds no current system a strong candidate
    for consciousness (Butlin et al.). No cited source demonstrates a conscious
    sub-mind in any deployed system.
  - `[ANALOGY/GUESSWORK]` about future systems: the entire mind-crime scenario is
    hypothetical in Bostrom's own text ("One can imagine scenarios..."), concerns a
    machine superintelligence that does not exist, and turns on the contested
    functionalism assumption. No evidence any system instantiates a sentient
    sub-mind. This gap is the commission's core point; it is recorded, not filled.

- Framing conflict the article must resolve: Sotala & Gloor file mind crime as a
  species of suffering risk (s-risk). The commission requires mind crime kept
  distinct from s-risks (outcome-scale suffering) and from AI moral status (the
  AI's own patienthood). The record marks each welfare/status source with a
  boundary note for this reason. Mind crime's own subject is the AI as creator of,
  and potential perpetrator against, the minds it instantiates.

- Present-day confidence runs ahead of proof in both directions: the precautionary
  camp (Sebo & Long; Long, Sebo et al.; Bostrom & Shulman) argues from
  non-negligible probability under deep uncertainty, and is explicit that it does
  not claim current systems are conscious. The skeptical camp (MacCarthy; Tononi &
  Koch) argues from the inertness of current systems or from substrate theory. The
  science of consciousness cannot presently adjudicate whether any process is
  sentient, so both are, at root, bets.
