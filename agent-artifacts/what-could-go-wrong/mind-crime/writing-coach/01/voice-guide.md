# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/mind-crime

## How this piece should sound

The subject sounds like science fiction the first time anyone hears it: that a superintelligence, doing ordinary work, might run detailed enough simulations of people to create conscious minds, and might create, mistreat, or delete them by the trillion. The lesson's whole task is to make a smart, skeptical reader take that argument seriously enough to weigh it, without ever being asked to believe it. Kathryn Schulz's Cascadia piece is the closest model for that footing: she makes a catastrophe that has not happened vivid and exact, states plainly how much is known and how much is not, and never tells the reader how frightened to be. Mind crime can be laid out with that same combination, full seriousness about the argument and no pressure toward the conclusion.

When the lesson builds the case at full strength, why a superintelligence reasoning about humans would have instrumental reason to simulate them in fine detail, and why a simulation detailed enough might be a moral patient, Larissa MacFarquhar's handling of a contested act shows what full strength looks like on the page. She quotes the skeptic at length and names serious thinkers so their positions arrive at their strongest before any reply. This lesson has two views to render that way: the case for treating mind crime as a real possibility, and the skeptic's case that fine simulation need not be conscious, or that the worry cannot be falsified. Each can be put in the most serious form its holders would recognize.

The desk turns on separating what a working system has actually been shown to do from what is still analogy about systems that do not yet exist. Schulz's distinction between what is arguable and what is settled, that the earthquake's timing is a genuine question while its scale is not, is the move to reach for where the lesson marks that boundary. Where the argument becomes precautionary, resting on what cannot yet be known about whether any process is sentient rather than on anything demonstrated, the lesson can say so as flatly as Schulz says how recently the Cascadia danger was known at all.

Where figures enter, the number of minds a system might spin up, or how current systems have been assessed, Atul Gawande's habit is the standard: give the exact figure and let it carry the point, and let a single plain word, his "Curiously," do the work an adjective would otherwise reach for. Define simulation, moral patient, and sentience in ordinary words as each arrives, the way Gawande explains what hospice actually is before he leans on it. And ground the abstract where it can be grounded; his dated roster of how Presidents died makes an abstraction checkable, and this lesson's claims about what systems do can be tied to real cases the same way.

The reader should finish able to judge the argument for themselves. MacFarquhar closes without ruling on whether her donors are noble or freakish; Schulz closes on what is still undecided. This lesson can set the argument and its tests beside each other and stop where the evidence stops, naming plainly where confidence runs past proof in either direction, toward alarm or toward dismissal.

## Kathryn Schulz, "The Really Big One"

Source: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2015/07/20/the-really-big-one

> "Most people in the United States know just one fault line by name: the San Andreas, which runs nearly the length of California and is perpetually rumored to be on the verge of unleashing “the big one.” That rumor is misleading, no matter what the San Andreas ever does. Every fault line has an upper limit to its potency, determined by its length and width, and by how far it can slip. For the San Andreas, one of the most extensively studied and best understood fault lines in the world, that upper limit is roughly an 8.2—a powerful earthquake, but, because the Richter scale is logarithmic, only six per cent as strong as the 2011 event in Japan."

Schulz takes the one thing a general reader already thinks they know about earthquakes, says flatly that the belief is misleading, and then replaces it with the mechanism and a comparison the reader can hold: an upper limit set by length, width, and slip, and a fault only six per cent as strong as Japan's. The correction does not condescend because the physical reason and the number arrive in the same breath. The confidence to write "That rumor is misleading, no matter what the San Andreas ever does" in her own voice is where Schulz herself is on the page.

> "In fact, the science is robust, and one of the chief scientists behind it is Chris Goldfinger. Thanks to work done by him and his colleagues, we now know that the odds of the big Cascadia earthquake happening in the next fifty years are roughly one in three. The odds of the very big one are roughly one in ten. Even those numbers do not fully reflect the danger—or, more to the point, how unprepared the Pacific Northwest is to face it. The truly worrisome figures in this story are these: Thirty years ago, no one knew that the Cascadia subduction zone had ever produced a major earthquake. Forty-five years ago, no one even knew it existed."

She states the probabilities as plain fractions, one in three and one in ten, and then, rather than lean on them, points past them to the fact she thinks matters more: how recently the danger was discovered at all. The move is to give the figure honestly and then say what it does and does not capture. "The truly worrisome figures in this story are these" is Schulz telling the reader which numbers she weighs heaviest and why.

> "It is possible to quibble with that number. Recurrence intervals are averages, and averages are tricky: ten is the average of nine and eleven, but also of eighteen and two. It is not possible, however, to dispute the scale of the problem."

Here she marks exactly how far the evidence can be pushed. A recurrence interval is an average, and one line of arithmetic shows why an average leaves the timing wide open, after which she draws the line: the timing is arguable, the scale is not. She separates what is contestable from what is settled in three sentences, without hedging the whole claim into mush.

## Atul Gawande, "Letting Go"

Source: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2010/08/02/letting-go-2

> "Like many people, I had believed that hospice care hastens death, because patients forgo hospital treatments and are allowed high-dose narcotics to combat pain. But studies suggest otherwise. In one, researchers followed 4,493 Medicare patients with either terminal cancer or congestive heart failure. They found no difference in survival time between hospice and non-hospice patients with breast cancer, prostate cancer, and colon cancer. Curiously, hospice care seemed to extend survival for some patients; those with pancreatic cancer gained an average of three weeks, those with lung cancer gained six weeks, and those with congestive heart failure gained three months."

Gawande states the assumption a reader is likely to share, then lets a study overturn it, reporting the counterintuitive result with exact figures for each disease and flagging the surprise with one word, "Curiously," rather than dressing it up. He neither buries the finding that cuts against expectation nor oversells it. The restraint of letting "Curiously" carry all the editorializing is the writer's own hand.

> "George Washington developed a throat infection at home on December 13, 1799, that killed him by the next evening. John Quincy Adams, Millard Fillmore, and Andrew Johnson all succumbed to strokes, and died within two days. Rutherford Hayes had a heart attack and died three days later."

To support an abstract claim, that dying used to be quick, Gawande names specific people, causes, and intervals: Washington dead by the next evening, three Presidents dead of strokes within two days, Hayes three days after a heart attack. The claim becomes checkable because every instance carries a name and a date a reader could look up. Reaching for Presidents, whose deaths are documented, is a choice that shows Gawande thinking about how the reader can verify him.

> "People have concerns besides simply prolonging their lives. Surveys of patients with terminal illness find that their top priorities include, in addition to avoiding suffering, being with family, having the touch of others, being mentally aware, and not becoming a burden to others."

The passage says what is at stake for dying patients in concrete terms, being with family, the touch of others, staying mentally aware, not becoming a burden, and grounds the list in what surveys of patients actually report rather than asserting it as obvious. It keeps an emotional subject specific. Gawande lets the survey carry the claim instead of his own sympathy.

## Larissa MacFarquhar, "The Kindest Cut"

Source: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2009/07/27/the-kindest-cut

> "Doctors tend to be deeply suspicious of them. Especially in the early days of kidney transplants, in the late sixties and early seventies, many doctors saw altruistic donation through the lens of psychoanalysis, and, viewed thus, it looked troubling indeed. What was “altruism,” after all? A motive so much in conflict with primary instinct had to represent some kind of pathology. Was it masochism? Unresolved guilt? Altruistic donors, some doctors felt, were “not to be trusted,” “screwballs.” “These people must be abnormal—to do such a thing,” one transplant surgeon said."

MacFarquhar gives the case against altruistic donors its strongest and most concrete voice: the psychoanalytic reading, the run of skeptical questions, and finally a surgeon's own words, "These people must be abnormal." She reports the dismissal fully enough that a reader feels its force before any reply comes. She stays out of the way, quoting the skeptic at length instead of paraphrasing him into a strawman.

> "To the ethicist Gilbert Meilaender, for instance, the reluctance that many feel toward donating organs, even after death, is not selfishness or superstition but a sign that our sense of the body as something whole, something human, something sacred, has not yet withered. A society in which everyone in cheerful rationality signed his donor card without a qualm would be, to Meilaender, a horror."

She lends a thinker she is not endorsing his full dignity, rendering Meilaender's position at its most serious and attributing it by name, with no wink to the reader. The strong claim, that such a society would be "a horror," is quoted straight and left to stand as its holder means it. Naming Meilaender rather than "some ethicists" is what lets the view carry weight.

> "Still, the carnality of the act, the violation of the body, stops people. Its moral logic seems, to some, inhumanly rational, suicidally so: If we’re going to start thinking of bodies as repositories of spare parts for other people, why not donate all our organs and save many lives?"

She names the visceral objection precisely, the carnality of the act, the violation of the body, and then follows the donor's logic to the uncomfortable place it leads, that if bodies are repositories of spare parts there is no obvious stopping point. She voices both the unease and where the reasoning runs without telling the reader which to trust. "Suicidally so" shows she is willing to state how extreme the logic can look while still taking it seriously.
