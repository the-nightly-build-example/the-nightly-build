# Voice guide: flash-crash-risk

For the writer of a "What Could Go Wrong" lesson on the argument that fast,
correlated automated trading could destabilize markets.

## The one move this piece has to make

Open as the argument's most careful defender, at full strength, before a word
against it. The reader should finish the first body section understanding why a
serious economist or regulator worries about speed plus strategy homogeneity.
Only then test it. The test is not a rebuttal. It is a line drawn between one
thing that happened in a real market (May 6, 2010) and a set of things that have
happened only in simulations and arguments (ML/LLM trading agents). Draw that
line so cleanly the reader could redraw it from memory.

## Register

Matt Yglesias explaining something he understands well. Plain claims, concrete
stakes, real figures, no fuss. The reader is smart, reads widely, and has never
worked a trading desk or a codebase. Define every term of the field the first
time it is used: high-frequency trading, liquidity, spoofing, a circuit breaker.
Assume algebra and probability; assume nothing about market microstructure.

## Even-handed, and earned

No hype, no doom. The Flash Crash was real and it was fast; say so with the
figures and stop. Do not let the 2010 event stand in for an AI event it is not.
Steelman both sides of the present dispute: the people who say AI trading agents
will make the next crash worse, and the people who say the one clear precedent
predates modern AI and proves nothing about it. Give each its strongest form,
then say exactly what evidence would settle it. Land a judgment only where the
record earns one, and let the reader decide how worried to be.

## Traps specific to this subject

- Do not call the 2010 algorithms "AI." They were rule-based high-frequency
  trading. That correction is half the lesson; make it once, clearly, and do not
  blur it later.
- Do not import fear from the word "crash." A 9% intraday drop that recovered in
  minutes is not 1929. Give the size and the recovery together.
- Keep this argument distinct from algorithmic-collusion (pricing) and
  algorithmic-monoculture (correlated decisions). Name the distinction, link
  them, do not re-argue them.
- Name no bank, no fund, no vendor as an authority. Cite the record.

## Prose discipline

Short, single-purpose sentences, varied in length. Concrete nouns carry every
sentence; run the slop test on the first and last line of each section. No
negative-parallelism reflex ("not X but Y"). Period over em-dash. The body
addresses no reader and never mentions the lesson; only the two bookends do.
