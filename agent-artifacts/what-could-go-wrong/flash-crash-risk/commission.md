# Commission: what-could-go-wrong/flash-crash-risk

## Authorized work
Scheduled `nb duty` for 2026-09-09 returned `what-could-go-wrong` in open mode.
This commission authorizes exactly one lesson on the argument that automated
trading agents could destabilize financial markets. Template: lesson. Series
floor: >=8 sources, >=4 primary, >=1 secondary. Word band 1200-2200. Verified
against the full published library: this desk's 46 lessons cover the alignment
catalogue densely, but market-instability from automated trading is a genuine
gap. It is NOT `algorithmic-collusion` (pricing algorithms learning high prices)
and NOT `algorithmic-monoculture` (correlated decision/hiring errors); the
distinct claim here is speed plus strategy homogeneity producing sudden systemic
instability (crashes). Name that distinction in the piece.

## The argument, and how to test it
Open the argument at full strength: automated agents trade far faster than
people, on strategies that can be highly correlated, and when many react the same
way at once, liquidity can vanish in seconds and prices can gap violently, faster
than any human can intervene. Lay out why serious people hold it (the mechanics
of high-frequency trading, feedback loops, and herding). Then test it against
what real systems have actually done, drawing the sharp line this desk requires:
- Demonstrated: the May 6, 2010 "Flash Crash," when major US indices fell about
  9% and recovered within minutes. Say precisely what the record shows caused it
  (the CFTC-SEC joint report; later analysis by Kirilenko and colleagues; the
  Navinder Sarao spoofing prosecution) and, crucially, that the algorithms
  involved were rule-based high-frequency trading, NOT machine-learning or
  LLM-based agents.
- Analogy / guesswork: that ML or LLM-driven trading agents will cause worse or
  novel crashes. Cover recent simulation and research evidence on RL/LLM trading
  agents and coordination or herding, and be clear it is simulation and argument,
  not a market event on record.
Bring it to the present: who presses the argument now (regulators, some
economists, some AI-safety writers) and what they want (circuit breakers, kill
switches, agent oversight), checked against the most recent evidence. Name the
gap: the confidence that AI specifically will cause the next crash outruns a
record whose one clear precedent predates modern AI, while dismissing the risk
ignores that the 2010 mechanism is real and automation is only deepening.

## What the lesson teaches (short, complete)
1. The argument at full strength: why fast, correlated automated trading can
   produce sudden instability (liquidity withdrawal, feedback, herding).
2. The one demonstrated case, told accurately: the 2010 Flash Crash, its size and
   recovery, what the official record attributes it to, and that it was
   rule-based HFT, not AI.
3. Where confidence outruns proof on both sides today, and what evidence would
   settle whether ML/LLM trading agents make crashes worse or new.
