# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/treacherous-turn

## How this piece should sound

This lesson has two jobs that pull in different directions, and the exemplars
below were chosen because each one shows how to do both at once. The first job
is Piper's: lay out a contested claim without letting the description of "the
skeptics" or "the believers" collapse into two teams. When this lesson
introduces who doubts the treacherous turn and why, give the doubt its actual
shape the way Piper does — distance skeptics, hype-fatigue skeptics, and
capability skeptics are three different objections, not one — and where a real
person's public position cuts against the tribe they're assigned to, say so
with their name attached, the way she does with LeCun. The neutrality
requirement in the commission is not a tone instruction to sound neutral; it is
a factual claim about the field that Piper's piece shows how to report.

The second job is Gawande's: a claim gets tested against evidence one specific
fact at a time, and the fact does the work, not the writer's adjective. This
lesson's spine is an audit — Bostrom's prediction against sleeper agents,
alignment-faking, and in-context scheming — and Gawande's dinner-table scene is
a model for exactly that structure: state what a plausible-sounding explanation
would predict, then produce the one number or one detail that shows what
actually happened. A sleeper agent's backdoor was trained in; that single fact,
placed where the reader can feel its weight, does more than a paragraph of
hedging about "the limits of induced behavior." Reach for the specific
mechanism of each experiment before reaching for the word "suggestive."

Ornes's Quanta piece is the closest register match for the sentences that carry
the actual audit, because the subject is adjacent: a capability that might be
real or might be an artifact of how it was measured, reported through a
researcher who states her own preferred answer and then admits she can't
currently tell which is right. That last move — a person with a stake in the
exciting answer saying plainly that the evidence doesn't yet decide it — is
close to the sentence this lesson needs wherever alignment-faking or scheming
evaluations are described: name what would make the result count as a real
treacherous turn, and say plainly that the 2024 record does not yet meet it,
without implying that it therefore means nothing. Ornes's habit of giving the
actual proportion (5% of tasks, not "several" or "a surprising number") is the
default for every figure in the empirical middle third: a backdoor rate, a
fraction of scheming evaluations that succeeded, the scale at which a behavior
appeared.

Two things follow from the commission's mandatory neutrality requirement that
none of the exemplars can settle for you, because none of them was written
under it. Where this lesson cites a safety team's own published result, the
sentence needs to make clear in its own grammar that a test was run, not that
an authority has spoken — the citation carries the finding, the institution
does not. And a published critic of that result needs the same treatment
Piper gives LeCun: quoted or cited by name, with their actual objection, not
folded into a clause like "though some have pushed back."

Bostrom's own prose is not a model for the opening section. His argument
should appear at full strength, but the sentences reporting it belong to this
lesson's own plain, declarative register — the one Piper, Gawande, and Ornes
all share regardless of subject — not to a pastiche of Superintelligence's
own voice. A technical term like "instrumental convergence" earns one plain
sentence of definition where it first appears and then gets used exactly that
way for the rest of the piece, the way Ornes reuses "emergent" once he's
established what it means here rather than reaching for synonyms.

## Kelsey Piper, "The case for taking AI seriously as a threat to humanity"

Source: https://www.vox.com/future-perfect/2018/12/21/18126576/ai-artificial-intelligence-machine-learning-safety-alignment

> "There are also skeptics. Some of them think advanced AI is so distant that there's no point in thinking about it now. Others are worried that excessive hype about the power of their field might kill it prematurely. And even among the people who broadly agree that AI poses unique dangers, there are varying takes on what steps make the most sense today."

This sentence-by-sentence breakdown gives "skeptic" three separate, incompatible reasons before the reader can flatten them into a single position. Piper is visible in the choice to spend three sentences on people she is not agreeing with, before the piece has argued anything of its own.

> "It's tempting to conclude that there's a pitched battle between AI-risk skeptics and AI-risk believers. In reality, they might not disagree as profoundly as you would think. Facebook's chief AI scientist Yann LeCun, for example, is a vocal voice on the skeptical side. But while he argues we shouldn't fear AI, he still believes we ought to have people working on, and thinking about, AI safety."

She names the tempting, simpler story and then produces one real, named person whose actual public position doesn't fit it. The correction is a fact about LeCun, not a claim about "many skeptics" — a reader could go check what he said.

> "To people who think the worrying is premature and the risks overblown, AI safety is competing with other priorities that sound, well, a bit less sci-fi — and it's not clear why AI should take precedence. To people who think the risks described are real and substantial, it's outrageous that we're dedicating so few resources to working on them."

The piece's last substantive move states both camps' case, each in one complete sentence, back to back, and stops. There's no third sentence declaring who's right; the two views are left standing next to each other.

## Atul Gawande, "The Cost Conundrum"

Source: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2009/06/01/the-cost-conundrum

> "Yet public-health statistics show that cardiovascular-disease rates in the county are actually lower than average, probably because its smoking rates are quite low. Rates of asthma, H.I.V., infant mortality, cancer, and injury are lower, too. El Paso County, eight hundred miles up the border, has essentially the same demographics. Both counties have a population of roughly seven hundred thousand, similar public-health statistics, and similar percentages of non-English speakers, illegal immigrants, and the unemployed. Yet in 2006 Medicare expenditures (our best approximation of over-all spending patterns) in El Paso were $7,504 per enrollee—half as much as in McAllen. An unhealthy population couldn't possibly be the reason that McAllen's health-care costs are so high."

He states a plausible explanation, then supplies a matched comparison town and one figure, and only after the arithmetic does he deliver the verdict on that explanation. "Half as much" is doing the persuading; the sentence that states the conclusion comes last and is short.

> "'It's malpractice,' a family physician who had practiced here for thirty-three years said. 'McAllen is legal hell,' the cardiologist agreed. Doctors order unnecessary tests just to protect themselves, he said. Everyone thought the lawyers here were worse than elsewhere. That explanation puzzled me. Several years ago, Texas passed a tough malpractice law that capped pain-and-suffering awards at two hundred and fifty thousand dollars. Didn't lawsuits go down? 'Practically to zero,' the cardiologist admitted."

Gawande lets the doctors state their explanation in full, in their own words, before he interrupts with a single factual question. The person who made the claim is the one who supplies the fact that undoes it; Gawande's sentence is only the question.

> "The real puzzle of American health care, I realized on the airplane home, is not why McAllen is different from El Paso. It's why El Paso isn't like McAllen. Every incentive in the system is an invitation to go the way McAllen has gone. Yet, across the country, large numbers of communities have managed to control their health costs rather than ratchet them up."

The reframing is earned by everything counted and compared before it, and it's introduced as something he arrived at ("I realized"), not announced as a finding. It restates the piece's whole investigation in seven words without adding a new claim.

## Stephen Ornes, "The Unpredictable Abilities Emerging From Large AI Models"

Source: https://www.quantamagazine.org/the-unpredictable-abilities-emerging-from-large-ai-models-20230316/

> "Recent findings like these suggest at least two possibilities for why emergence occurs, said Ellie Pavlick... One is that, as suggested by comparisons to biological systems, larger models truly do gain new abilities spontaneously. 'It may very well be that the model has learned something fundamentally new and different that it didn't have at a smaller size,' she said. 'That's what we're all hoping is the case, that there's some fundamental shift that happens when models are scaled up.' The other, less sensational possibility, she said, is that what appears to be emergent may instead be the culmination of an internal, statistics-driven process that works through chain-of-thought-type reasoning... But, she said, finding out which of those explanations is more likely hinges on a better understanding of how LLMs work at all. 'Since we don't know how they work under the hood, we can't say which of those things is happening.'"

Ornes gives the more exciting hypothesis and the duller one in the researcher's own words, including her admission that the exciting one is "what we're all hoping is the case." The paragraph ends with her saying nobody can currently tell which is true, and that sentence is the last word — the uncertainty isn't resolved for effect.

> "As one would expect, on some tasks a model's performance improved smoothly and predictably as complexity increased. And on other tasks, scaling up the number of parameters did not yield any improvement. But for about 5% of the tasks, the researchers found what they called 'breakthroughs'—rapid, dramatic jumps in performance at some threshold scale. That threshold varied based on the task and model."

He reports three outcomes (smooth, flat, and a jump) and attaches the actual proportion to the interesting one instead of a word like "several" or "surprisingly many." The precise figure sits where an adjective would otherwise have to do the work.

> "'We're increasingly relying on these models to do basic work,' Ganguli said, 'but I do not just trust these. I check their work.'"

The quote states a practice, not an opinion — what Ganguli does, not what he thinks about the models in the abstract. It's short, and it comes from someone using the systems for his own work rather than commenting on them from outside.
