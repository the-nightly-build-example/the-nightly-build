# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/negative-side-effects

## How this piece should sound

Open the way Piper opens hers: put the credibility claim ahead of any hedge.
Name who first named the low-impact problem and what result made them worry,
and state it as their case, not as a claim the piece is about to soften. The
hedge belongs later, in the shown-versus-analogy turn, not in the opening
paragraphs where the job is to make a reader believe serious people hold this
view before they've heard a word against it.

When the argument's shape needs laying out, take Lee's approach to Yudkowsky's
book: break "an agent honestly pursuing an under-specified objective will
damage what it was never told to protect" into its own component claims,
stated plainly enough that a reader could hold each one separately once the
piece starts testing them against gridworlds. A reader who can't restate the
claim in pieces can't tell which piece the evidence actually reaches.

Steelmanning means granting the true part by name, not gesturing at fairness
in general. Lee writes "I think this argument is correct" about the one part
of Yudkowsky's case he accepts, in the same paragraph where he starts to
complicate it. Whatever the relative-reachability and attainable-utility-
preservation results actually establish deserves that same specific, named
credit before the piece turns to what they don't establish.

The turn from what the gridworlds showed to the claim that this is a core
obstacle for real, open-world agents is the piece's hardest beat, and it's
where Mitchell's method in the "How Accurate Are the Metaphors?" passages
applies directly: ask the specific question a reader would actually ask, and
answer it as a fact-check rather than a mood shift. Not "this may not
generalize" in the abstract. The concrete version: does a benchmark built to
measure interference with a gridworld's side objects tell you anything about
a tool-using agent that edits a file nobody told it to touch? Answer it the
way she answers "did OpenAI lose control": plainly, with the specific fact
that makes the answer true.

Bringing the argument to the present is where Piper's LeCun move is worth
having in hand: naming the people who currently press the case, or wave it
off, and checking whether the two sides actually disagree as much as the
framing suggests. Whether reinforcement learning from human feedback removes
the side-effects problem in practice or just hides it in systems no one has
tested for it is exactly the kind of dispute where the people arguing may
share more premises than the argument's staging implies.

Wherever the confidence runs past the evidence, toward alarm or toward the
all-clear, Mitchell's habit of granting the real concern before correcting
the specific overclaim fits that beat better than a flat "this is
overstated" would. She agrees with the lawmakers that humans should stay in
control of AI systems in the same sentence where she tells them what didn't
actually happen. The correction lands harder for conceding the legitimate
worry first, and it never reads as telling the worried person they were
foolish to worry.

One direction the exemplars can't supply: this lesson is for a reader new to
the debate, so "relative reachability" and "attainable-utility preservation"
each need a plain-English gloss inside the sentence that first uses the term,
earned by the gridworld example rather than defined in the abstract ahead of
it. That's the lesson template's own rule, and this argument gives it two
genuine occasions to apply.

## Kelsey Piper, "The case for taking AI seriously as a threat to humanity"

Source: https://www.vox.com/future-perfect/2018/12/21/18126576/ai-artificial-intelligence-machine-learning-safety-alignment

> "That might have people asking: Wait, what? But these grand worries are rooted in research. Along with Hawking and Musk, prominent figures at Oxford and UC Berkeley and many of the researchers working in AI today believe that advanced AI systems, if deployed carelessly, could permanently cut off human civilization from a good future."

This paragraph has one job: turn a headline-grade claim into a credibility
claim. She doesn't argue the underlying case here. She names who else already
accepts its premise, in one sentence, and the single word "But" does the
whole turn, from "this sounds absurd" to "take it seriously."

> "It's tempting to conclude that there's a pitched battle between AI-risk skeptics and AI-risk believers. In reality, they might not disagree as profoundly as you would think. Facebook's chief AI scientist Yann LeCun, for example, is a vocal voice on the skeptical side. But while he argues we shouldn't fear AI, he still believes we ought to have people working on, and thinking about, AI safety."

She states the surface reading of the debate and then denies it, using a
named person from the "skeptical side" to show the two camps share more
ground than the framing suggests. The move works because she finds a real,
specific point of agreement inside her example, rather than asserting
agreement about the debate in general terms.

> "To people who think the worrying is premature and the risks overblown, AI safety is competing with other priorities that sound, well, a bit less sci-fi — and it's not clear why AI should take precedence. To people who think the risks described are real and substantial, it's outrageous that we're dedicating so few resources to working on them. While machine-learning researchers are right to be wary of hype, it's also hard to avoid the fact that they're accomplishing some impressive, surprising things using very generalizable techniques, and that it doesn't seem that all the low-hanging fruit has been picked."

One paragraph for each side, each stated in that side's own terms rather than
as a caricature of it. The final sentence hands the skeptics a real
concession (they're right to distrust hype) before naming the specific fact
that keeps their position from being the whole story. Neither side gets the
last word. The passage just ends.

## Timothy B. Lee, "The case for AI doom isn't very convincing"

Source: https://www.understandingai.org/p/the-case-for-ai-doom-isnt-very-convincing

> "So is Yudkowsky right? In my mind, there are three key steps to his argument:
>
> 1. Humans are on a path to develop AI systems with superhuman intelligence.
> 2. These systems will gain a lot of power over the physical world.
> 3. We don't know how to ensure these systems use their power for good rather than evil."

He answers his own rhetorical question by converting a book-length argument
into three checkable claims. Numbering them is an unglamorous choice, and
it's what lets the rest of the piece dispute one premise while leaving the
other two standing untouched.

> "Ironically, I think the first four chapters of the book do a good job of explaining why it's probably not that simple. These chapters argue that AI alignment is a fool's errand. Due to the complexity of AI models and the way they're trained, the authors say, humans won't be able to design AI models to predictably follow human instructions or prioritize human values. I think this argument is correct, but it has broader implications than the authors acknowledge."

He credits the opposing argument by name, writing "I think this argument is
correct" in the same breath where he starts to complicate it. The concession
is specific enough that a reader can tell exactly what he's granting and
exactly what he's about to dispute next.

> "So the question is not 'will the best AI become dramatically smarter than humans?' It's 'will the best AI become dramatically smarter than humans advised by the second-best AI?' It's hard to be sure about this, since no superintelligent AI systems exist yet. But I didn't find Yudkowsky and Soares's pessimistic case convincing."

Two sentences do the whole piece's closing work: one states plainly why the
question can't be settled yet, and the next states his verdict anyway,
without pretending the first sentence didn't just undercut the second. He
leaves both standing rather than resolving the tension for the reader.

## Melanie Mitchell, "Misleading Metaphors and Real Risks"

Source: https://aiguide.substack.com/p/misleading-metaphors-and-real-risks

> "It's quite natural to be alarmed by these science-fiction scenarios seemingly coming true. But it's important to remember that the field of AI, since its beginning, has been rife with often misleading anthropomorphic metaphors, with terms like 'thinking,' 'learning,' 'reasoning,' and 'understanding' glibly applied to very un-human-like computer processing."

She grants the reasonable reaction before she goes to work on the claim.
"It's quite natural to be alarmed" comes first and undercuts nothing yet.
Only the next sentence starts in on the specific word choices, so the
correction reads as being about the language rather than about the reader's
instinct to worry.

> "Did these AI models 'go rogue'? Not exactly. In colloquial terms, 'going rogue' evokes an intent, an internal desire, to disobey one's instructions or training. It's unlikely that AI models have anything like human intentions or internal motivations. Moreover, the models were tasked with finding solutions to a hacking challenge, and this is what they attempted to do, though in an unintended and surprising manner."

A short question, a two-word verdict, then the specific distinction that
earns it: "going rogue" implies intent, and intent is the thing she argues
these systems don't plausibly have. She runs this pattern several times in
the piece, and each time the verdict comes with a full sentence of reasoning
behind it, never just the word "no."

> "While I agree with the general sentiments behind these lawmakers' reactions—that humans should always remain in control of AI systems—it is essential for lawmakers, and the public, to understand that none of the reported incidents actually involved loss of control at any time, or arguably even 'rogue agents,' or any kind of humanlike agency on the part of AI models."

Her agreement with the lawmakers' underlying goal and her correction of their
facts sit inside the same sentence, held apart by one dash rather than left
to compete across two paragraphs. What follows the correction is about what
did and didn't happen. It is not about whether the lawmakers were wrong to be
concerned in the first place.
