# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/ai-boxing (01)

## How this piece should sound

This lesson teaches the argument that you cannot safely contain a
superintelligent system by boxing it: sealing it off as an output-only oracle
and trusting the seal. The reader is quick and widely read but has never worked
inside a codebase, so every term the field takes for granted (an air-gapped
system, an oracle, a side channel, a sandbox) is built in plain words the first
time it appears. The register is a person explaining something they understand,
calm and unhurried, closer to Matt Yglesias than to either an alarm or a
debunking.

The piece opens with the argument at full strength, and the opening can read the
way Dan Luu states the case against Ballmer before he answers it: in the words
its most serious holders would use, with nothing filed off. A reader who already
believes a box cannot hold should meet their own reasoning here at full force.
The capability asymmetry, the manipulable human gatekeeper, and the many
channels a sealed system could exploit each get their real weight before a word
of test arrives.

The turn of the lesson is a line between what a working system has actually shown
and what is still analogy about systems that do not exist. Devereaux draws that
kind of line when he fixes his claim to "usually" and to where farming was
possible, and no wider. Where the lesson reports that a system did something, the
sentence can say what the setup supplied and what the system itself did, and hold
the claim to that size. When the case rests on the AI-box roleplays, which are
human against human and mostly unlogged, the writing can say plainly what that
evidence can and cannot carry, the way Alexander tells the reader outright that
the minimum-wage literature is unsettled and looks settled.

Where the evidence runs out, the verdict is sized to it. Alexander reaches a
conclusion but calls it "some evidence" and "at least some". A claim about
whether a box holds can be pressed only as far as what has been observed, and
where confidence outruns proof the sentence can say so, whether the confidence
points toward doom or toward dismissal. Luu's habit of naming what a criticism
would have to show, then checking that exact measure, fits the containment
question directly: for each result on record, the writing can ask what would
count as an escape, and whether the system did that or the researchers arranged
it.

Keep the prose plain enough to say aloud. The subject supplies plenty of real
nouns (exfiltration evals, control protocols, gatekeepers, oracles, side
channels); reach for those rather than for a grand word, and let a genuinely new
or genuinely dangerous result carry its own weight once the evidence is on the
page.

## Dan Luu, "Steve Ballmer was an underrated CEO"

Source: https://danluu.com/ballmer/

> "To sum it up, for the past twenty years, people having been dunking on Ballmer for being a buffoon who doesn't understand tech and who was, at best, some kind of bean counter who knew how to keep the lights on but didn't know how to foster innovation and caused Microsoft to fall behind in every important market."

Before he argues against the standard view of Ballmer, Luu states it at its most
cutting, in the words its holders actually use ("buffoon", "bean counter"). He
does not soften the charge he is about to dispute, so a reader who holds it
recognizes their own position rather than a watered-down copy of it.

> "This is like pointing to a list of failed companies a VC has funded as evidence the VC doesn't know what they're doing. But that's silly in a hits based industry like venture capital. If you want to claim the VC is bad, you need to point out poor total return or a lack of big successes, which would imply poor total return."

Luu does not just deny that the failures prove the point. He says what a real
version of the criticism would have to show, then goes and checks that measure.
The care is visible in how he keeps separating a list of misses from the
total-return figure that would actually settle the question.

> "...but the criticism of Ballmer that says that he had some failures and some $1T successes is a criticism that he wasn't the greatest CEO of all time by a gigantic margin. True, but not much of a criticism."

He grants the point instead of fighting it, then measures how much it is worth
once granted. The plainness of "True, but not much of a criticism" is Luu
weighing a concession in the open rather than arguing it away.

## Scott Alexander, "Beware The Man Of One Study"

Source: https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/12/12/beware-the-man-of-one-study/

> "For example, take medical research. Suppose a certain drug is weakly effective against a certain disease. After a few years, a bunch of different research groups have gotten their hands on it and done all sorts of different studies. In the best case scenario the average study will find the true result – that it's weakly effective."

Alexander builds the idea from a concrete case a reader can hold, one drug and
its studies, before he draws any lesson from it. The "suppose" is him walking the
reader through the setup step by step instead of stating the conclusion and
asking them to trust it.

> "So the whole thing is fiendishly complicated. But unless you look very very hard, you will never know that."

After laying study against study on the minimum wage, he tells the reader
directly that the question is unsettled and easy to mistake for settled. He says
the difficulty out loud instead of covering it with a tidy answer.

> "We see it's centered around 0, which means there's some evidence that's the real signal among all this noise. The bell skews more to left than to the right, which means more studies have found negative effects of the minimum wage than positive effects of the minimum wage. But since the bell curve is asymmetrical, we intepret that as probably publication bias. So all in all, I think there's at least some evidence that the liberals are right on this one."

He does reach a conclusion, but he sizes it to the evidence: "some evidence", "at
least some". The verdict shows up as the small claim the funnel plot supports,
not the larger one the topic would tempt him toward.

## Bret Devereaux, "Collections: The Fremen Mirage, Part I: War at the Dawn of Civilization"

Source: https://acoup.blog/2020/01/17/collections-the-fremen-mirage-part-i-war-at-the-dawn-of-civilization/

> "Under the assumptions of our Fremen Mirage, we ought to expect farming societies to be frequently overtaken and subjugated by their non-farming neighbors, who still possess all of the skills and supposed 'toughness' that comes from the life of a hunter-gatherer."

Devereaux states what the theory he doubts would have to predict if it were true,
in the theory's own terms, before he brings any evidence against it. Taking the
opposing view seriously enough to say what it forecasts is what lets the evidence
that follows actually bear on it.

> "This march of agriculture and the state I've laid out doesn't mean that farmers and states *always* win, merely that – in areas where agriculture is possible – they *usually* won."

The whole sentence is built to fix the size of the claim: *always* against
*usually*, and only where farming was possible. Devereaux is marking the line
between what his evidence shows and what it does not, and the weight he puts on
those two words is where the care is visible.

> "If I may lean on a sports analogy, we would not call a team 'better' if they lost 98 games but happened to win the last 2. The question is both the ratio of victories to defeats, and the impact of those results."

One concrete comparison does the work of defining what the disputed word
"stronger" should mean before the record gets judged. Devereaux is setting the
standard the evidence will be read against, in plain terms a reader can apply for
themselves.
