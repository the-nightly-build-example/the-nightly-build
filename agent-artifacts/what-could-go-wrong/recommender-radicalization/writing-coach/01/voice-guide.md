# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/recommender-radicalization

## How this piece should sound

Open the way Engber opens "Everything Is Crumbling": with the claim in its most
vivid, undefended form, in the terms its own advocates used, before anything
complicates it. For this lesson that means Pariser's "filter bubble" and
Tufekci's "Great Radicalizer," named, with what each of them had seen, and the
engagement-optimization mechanism let to stand on its own strength the way
Engber lets Baumeister's willpower-as-muscle stand before any pushback arrives.
Define "recommender system," "engagement optimization," and "filter bubble" in
the same clause where each first appears, the way Yong defines social priming,
ego depletion, and the facial-feedback hypothesis in a single plain clause
apiece as he names them — unless an earlier lesson already owns the term, in
which case a link does the work instead of a redefinition.

When the piece turns to the studies, put the real numbers ahead of the
interpretation, the way "14 out of 28" and "15,305 participants... from 36
different countries" arrive before Yong says what they imply. Hosseinmardi's
sample, the Chen and Nyhan design, the platforms and windows of the 2020
election studies are all countable; give the reader the count first. A flatly
negative finding earns a flat sentence, not an adjective — "the study found
exactly nothing" gets its force from refusing to editorialize, and this piece
has at least one finding that deserves the same treatment.

Where the two sides' confidence is being weighed against each other — this
lesson's own contribution — the comparative verdict Belluz gives spinal
manipulation is the shape to reach for: a finding stated only relative to a
named alternative ("no more than over-the-counter painkillers or exercise"),
never a bare yes-or-no. Both "the algorithm radicalizes ordinary users" and
"recommenders are harmless" can take this treatment: say what each claim would
need, that the cited study didn't supply, in one sentence that gives the
ceiling and the floor together, the way Belluz's massage verdict states the
short-term benefit and the missing long-term evidence in the same breath.

Attribute a contested reading to the person who holds it, named, in their own
words, rather than to "researchers" or "critics" in general. Yong hands the
verdict to Simine Vazire by name and lets her state the tradeoff — short-run
credibility against long-run damage — in her own words rather than paraphrasing
it for her. This lesson has real names on every side of the argument: Pariser,
Tufekci, Hosseinmardi, Nyhan, Guess, and whoever today's regulators and
plaintiffs turn out to be. Any of them can carry a judgment the same way.

Keep the graded, comparative habit these three writers share without the moral
charge some of them allow themselves. Belluz calls the untreated status quo a
"horror"; this paper keeps no hype and no doom, so the same precision about what
a study did and didn't show should arrive without being dressed up as outrage or
relief. The gap this lesson closes on — where each side's confidence outruns
its proof — is the payoff on its own. It doesn't need a stronger adjective than
the studies earn it.

## Ed Yong, "Psychology's Replication Crisis Is Running Out of Excuses"

Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/science/archive/2018/11/psychologys-replication-crisis-real/576223/

> "Over the past few years, an international team of almost 200 psychologists has been trying to repeat a set of previously published experiments from its field, to see if it can get the same results. Despite its best efforts, the project, called Many Labs 2, has only succeeded in 14 out of 28 cases. Six years ago, that might have been shocking. Now it comes as expected (if still somewhat disturbing) news."

The finding arrives as a plain fact, with its exact count, before any
interpretation. The last sentence carries Yong's own judgment without an
adjective doing the work: "now it comes as expected" states that he isn't
surprised instead of performing surprise, and the parenthetical admits the
finding is still bad news without raising its volume.

> "In recent years, it has become painfully clear that psychology is facing a “reproducibility crisis,” in which even famous, long-established phenomena—the stuff of textbooks and TED Talks—might not be real. There's social priming, where subliminal exposures can influence our behavior. And ego depletion, the idea that we have a limited supply of willpower that can be exhausted. And the facial-feedback hypothesis, which simply says that smiling makes us feel happier."

Three claims are named and each is defined in the same breath it's introduced,
in one plain clause. A reader who has never heard of any of them is caught up
by the end of the sentence. The short fragments starting "And ego depletion..."
and "And the facial-feedback hypothesis..." give the list a spoken rhythm
rather than a bulleted one.

> "That failure rate is especially galling, says Simine Vazire from the University of California at Davis, because the Many Labs 2 teams tried to replicate studies that had made a big splash and been highly cited. Psychologists “should admit we haven't been producing results that are as robust as we'd hoped, or as we'd been advertising them to be in the media or to policy makers,” she says. “That might risk undermining our credibility in the short run, but denying this problem in the face of such strong evidence will do more damage in the long run.”"

Instead of paraphrasing what the finding means, Yong hands the interpretation
to a named researcher and lets her state it in her own words, including the
specific tradeoff she's weighing. His own sentence does only the work of
saying who is talking and why she's positioned to say it; the judgment itself
is entirely hers, on the record.

## Daniel Engber, "Everything Is Crumbling"

Source: https://www.slate.com/articles/health_and_science/cover_story/2016/03/ego_depletion_an_influential_theory_in_psychology_may_have_just_been_debunked.html

> "Nearly 20 years ago, psychologists Roy Baumeister and Dianne Tice, a married couple at Case Western Reserve University, devised a foundational experiment on self-control. “Chocolate chip cookies were baked in the room in a small oven,” they wrote in a paper that has been cited more than 3,000 times. “As a result, the laboratory was filled with the delicious aroma of fresh chocolate and baking.”"

The piece opens on the actual experiment and a specific physical detail —
what the room smelled like, in the researchers' own words — rather than a
topic sentence announcing that willpower research matters. The citation count
sits right beside the anecdote, so the reader has both the vivid detail and the
claim's institutional weight before the argument even starts.

> "The authors called this effect “ego depletion” and said it revealed a fundamental fact about the human mind: We all have a limited supply of willpower, and it decreases with overuse. Eating a radish when you're surrounded by fresh-baked cookies represents an epic feat of self-denial, and one that really wears you out. Willpower, argued Baumeister and Tice, draws down mental energy—it's a muscle that can be exercised to exhaustion."

The theory is stated with the same confidence its inventors held, in their own
metaphor, with no hedge like "researchers claimed" softening it in advance.
Nothing in the passage signals yet that the idea is in trouble — the
full-strength version is allowed to stand on its own before any complication
arrives later in the piece.

> "Comprising more than 2,000 subjects tested at two-dozen different labs on several continents, the study found exactly nothing. A zero-effect for ego depletion: No sign that the human will works as it's been described, or that these hundreds of studies amount to very much at all."

The turn arrives on a short, flat sentence with a number attached before any
interpretation. "Found exactly nothing" carries its force by refusing an
adjective. The colon that follows spells out precisely what the null result
does and doesn't mean, instead of leaving "nothing" to do unclarified work.

## Julia Belluz, "A comprehensive guide to the new science of treating lower back pain"

Source: https://www.vox.com/science-and-health/2017/8/4/15929484/chronic-back-pain-treatment-mainstream-vs-alternative

> "What I found surprised me: Many of these approaches really do seem to help, though often with modest effects. … “No one dies of low back pain,” one back pain expert, University of Amsterdam assistant professor Sidney Rubinstein, summed up, “but people are now dying from the treatment.”"

Belluz states her own finding as a personal surprise and qualifies it in the
same sentence ("though often with modest effects") rather than walking it back
later. She then hands the sharpest line to a named specialist in his own
words instead of writing it herself; the line earns its place because it
states a fact, not an opinion.

> "There is some evidence the approach can help people with chronic back pain — but not any more than over-the-counter painkillers or exercise, and you need to take precautions when seeking out a chiropractor."

The verdict on spinal manipulation is one sentence with the comparison built
in: better than nothing, no better than the cheap alternatives. The claim
doesn't exist as a bare "it works" or "it doesn't" — it exists only relative to
a specific alternative, which is what a reader trying to decide something
actually needs.

> "According to AHRQ, for subacute (lasting between seven and 12 weeks) and chronic low back pain, massage seems to improve symptoms and function in the short term (i.e., one week) — but there's no evidence that it leads to any long-term change. At best, you'll get a bit of immediate relief, but nothing lasting."

The ceiling and the floor of the evidence sit in the same sentence: what was
shown (short-term relief) and what wasn't (anything lasting). A reader can't
come away thinking either that massage is useless or that it's a fix, because
both limits are stated together instead of one arriving as a later
qualification.
