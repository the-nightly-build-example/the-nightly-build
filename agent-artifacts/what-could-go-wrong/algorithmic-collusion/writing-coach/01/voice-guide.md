# Voice guide: algorithmic collusion

## How this piece should sound

This lesson teaches algorithmic collusion to a reader who is quick and
well-read but new to both the pricing economics and the AI-safety worry.
Keep the sentences plain and the stakes concrete. The claim at the center,
that independent pricing systems can arrive at high prices without any
instruction to coordinate, stays abstract until it is worked through a
specific case, and Holden Karnofsky's habit of pegging an abstract rate to
years the reader already remembers ("2019 was pretty similar to 2018 ...
hugely but not crazily different from 1980") is the kind of grounding the
collusion mechanism needs before the reader is asked to reason about it.

The series opens each argument at full strength, and this one should reach the
reader as its most careful defender would put it: the reasoning of the
economists who first worried that pricing algorithms, left to maximize profit
against each other, could learn to keep prices high without ever being told to
coordinate. State it so a skeptic would grant it a fair hearing before a word
tests it. Scott Alexander's numbered explanations show the discipline of
giving even the possibility he means to reject its honest first hearing, and
the collusion argument earns that same hearing before the piece pushes on it.

The craft this piece turns on is keeping two things apart on the page: what a
simulated or tightly constrained pricing system has actually been shown to do,
and what is still analogy or guesswork about real markets, real firms, and the
regulators watching them. The reader should always be able to tell which side
of that line a given sentence sits on. Karnofsky does this in the open,
granting what is imaginable and then marking exactly where his own confidence
stops ("Sure - but this would require ..."), and saying plainly what a claim
rests on ("basic observations, not detailed reasoning about AI"). When the
collusion case rests on a laboratory result, the reader should know it rests on
one; when it rests on extrapolating to markets that may behave differently, the
reader should know that instead.

Test the argument against the evidence the way Alexander tests his candidates,
rejecting the government-inefficiency explanation because specific comparisons
that would have to hold do not, and declining to resolve the pet-care
counterexample when he genuinely cannot ("I'm not sure what to make of this").
Where the recent evidence cuts against the collusion worry, the piece can say
so; where the question is genuinely open, saying it is open is an honest place
to end, and closing it with a manufactured verdict is not. Dan Luu's weighing
is the same discipline applied to judgment: grant the criticism that is
correct, measure how much it actually damages the case, and keep a proven point
apart from an unproven one.

Bring the argument to the present without alarm and without dismissal, naming
who presses it now and what they want done, and checking their confidence,
whether it runs to doom or to dismissal, against what has actually been shown.
The strongest words are worth holding back until the evidence has established
them. One thing the exemplars cannot settle for you: each of them shows honesty
in the first person, in an "I don't know" or an "I live in a different
headspace," and this lesson's body speaks to no one and never mentions itself.
Here the same honesty has to come through prose that commits exactly as far as
the evidence allows and names what is unresolved plainly, with the direct
address to the reader kept to the two bookends.

## Scott Alexander, "Considerations On Cost Disease"

Source: https://slatestarcodex.com/2017/02/09/considerations-on-cost-disease/

> "First, can we dismiss all of this as an illusion? Maybe adjusting for
> inflation is harder than I think. Inflation is an average, so some things
> have to have higher-than-average inflation; maybe it's education, health
> care, etc. Or maybe my sources have the wrong statistics."

He states the strongest version of the objection to his own claim before he
answers it, and states it as a real possibility a careful reader might hold
rather than a weak one he can knock down. Each candidate explanation is
numbered and gets its fair hearing first. The plainness of "maybe my sources
have the wrong statistics" is a writer declining to make the opposing view
sound foolish.

> "Third, can we attribute this to the inefficiency of government relative to
> private industry? I don't think so. The government handles most primary
> education and subways, and has its hand in health care. But we know that
> for-profit hospitals aren't much cheaper than government hospitals, and that
> private schools usually aren't much cheaper (and are sometimes more
> expensive) than government schools. And private colleges cost more than
> government-funded ones."

He names a hypothesis, then rejects it by lining up the specific comparisons
that would have to hold if it were true and pointing out that they do not. The
reasoning is all on the page: for-profit against government hospitals, private
against government schools, and the direction each comparison cuts. Nothing is
asserted that the next clause does not back with a case.

> "Here's a more interesting (and more fun) argument against regulations being
> to blame: what about pet health care? Veterinary care is much less regulated
> than human health care, yet its cost is rising as fast (or faster) than that
> of the human medical system. … I'm not sure what to make of this."

He raises the counterexample that works against his own leading explanation,
calls it "more fun" because it complicates the case he is building, and then
declines to resolve it. "I'm not sure what to make of this" is a writer who
would rather leave a question open than close it dishonestly, and the honesty
is visible because he is the one who volunteered the evidence that hurt him.

## Holden Karnofsky, "This Can't Go On"

Source: https://www.cold-takes.com/this-cant-go-on/

> "In Business As Usual, the world is constantly changing, and the change is
> noticeable, but it's not overwhelming or impossible to keep up with. There is
> a constant stream of new opportunities and new challenges, but if you want to
> take a few extra years to adapt to them while you mostly do things the way
> you were doing them before, you can usually (personally) get away with that.
> In terms of day-to-day life, 2019 was pretty similar to 2018, noticeably but
> not hugely different from 2010, and hugely but not crazily different from
> 1980."

He turns an abstract idea, a steady rate of change, into something the reader
can feel by scaling it to years they lived through. The calibration is careful
and stepped: similar, then noticeably different, then hugely different, each
pegged to a specific span of time. The parenthetical "(personally)" is a writer
catching and correcting his own overstatement inside the sentence.

> "Is it imaginable that we could develop the technology to support multiple
> equivalents of today's entire civilization, per atom available? Sure - but
> this would require a radical degree of transformation of our lives and
> societies, far beyond how much change we've seen over the course of human
> history to date. And I wouldn't exactly bet that this is how things are going
> to go over the next several thousand years."

He grants the strong version of the possibility outright ("Sure"), then marks
precisely where his agreement stops and his doubt starts. His own position is
given as a bet he would or would not make, which tells the reader how confident
he is without inflating it. The concession and the doubt share the same
sentence, so neither one gets lost.

> "So it seems like this is a quite remarkable century, with some chance of
> being the most remarkable. This is all based on pretty basic observations,
> not detailed reasoning about AI (which I will get to in future pieces)."

He states his conclusion and, in the same breath, says exactly what it does and
does not rest on. Telling the reader the claim comes from "basic observations,
not detailed reasoning about AI" is a writer marking the strength of his own
evidence so no one takes it for more than it is. He keeps his claim and his
proof in view at the same time.

## Dan Luu, "Steve Ballmer was an underrated CEO"

Source: https://danluu.com/ballmer/

> "There's a common narrative that Microsoft was moribund under Steve Ballmer
> and then later saved by the miraculous leadership of Satya Nadella. This is
> the dominant narrative in every online discussion about the topic I've seen
> and it's a commonly expressed belief 'in real life' as well. While I don't
> have anything negative to say about Nadella's leadership in this post, this
> narrative underrates Ballmer's role in Microsoft's success."

He states the received view plainly and in full before disagreeing with it, and
concedes what he is not arguing so the disagreement lands narrowly. "Underrates"
is a measured verb: he is adjusting an estimate, and the reader can see the
exact size of the claim he is making. The concession about Nadella keeps him
from overreaching for a stronger claim than he needs.

> "When people point to a long list of failures like Bing, Zune, Windows Phone,
> and HoloLens as evidence that Ballmer was some kind of buffoon who was
> holding Microsoft back, this demonstrates a lack of understanding of the tech
> industry. This is like pointing to a list of failed companies a VC has funded
> as evidence the VC doesn't know what they're doing. But that's silly in a
> hits based industry like venture capital. If you want to claim the VC is bad,
> you need to point out poor total return or a lack of big successes, which
> would imply poor total return."

He shows why a pile of failures is weak evidence by supplying the standard the
evidence should be judged against, total return in a business where a few big
wins pay for many losses. The analogy to venture capital does the teaching, and
the reader comes away able to weigh the same evidence on their own. The
criterion is set out before the verdict, so the judgment is earned.

> "As criticisms go, I think the former criticism is correct, but not an
> indictment of Ballmer unless you expect CEOs to be infallible, so as evidence
> that Ballmer was a bad CEO, this would be a very weak criticism. And it's not
> clear that the latter criticism is correct."

He grants that one criticism is correct, then measures how much it actually
damages his case, which is little, while flagging that the other criticism may
not hold at all. Conceding the valid point and then weighing its force is a
writer keeping score honestly rather than defensively. The distinctions are
fine: a correct-but-weak objection is handled differently from a possibly-wrong
one.
