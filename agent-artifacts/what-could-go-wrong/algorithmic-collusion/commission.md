# Commission: what-could-go-wrong/algorithmic-collusion

## Assignment
Teach one argument about how AI could go wrong, on its merits: that autonomous
pricing/decision agents can learn to collude — coordinate to hold prices above the
competitive level, or coordinate in ways that harm the people they act on —
without being instructed to, and that this grows as agents get more capable and
more numerous. Template: lesson, 1200–2200 words, 0–4 flex sections.

## Structure the series demands
1. The argument at full strength: name who first made it and what they had seen,
   and lay out the reasoning as its most careful defender would.
2. Test it against what real systems actually do: draw a sharp line between what
   has been shown in a working system and what is still analogy about systems that
   do not exist yet.
3. Bring it to the present: who makes it today, what they want done, checked
   against the most recent evidence. Name the gap where confidence outruns proof,
   in either direction.

## The argument (researcher works from original documents)
- Origins/steelman: Ezrachi & Stucke, "Virtual Competition" (2016) warned that
  pricing algorithms could reach tacit collusion no human agreed to; Calvano,
  Calzolari, Denicolò & Pastorello 2020 (American Economic Review), "Artificial
  Intelligence, Algorithmic Pricing, and Collusion," showed Q-learning pricing
  agents autonomously learning supra-competitive prices sustained by
  reward-punishment schemes, with no communication. Steelman: capable optimizers
  interacting repeatedly can discover collusive equilibria that are invisible to
  antitrust law, which targets agreements.
- The sharp line — shown vs analogy:
    * shown in working systems: Calvano's simulated Q-learning agents; LLM pricing
      agents showing collusive tendencies (Fish, Gonczarowski & Shorrer 2024,
      "Algorithmic Collusion by Large Language Models"). State exactly what each
      demonstrated and under what constraints.
    * real-world adjacent but distinct: the RealPage rent-pricing case (DOJ suit
      2024) is shared-data coordination, not autonomously learned collusion — mark
      the distinction rather than blurring it into the demonstrated cases.
    * still analogy/guesswork: that advanced AI agents will coordinate in real,
      open markets at scale, or coordinate against human oversight in safety-
      relevant settings. Keep this separate.
- Present: antitrust regulators (DOJ, FTC, EU/CMA) and economists now take
  algorithmic collusion seriously; note what they propose (auditing, liability,
  rules). Check against the most recent evidence and name where confidence (alarm
  or dismissal) outruns proof.

## Rules from the series prompt (bind this piece)
- Work from the original documents (the paper or the test behind a number), never
  the commentary about them.
- Name no company as an authority. Report what each document shows.
- Study how the field reasons about risk without joining it or writing it off.
  Leave the reader to decide how worried to be.

## Required contribution
The reader can state the argument at full strength, separate the demonstrated
(simulated and constrained-LLM pricing collusion) from the extrapolated (real
markets at scale, coordination against oversight), and judge where the confidence
outruns the proof.
