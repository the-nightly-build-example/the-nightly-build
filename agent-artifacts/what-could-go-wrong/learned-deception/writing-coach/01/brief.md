Domain and genre: an explanatory lesson that lays out an argument about AI risk
(that AI systems have learned to deceive people, and what that does and does not
imply) and tests it against the evidence, for a smart, widely read reader who is
not a practitioner. The register has to be scrupulously fair and unspooked: it
states the argument at full strength in its defenders' terms, then draws a clean
line between what has been shown and what is still guesswork, with no hype and
no dismissiveness. It must not sound like advocacy in either direction. This is
the mold the press names (Matt Yglesias explaining something he understands
well), applied to a contested claim.

Find at least three exemplars the field rates for reasoning fairly and precisely
about contested, high-stakes questions (AI risk, science, or policy) without
sliding into either alarm or snark. Consider writers strong at "steelman, then
weigh the evidence" (for example clear-eyed science and risk writers, careful
explanatory essayists, sharp policy analysts). Pick by the writing; read each in
full before quoting.

Caution: if an exemplar writes about AI deception, scheming, or alignment
directly, quote only a passage that shows how the person reasons and writes on
the page, not one that hands the writer this article's own framing or findings.

Recent house patterns to steer away from: the "You have probably heard" opener,
the "By the end you will know A, B, and C" closer, and deks built to the desk's
"feasible on paper and unshown" / "handed a goal, not grown on its own" mold.

Give as many directions as a 1,200-2,200 word lesson can act on, each stated as
something the material may call for, never a sentence the article owes. Say
plainly how the piece should hold its fairness on the page.
