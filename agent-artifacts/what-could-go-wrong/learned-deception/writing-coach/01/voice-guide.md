# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong / learned-deception

## Timothy B. Lee, "I don't think we are close to 'AI scientists'"

Source: https://www.understandingai.org/p/i-dont-think-we-are-close-to-ai-scientists

> "Recently, there has been a lot of excitement about AI agents like Claude Code and OpenClaw. Much of the hype is justified. Claude Code really is revolutionizing computer programming, and agents like OpenClaw very well might transform other parts of the economy and our daily lives."

Checked: https://www.understandingai.org/p/i-dont-think-we-are-close-to-ai-scientists, retrieved 2026-09-26
He credits the strong case in specifics, naming the actual products and what each one does, before he turns against any part of it. Nothing here is thrown-away enthusiasm; each claim is something a reader could go check.

> "Some people expect this (or similar breakthroughs by rivals) to set off a recursive self-improvement loop that radically accelerates scientific and technological progress. That might happen eventually, but I think it will take a while."

Checked: https://www.understandingai.org/p/i-dont-think-we-are-close-to-ai-scientists, retrieved 2026-09-26
He states the boldest form of the claim he's about to push back on (an industry leader's own timeline) without shrinking it first, and then answers it in one plain sentence. No stacked qualifiers, no "to be fair" — the turn is nine words and it's a direct disagreement, not a hedge.

> "My explicit beliefs — things I'm able to articulate in conversation or write down in an email — are the tip of an iceberg. Below the water line is a much larger set of hunches, vague associations, and half-formed theories. Because this stuff is implicit, it can't easily be transferred to another person. But it's essential for me to do my job well."

Checked: https://www.understandingai.org/p/i-dont-think-we-are-close-to-ai-scientists, retrieved 2026-09-26
The one image in the passage (iceberg) is doing real explanatory work — it names a distinction (explicit vs. implicit knowledge) the rest of the piece depends on — and he immediately cashes it out in his own twenty-year work history rather than leaving it to float as decoration.

## Kelsey Piper, "After 7 years at Vox's Future Perfect, here's what I think my journalism achieved"

Source: https://www.vox.com/future-perfect/421925/future-perfect-retrospective-leaving-journalism-impact

> "Over the past few weeks, I've been reading a lot of our old articles, asking myself, what holds up? What did we do best? What did we get wrong?"

Checked: https://www.vox.com/future-perfect/421925/future-perfect-retrospective-leaving-journalism-impact, retrieved 2026-09-26
Three plain questions in a row, each one doing a different job (durability, quality, error), and she answers none of them yet. The rhythm comes from the questions being genuinely different from each other, not from repetition.

> "It's a sober sort of accounting, because while I think we got a lot of stuff right that no one else did — our 2018 and 2019 coverage of the importance of preventing the next pandemic holds up particularly well — I am never quite sure if it mattered. The way that we learned we were right, after all, is that a pandemic happened, killing millions and devastating our world in a way that will take a long time to recover from."

Checked: https://www.vox.com/future-perfect/421925/future-perfect-retrospective-leaving-journalism-impact, retrieved 2026-09-26
She ties her own track record to the actual cost that proved it, not to credit. Being right is stated as a fact and then immediately weighed against what it cost to find out, which keeps the sentence from reading as self-congratulation.

> "I take comfort in the fact that, as grim as the world seems today, along every single dimension I lose sleep over, it has been worse before. Government corruption and political weaponization of the Department of Justice has been worse. Child mortality and the toll of infectious disease has been much worse. Even the blatantly stupid flirtation with annihilation, which I fear characterizes our current approach to AI, has been worse — it's hard to surpass the recklessness of the nuclear arms race early in the Cold War. We are not uniquely doomed any more than humans have ever been."

Checked: https://www.vox.com/future-perfect/421925/future-perfect-retrospective-leaving-journalism-impact, retrieved 2026-09-26
Four specific, checkable comparisons come before the flat closing sentence, so the plainness of "we are not uniquely doomed" is earned by what precedes it rather than asserted on its own. Note this passage names AI only as one item in a list about historical scale — it is not her reasoning about the deception question this article covers, and nothing about that question should be taken from it.

## Scott Alexander, "Ivermectin: Much More Than You Wanted To Know"

Source: https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/ivermectin-much-more-than-you-wanted

> "But here's my pitch: this is one of the most carefully-pored-over scientific issues of our time. Dozens of teams published studies saying ivermectin definitely worked. Then most scientists concluded it didn't. What a great opportunity to exercise our study-analyzing muscles!"

Checked: https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/ivermectin-much-more-than-you-wanted, retrieved 2026-09-26
He states the flat contradiction — dozens of studies said yes, then most scientists said no — as the reason the question is worth a close look, not as ammunition for either side. The contradiction itself is the hook, before he has taken a position.

> "If you see one green box, it could mean the ivermectin group just got a little luckier than the placebo group. When you see 26 boxes compared to only 4 red ones, you know that nobody gets that lucky."

Checked: https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/ivermectin-much-more-than-you-wanted, retrieved 2026-09-26
He gives the reader the arithmetic that would make the strong case convincing — one green box proves nothing, 26 against 4 does — before he starts subtracting anything from it. The strong case gets to be genuinely strong for a sentence before it gets tested.

> "We've gone from 29 studies to 11, getting rid of 18 along the way. For the record, we eliminated 2/19 for fraud, 1/19 for severe preregistration violations, 10 for methodological problems, and 6 because Meyerowitz-Katz was suspicious of them."

Checked: https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/ivermectin-much-more-than-you-wanted, retrieved 2026-09-26
Every discount gets an exact count and a named reason, so a reader could rebuild the tally and check it instead of taking the conclusion on trust. This is the passage's whole method in two sentences: nothing is thrown out without saying how many and why.

Note on this exemplar: the piece this article covers is unrelated (ivermectin, not AI), chosen deliberately so its method of steelmanning-then-discounting could be shown without also importing any position on AI risk.

## How this piece should sound

This is a lesson that tests one claim — that AI systems have learned to deceive people — the way Lee tests a claim about AI scientists and Alexander tests a claim about a drug: state the strong case in a form its own believers would recognize, then find out how much of it survives contact with what has actually been shown. The three exemplars above were picked because each one does that turn without raising its voice in either direction, and a smart reader who has never worked in a lab or a lab-adjacent job should be able to follow every step.

Name who first made the argument and what specific thing they had seen, in the manner Lee names Altman's own stated date rather than a vague "some people think AI will..." A claim is more crediting when it's attached to a person and a document than when it floats. The same goes for the evidence tested against it: where a result is cited, name the system, the setup, and what was actually observed, the way Lee names Claude Code and OpenClaw rather than "AI agents" in general, and the way Alexander names which specific study got thrown out and why.

Where the piece turns from stating the strong case to testing it, do the turn in one plain sentence rather than a run of hedges. Lee's "That might happen eventually, but I think it will take a while" disagrees with an industry leader's own timeline without a single qualifier stacked in front of it. A lesson that spends three sentences signaling that it is about to disagree loses the reader before it disagrees.

The house distinction this desk draws — what a system has done in a demonstrated setup against what it might do once given more autonomy or a longer run, and what a system was explicitly told to do against what it did without being told — is exactly the kind of distinction Alexander's tally of "2/19 for fraud, 1/19 for preregistration violations, 10 for methodological problems" is built to hold up under: a reader can check each count. Wherever this lesson draws its own version of that line, give the reader the count and the reason for each piece of evidence it keeps or discounts, not a summary verdict on the pile.

Confidence, where the piece states it, should attach to a specific fact rather than to the writer's own care in reasoning — Piper's "the way we learned we were right… is that a pandemic happened" ties being right to a cost, not to credit. A sentence that rates its own carefulness rates nothing a reader can check; a sentence that names what was measured, what wasn't, and what would settle the remaining question does.

Technical vocabulary from this field — the terms for how these systems are trained, tested, and observed to behave — should enter exactly as the lesson template requires: defined in plain words the sentence it first appears in, and never re-explained after that. None of the three exemplars needed decoration to carry a hard claim; the difficulty in this lesson is the subject, not the sentences describing it.
