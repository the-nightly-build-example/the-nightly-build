# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/normal-accidents

## How this piece should sound

This is a lesson that presents normal-accident theory at full strength, tests it
against what real systems do, and then decides how much of it survives. The
register is calm exposition. The reader is smart and new to Charles Perrow's
argument, and the whole value of the piece is that they can weigh it on its
merits, so the writing has to stay level whether it is making the case for the
theory or against it.

Perrow's two axes are the load-bearing terms, and they are abstractions:
interactive complexity, where parts affect each other in ways operators cannot
foresee, and tight coupling, where there is no slack, so a fault propagates
before anyone can step in. Schneier's feeling-versus-reality passage shows what
it looks like to set a distinction like that in the plainest available words and
trust it to carry, and his bulletproof-vest example shows pinning an abstract
claim to a case the reader can turn over in their hand. The piece has room to
introduce each axis the same way: state it plainly, then ground it in a worked
instance before leaning on it again.

The desk opens the argument the way its most careful defender would. Alexander's
two moves are available here: tightening a claim into the more exact version he
actually means, and voicing the skeptical objection inside his own prose rather
than saving it for later. Both bear on the stretch where the reader should
understand why serious people find Perrow persuasive, and again on the High
Reliability Organization critics, who argued that some complex, tightly coupled
systems run safely for decades. Their case gets its full strength before it is
weighed.

The line the piece turns on is between what has already been shown in a working
system and what is still analogy about systems that do not exist yet. Schneier
saying flatly what his material does not settle, and Luu holding to what the
incident record actually shows against the story that traveled, both model
marking that line in the same voice used for the evidence itself. Tightly coupled
automated failures that ran faster than intervention are a matter of record;
catastrophic normal accidents in agentic AI infrastructure are still guesswork,
and where a present-day proponent's or a critic's confidence outruns the proof,
the passages show naming that gap plainly rather than smoothing it.

Some of the incidents in view, the 2010 Flash Crash among them, reach the reader
as half-remembered headlines. This lesson links flash-crash-risk and
algorithmic-monoculture instead of re-reporting them, and Luu's cottage-industry
passage bears on the difference between the precise record and the version that
went viral. The takeaway is where the lesson lands its judgment on how worried
the reader should be. Alexander's refusal of a tidy resolution, settling for what
a reader can actually do with the argument, is available there: a verdict earned
from the evidence, held to the weight the evidence carries.

## Bruce Schneier, "The Psychology of Security (Part 1)"

Source: https://www.schneier.com/essays/archives/2008/01/the_psychology_of_se.html

> "Or, more generally, you can be secure even though you don't feel secure. And you can feel secure even though you're not. The feeling and reality of security are certainly related to each other, but they're just as certainly not the same as each other. We'd probably be better off if we had two different words for them."

Schneier sets one distinction and states it in words a reader keeps: the feeling
of security and the reality of it can come apart, and either can be present
without the other. He cares more about the reader holding the idea than about
sounding authoritative, which is why he can afford to admit the language is short
a word for what he means.

> "It makes no sense to just look at security in terms of effectiveness. "Is this effective against the threat?" is the wrong question to ask. You need to ask: "Is it a good trade-off?" Bulletproof vests work well, and are very effective at stopping bullets. But for most of us, living in lawful and relatively safe industrialized countries, wearing one is not a good trade-off."

An abstract claim, that security is a trade-off rather than a matter of pure
effectiveness, gets pinned to a concrete case the reader can weigh: a vest that
works and is still not worth wearing. Schneier is visible in the choice to test
the idea against an ordinary object instead of restating it in more abstract
terms.

> "But most of the research is anecdotal, and sometimes the results seem to contradict each other. I would be interested in seeing not only studies about particular heuristics and when they come into play, but how people deal with instances of contradictory heuristics."

Here Schneier marks the limit of his own material. He says what the research does
not settle, and what he would still want to see, in the same flat voice he uses
for what it does show. His judgment stays inside what he has actually read.

## Scott Alexander, "Beware The Man Of One Study"

Source: https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/12/12/beware-the-man-of-one-study/

> "So it's not so much "beware the man of one study" as "beware the man of any number of studies less than a relatively complete and not-cherry-picked survey of the research"."

Alexander revises his own title inside the sentence, tightening a catchy line
into the more careful claim he actually means. The correction is visible on the
page, and it lands the real standard: a single study, or a handful, is not a
survey of the evidence.

> "At some point in their education, most smart people usually learn not to credit arguments from authority. If someone says "Believe me about the minimum wage because I seem like a trustworthy guy," most of them will have at least one neuron in their head that says "I should ask for some evidence". If they're really smart, they'll use the magic words "peer-reviewed experimental studies.""

The paragraph teaches by staging a small exchange: someone asking to be believed,
and the reader's one skeptical neuron answering back. Alexander builds the point
from a concrete scene rather than asserting it, and the light handling keeps a
passage about being fooled from lecturing the reader.

> "Most important, even if someone gives you what seems like overwhelming evidence in favor of a certain point of view, don't trust it until you've done a simple Google search to see if the opposite side has equally overwhelming evidence."

After pages of showing how one-sided evidence can look complete, Alexander
declines a tidy resolution and gives the reader a plain, checkable habit instead.
The restraint is where his point of view shows: he offers what a reader can
actually do rather than a grander answer he does not have.

## Dan Luu, "A decade of major cache incidents at Twitter"

Source: https://danluu.com/cache-incidents/

> "There are a couple reasons we want to write this down. First, historical knowledge about what happens at tech companies is lost at a fairly high rate and we think it's nice to preserve some of it. Second, we think it can be useful to look at incidents and reliability from a specific angle, putting all of the information into one place, because that can sometimes make some patterns very obvious."

Luu opens by saying plainly why the piece exists, in two numbered reasons with no
throat-clearing before them. The writing trusts that a clear statement of purpose
is worth more than an introduction that performs its own importance.

> "Another is that there's a cottage industry of former directors / VPs who tell self-aggrandizing stories about all the great things they did that, to put it mildly, frequently distort the truth (although there's nothing stopping ICs from doing this, the most spread false stories we see tend to come from people on the management track). In both cases, there's a kind of Gresham's law of stories in play, where incorrect stories tend to win out over correct stories."

Luu explains why the loudest account of a failure is usually the wrong one, and
does it without heat: "to put it mildly" is as far as the verdict goes. The
Gresham's-law framing names the pattern precisely, and the parenthetical checks
his own claim before a reader can raise the same objection.

> "That knowledge decays at a high rate isn't unique to Twitter. In fact, of all the companies I've worked at as a full-time employee, I think Twitter is the best at preserving knowledge."

Luu makes a comparative claim and grounds it at once in his own work history
rather than leaving it as a general impression. The voice is measured: the
company outsiders mock for chaos was, by his direct experience, the best of them
at this, and he says so without dressing the surprise up.
