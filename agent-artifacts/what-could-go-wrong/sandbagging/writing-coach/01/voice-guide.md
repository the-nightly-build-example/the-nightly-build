# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/sandbagging (01)

Write calm, plain, and teacherly, the house register. What this lesson adds
to the default is a discipline the default does not carry: it runs two voices
and must keep them apart. One voice makes the worry at full conviction. The
other reports what real experiments show and files each result. The reader
should be able to tell, sentence by sentence, which voice is talking, and
should finish able to sort any "the model is hiding its abilities" claim into
worry, demonstrated result, or speculation. That sorting is the article's job,
so the sentences do the sorting, not a summary that sorts for the reader.

Two moves change most sentences here.

First, keep the case-maker's claim and the evidence's verdict on separate
sentences. When the worry is stated at strength, let it stand at strength;
do not defuse it mid-clause with a hedge, and do not blur it into the writer's
own view. The bounding is a later sentence, not a qualifier smuggled into this
one. A steelman that flinches while it speaks has not steelmanned.

Second, report every result with the condition that produced it in the same
breath. Prompted, fine-tuned, given a target score, told it was under test:
the setup is where this argument turns, and a finding stripped of its setup
overclaims by omission. Name what was done to the model before you name what
the model did.

Proportion the verb to the evidence in both directions. "Shown" for what a
working system did; a plainer, weaker verb for what is still analogy. Name the
gap where confidence outruns proof, whether the confidence is alarm or
dismissal. Neither leaks if every claim wears its evidence.

## Licenses

form: the advocate's full-strength sentence
move: Carlsmith and Karnofsky state the alarming inference in the case-maker's
      own confidence, plainly, without hedging each clause, and mark whose
      inference it is by the surrounding structure rather than by softening the
      words.
bar:  the sentence stays only if the structure around it makes clear it is the
      argument being built, not the lesson's own conclusion. If a reader could
      lift the sentence and quote it as the article's verdict, reframe it or
      cut it.

form: the epistemic-status tag
move: Piper separates one category of claim from another in a short plain
      clause ("an entirely different category of claim"); Karnofsky marks "when
      and if." A brief tag files a claim as demonstrated, induced, or unproven.
bar:  each tag must reclassify a specific claim the reader would otherwise
      misfile. A tag that restates a status already obvious from the sentence
      is filler and is cut.

form: defensive quotation on an intent-carrying verb
move: Karnofsky sets "aims" and "defeat" in quotation marks to flag that the
      word is doing metaphorical work the argument has not yet earned.
bar:  quotation marks appear at first use only, and only on a word whose plain
      use would assert the very intent the lesson calls unproven (a model that
      "chooses" to fail, that "hides", that "knows" it is tested). Decorative
      or repeated scare-quotes are cut; once flagged, the word runs plain.

## Joe Carlsmith, "Existential Risk from Power-Seeking AI (shorter version)"
Source: https://joecarlsmith.com/2023/03/22/existential-risk-from-power-seeking-ai-shorter-version/
Craft:
- cadence: measured, conditional clauses anchored by short declaratives; the
  rhythm never accelerates into rush, so the calm itself reads as seriousness.
- argument: decomposes the worry into named steps and treats each separately,
  so agreeing with step one commits the reader to nothing downstream.
- evidence: attaches an explicit confidence to each step rather than pooling
  everything into one verdict ("I think: greater than 10%").
- stance: takes the worry seriously without performing alarm; the restraint is
  what makes the concern land.
- notice: incentive structure and the difficulty of control, not the dramatic
  outcome.
- diction: modal verbs carry the calibration ("plausible", "quite plausible"),
  each chosen for a different weight, not for variety.
- reader: addressed as a peer who can follow a chain of conditionals and hold
  a probability without panicking.
- the important move: he ranks his own conviction out loud and keeps the
  ranking separate from the claim, so the reader always knows how hard he is
  standing behind each sentence.

## Holden Karnofsky, "Why Would AI 'Aim' To Defeat Humanity?"
Source: https://www.cold-takes.com/why-would-ai-aim-to-defeat-humanity/
Craft:
- cadence: short claim, then two or three sentences of plain elaboration; a
  deliberate, unhurried pace built by accumulated qualification.
- argument: names every assumption upfront and reasons downward from them, so
  the reader is never trapped by an unstated premise.
- evidence: familiar examples (game-playing systems) carry unfamiliar claims;
  a concrete scenario stands in for an abstract mechanism.
- stance: serious about catastrophe, allergic to performance; ranks his own
  conviction section by section ("less so than the rest").
- notice: the difference between what today's systems do and what future ones
  might, marked with temporal words rather than asserted.
- diction: Anglo-Saxon over Latinate, "aim" over "objective"; understatement
  as the default setting.
- reader: direct address, good faith assumed, technical background not; every
  necessary term defined in plain words as it enters.
- the important move: quotation marks around a loaded word ("aims", "defeat")
  flag that the metaphor is doing work the argument has not yet earned, so the
  word cannot smuggle intent past the reader.

## Kelsey Piper, "AI's biggest critic has lost the plot"
Source: https://www.theargumentmag.com/p/ais-biggest-critic-has-lost-the-plot
Craft:
- cadence: plain reportorial sentences, then a sharp short line that draws the
  distinction the paragraph was building toward.
- argument: separates what was claimed from what actually happened, and one
  category of claim from another (financial trouble is not fraud), so the
  reader sees exactly which claim the evidence touches.
- evidence: reports a finding neutrally, then marks the line between the
  finding and what observers extract from it.
- stance: non-alarmist and non-dismissive at once; refuses hype and refuses
  reflexive skepticism, asking instead for better skepticism.
- notice: the mundane, checkable case over the dramatic one, which keeps the
  claim anchored to what can be verified.
- diction: concrete and unshowy; the strong word arrives only after the
  evidence for it.
- reader: assumed intelligent and in good faith, invited into the analysis
  rather than steered toward a camp.
- the important move: she states plainly what a result does not show, in the
  same passage that reports what it does, so the reader leaves able to sort the
  next claim themselves.

## Self-test

The house default already gives plain claims, short sentences, concrete
stakes, and no hype or doom. It does not, on its own, tell the writer how to
hold an argument at full strength without adopting it, how to report a scary
experiment without letting its setup drop, or how to hand the reader the
apparatus to classify claims instead of classifying for them. That is what
this guide adds: a lesson that inhabits the worry honestly, bounds it by
attaching evidence to every claim rather than by hedging, and keeps the
advocate's voice and the evidence's verdict on separate, distinguishable
lines. A writer who followed only the default would produce a competent
explainer; this guide asks for a controlled steelman.
