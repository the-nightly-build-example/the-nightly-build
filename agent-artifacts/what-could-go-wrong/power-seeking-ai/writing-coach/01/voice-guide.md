# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/power-seeking-ai (01)

## How this piece should sound

This lesson has one job Carlsmith's report itself performs: turn a chain of
premises into a number, and let the reader watch the arithmetic happen. Harford
builds his lockdown estimate the same way, one round figure at a time, until a
fatality-rate assumption becomes a dollar figure the reader can weigh. Do that
with the premises: name each one, give Carlsmith's own stated probability for
it, and show what multiplying them actually produces, the way Harford's "you
don't need a complex model to predict" paragraph turns an assumption into 2
million deaths and a $20 trillion figure without hiding a step.

When the piece reaches the conjunctive-versus-disjunctive critique, take
Harford's other move: name exactly where a calculation could break, in the
calculation's own terms, and then let the number stand as what it actually is.
"One could quibble with every step of this calculation" is followed by which
steps, not a retreat from the number. The critique here has a specific target
too: the premises may not be as independent as multiplying them assumes. It
deserves that same precision rather than a general gesture at uncertainty.

Carlsmith's revision from roughly 5% to greater than 10% is the kind of thing
Silver's piece is built to explain correctly: a probability is not a
deterministic claim, and a change in a stated number does not by itself mean
the world changed or the reasoning was wrong. Whatever the piece says about the
revision, it should say plainly what moved (which premise, whose review) rather
than treating the new figure as either an admission or a vindication. When a
number needs grounding, do it the way Silver grounds a margin of error: a full
worked example with real figures, not a gesture at "some uncertainty."

Levine's register is the one for testing the method itself. He states a claim
plainly, registers mild doubt without mockery, and then explains the real
principle in one clean sentence tied to a concrete quantity. That is the
posture for weighing whether multiplying probabilities that may not be
independent licenses the headline figure the way multiplying independent ones
would. It is skeptical of the specific move, not of the person making it, and
willing to say exactly what the number does and does not license once the
explaining is done.

The reader is smart and has no codebase; probability itself needs no
introduction, so nothing here should slow down to re-explain a percentage. What
it should slow down for is Carlsmith's own terms the moment each appears:
conjunctive, disjunctive, existential catastrophe, defined in the sentence
that uses them, the way Levine defines the Kelly criterion in the sentence that
names it. And where the piece lands a verdict on the method or the number, it
can afford Harford's directness: state what is clear, once it has been earned,
rather than close the desk's recent run of pieces on a hedge about how thin the
evidence is. This one has a real number and a stated method; the honest ending
says what happened to them, not how worried to be.

## Tim Harford, "How do we value a statistical life?"

Source: https://timharford.com/2020/04/how-do-we-value-a-statistical-life/
(first published in the Financial Times, 3 April 2020)

> "Implicitly, we constantly weigh up small risks of death and decide if they
> are worth it. Despite inconsistencies and blind spots in our behaviour, we
> value reducing risks to our own lives very highly, but not infinitely so. We
> vote for governments that hold our lives in similarly high regard. For
> example, the US Environmental Protection Agency values a statistical life at
> nearly $10m in today's money, or $10 per micromort averted."

Harford anchors an abstract idea, the value of a statistical life, to a real
government figure the moment he states it. He does not define "statistical
life" and then separately illustrate it; the definition and the number arrive
in the same breath, so the abstraction never floats free of something the
reader can check.

> "You don't need a complex epidemiological model to predict that if we take
> no serious steps to halt the spread of the virus, more than half the world is
> likely to contract it. That suggests 2m US deaths and 500,000 in Britain —
> assuming, again, a 1 per cent fatality rate. If an economic lockdown in the
> US saves most of these lives, and costs less than $20tn, then it would seem
> to be value for money. (By way of comparison, each 20 per cent loss of gross
> domestic product for a quarter represents a cost of about $1tn.)"

This is a chain of assumptions turned into a headline number in view of the
reader: a fatality rate becomes a death count, the death count becomes a dollar
figure, and the dollar figure gets a comparison the reader already holds
(GDP loss per quarter). Nothing is asserted that the previous sentence did not
supply.

> "One could quibble with every step of this calculation. Perhaps some of
> those who die were so ill that they would have died of other causes within
> days. Perhaps Covid-19 is not quite so dangerous. Yet it is clear that with
> so many lives at stake, we should be willing to pay huge costs to protect
> them."

Harford names the calculation's own weak points instead of a general disclaimer
about uncertainty, and he names them in the terms of the calculation itself
(the fatality rate, the cause of death). Having named them, he still commits to
a conclusion. The hedge and the verdict share the same paragraph.

## Matt Levine, "SBF Was Reckless From the Start"

Source: https://www.bloomberg.com/opinion/articles/2023-10-04/sbf-was-reckless-from-the-start
(Money Stuff, Bloomberg Opinion, 4 October 2023; quoted here from the verified
excerpt reproduced at https://www.actuarial.news/2023/10/04/sbf-was-reckless-from-the-start/)

> "First: 'A Jane Street intern had what amounted to a professional obligation
> to take any bet with a positive expected value'? Really? I feel like, if you
> are a trading intern, you are really there to learn two things. One is,
> sure, take bets with positive expected value and avoid bets with negative
> expected value."

Levine quotes the claim he is about to test, flags his doubt in one word, and
then states what he thinks the real lesson is, all before he has made a single
argument. The "Really?" is doing real work: it marks exactly where he starts to
disagree, rather than coloring the whole passage with sarcasm.

> "People have thought about this question! Like, this is very much a central
> thing that traders and trading firms worry about. The standard starting
> point is the Kelly criterion, which computes a maximum bet size based on your
> edge and the size of your bankroll."

He defines a technical term, the Kelly criterion, in the same sentence that
introduces it, and the definition is a plain description of what the thing
computes, not a metaphor for it. The two short sentences before the definition
are doing the work of a person catching themselves being surprised, in
real time.

> "Jane Street does not go around taking any bet with a positive expected
> value. The point of Kelly is not about utility curves; it's not 'having $200
> is less than twice as pleasant as having $100, so you should be less willing
> to take big risks for big rewards.' The point of Kelly is about maximizing
> your chances of surviving and obtaining long-run returns: it's 'if you bet
> 50% of your bankroll on 1%-edge bets, you'll be more likely to win each bet
> than lose it, but if you keep doing that you will probably lose all your
> money eventually.' Kelly is about sizing your bets so you can keep playing
> the game and make the most money possible in the long run. Betting more can
> make you more money in the short run, but if you keep doing it you will end
> in ruin."

Levine states what a concept is not before he states what it is, and both
halves are specific claims about bet sizing, not a rhetorical setup. The
paragraph ends on the plain consequence, ruin, stated once, without being
dressed up as a warning.

## Nate Silver, "Don't let randomness make a fool of you"

Source: https://www.natesilver.net/p/dont-let-randomness-make-a-fool-of
(Silver Bulletin, 19 September 2024)

> "And there was some reputational risk: if our forecast was 80/20 on Election
> Day and the 20 percent came up — as 20 percent probabilities have an
> uncanny knack for doing 20 percent of the time — it was going to negatively
> affect my life."

Silver states the exact thing a stated probability means, that the less
likely outcome will still happen one time in five, inside a sentence that is
ostensibly about his own career risk. The joke and the technical point are the
same sentence; neither is doing its work without the other.

> "People treat probabilistic predictions as deterministic ones, e.g. if Trump
> goes from a 48 percent chance of winning Wisconsin to a 52 percent chance,
> you'll get a lot of Nate Silver is calling Wisconsin for Trump!!! even though
> the forecast expresses a high degree of uncertainty and nothing in the model
> has really changed."

He names the specific misreading (a four-point shift in a stated probability
treated as a call) with a concrete example before naming what actually
happened underneath it (nothing). The mistake is shown, not just labeled.

> "A typical poll has about 800 respondents. Well, the margin of error in an
> 800-person poll is plus or minus 3.5 points. Except, that substantially
> understates the case because the margin of error pertains only to one
> candidate's vote share. In an election like this one where third-party
> candidates play little role, basically every vote that isn't a Harris vote
> is a Trump vote and vice versa. So the margin of error on the difference
> separating the candidates is roughly twice that: about 7 points."

Silver walks the arithmetic in full, including the correction most people
would skip (doubling the margin of error because two candidates' shares move
against each other), with a real sample size and real numbers at every step.
The reader never has to take the final figure, 7 points, on faith.
