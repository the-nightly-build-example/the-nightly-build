# Evidence: what-could-go-wrong/power-seeking-ai (round 01)

The evidence strongly supports the commissioned angle: Carlsmith's own report gives exact,
quotable premises, per-premise credences, and a self-authored revision note, all read in full from
the primary text (not summaries of it). The record also turns up something sharper than the
commission asked for: named reviewers do not converge on one objection to the six-premise
multiplication. Nate Soares argues the conjunctive structure mechanically biases the number
**down**; David Thorstad argues several individual premises are still far too generous and the
true number is many orders of magnitude **lower**; a panel of 21 superforecasters split the
difference in an unexpected way, giving *higher* credence than Carlsmith on the first three
premises and much *lower* credence on the last three, netting a lower headline number. This
three-way disagreement is real and citable, and it is more teachable than a flat "reviewers
pushed back." The weak point in the record is access: most of the ten individually named
reviewers Carlsmith collected are held in Google Docs that returned 401 Unauthorized on every
attempt, so the record can speak with full traceability for only three of the ten named
reviewers (Soares, Kokotajlo, Thorstad) plus the superforecaster panel as a group. That is
sufficient to source the conjunctive-probability beat honestly, but a writer should not attribute
specific arguments to Garfinkel, Aschenbrenner, Levinstein, Lifland, Nanda, Tarsney, or Wallace
beyond what Carlsmith's own compilation page says about them secondhand.

### Contradictions

- **Direction of the conjunctive-probability bias, disputed among the reviewers themselves.**
  Nate Soares argues the six-premise structure mechanically pushes the final number *down*,
  regardless of the premises' true correlation ("this will tend to drive our probability of any
  event happening to around 1 in 2^n"). David Thorstad, reviewing the same six premises, argues
  several of them are individually too generous and lands on a *far lower* number than
  Carlsmith's own (0.00002%, which he says was already too generous to the argument). These are
  not the same objection: Soares thinks the method understates the risk; Thorstad thinks the
  premises themselves overstate it. A lesson that flattens both into "reviewers thought the
  method was flawed" would misrepresent both.
- **The superforecaster panel disagreed with Carlsmith in both directions at once.** The 21
  superforecasters gave *higher* aggregate credence than Carlsmith on the first three premises
  (timelines, incentives, alignment difficulty) and *much lower* credence on the last three
  (high-impact power-seeking, disempowerment, catastrophe), netting an overall 1% versus
  Carlsmith's 5%. This means the panel's skepticism concentrates specifically in the
  disempowerment-and-catastrophe end of the argument -- the same end Thorstad independently
  targets in Part 8 -- rather than in the "will we even build such systems" end.
  (joecarlsmith.com/2023/10/18/superforecasting-the-premises...)
- **Carlsmith's own headline number moved without a re-run of the multiplication.** The 2021
  report's ~5% comes from an explicit product of six stated credences (65% x 80% x 40% x 65% x
  40% x 95% ~= 5.1%). The May 2022 revision to >10% is a personal update Carlsmith states in a
  one-sentence author's note; the report gives no revised per-premise table to justify it, and
  his own Oct 2023 post shows he still had not, by that point, revised the six numbers to match
  either the >10% figure or the superforecasters' input. A writer should present >10% as
  Carlsmith's stated current credence, not as the output of an updated version of the six-premise
  calculation, because no such updated calculation exists in the record.
- **Carlsmith explicitly declined to defer to the superforecasters**, despite their having more
  formal forecasting credentials than any individual reviewer: "My engagement thus far with the
  written arguments in the reviews... hasn't moved me much," and he states he does not fully
  trust "raw superforecaster numbers... absent object-level arguments I find persuasive." This
  matters for the "who advances this framing now" beat: Carlsmith continues to hold, and has
  raised, his own number after seeing organized disagreement from people whose calibration on
  other questions is unusually well tested.
