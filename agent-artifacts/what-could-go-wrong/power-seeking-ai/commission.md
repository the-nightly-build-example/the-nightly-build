# Commission: what-could-go-wrong/power-seeking-ai

## The assignment

Teach the argument that advanced AI poses an existential risk because it would seek
power, as it was assembled and quantified in Joseph Carlsmith's report "Is
Power-Seeking AI an Existential Risk?" (2021, revised 2022). The lesson teaches one
argument so the reader can judge it on its merits. Lesson template. No code.

The reader is the paper's declared reader: smart, widely read, no codebase.
Probability is assumable; teach the rest. Leave the reader able to weigh the argument,
not told what to conclude.

## Why this argument, tonight

What Could Go Wrong has taught the mechanism-level pieces of the power-seeking case
(instrumental-convergence, orthogonality-thesis, mesa-optimization,
deceptive-alignment, the-off-switch, treacherous-turn). What it has not taught is the
thing people actually cite: Carlsmith's report, which assembled those pieces into one
explicit chain of premises, assigned a probability to each, multiplied them into a
single number for catastrophe by 2070, and was then revised upward by its own author.
Teaching it gives the reader the canonical structured statement of the risk and, just
as important, a worked example of how the field reasons about risk with explicit
probabilities, which is exactly this desk's remit.

## The angle

This lesson is about the assembled, quantified argument and what happened to it, not a
re-derivation of instrumental convergence. Keep that boundary sharp.

- The argument at full strength. Name Carlsmith (then at Open Philanthropy) and the
  report. Lay out the six premises as he did (roughly: it will become possible and
  incentivized to build advanced, agentic, strategically aware planning systems; some
  such systems will be built with objectives that give them instrumental reason to seek
  power in unintended ways; that power-seeking will scale to permanently disempowering
  humanity; and this constitutes an existential catastrophe). Present each premise the
  way its most careful defender would, using the already-taught mechanism lessons as
  named, linked building blocks (instrumental-convergence for why capable planners seek
  power; deceptive-alignment / the-off-switch where they fit) rather than re-teaching
  them. The reader should understand why a serious researcher assigned this real weight.
- The number, and why the method matters. Carlsmith multiplied his per-premise
  probabilities to a headline figure for existential catastrophe from power-seeking AI
  by 2070, and stated it as roughly 5% in the 2021 report, then revised it to greater
  than 10% in a 2022 note. Give the actual figures and dates from the primary. Teach
  plainly what multiplying independent-looking premise probabilities does and does not
  license (the conjunctive-versus-disjunctive critique that reviewers raised), so the
  reader can see both why the method is honest and where it is contestable.
- Test it against what real systems show versus what is still analogy. Draw the sharp
  line the beat requires: which premises now have some empirical purchase (early,
  contested demonstrations of goal-guarding or specification-gaming or situational
  awareness in real models, which the neighboring lessons cover and which should be
  linked and characterized honestly as built or narrow) and which remain forecasts
  about systems that do not yet exist (durable, world-scale disempowerment). The case
  turns on that line.
- Bring it to the present. Who advances this framing now and what they want done, and
  how it has held up: the reviewers' responses collected with the report, Carlsmith's
  own upward revision, and where independent forecasts sit. Name the gap where
  confidence outruns proof, in either direction (doom or dismissal), without joining or
  writing off the argument. Name no company as an authority.

## Boundaries

- Work from the original document: Carlsmith's report and its published reviews, his
  2022 revision note, and the primary premise-lessons already in the library. Not
  commentary about the report.
- Do not re-teach instrumental convergence or orthogonality; link them. This lesson's
  own contribution is the structure, the probability method, and the revision.
- Steelman before weighing. Present the strongest form before any critique.
- No code. A small table laying out the premises with Carlsmith's stated probabilities
  is welcome furniture if every figure is sourced to the primary.

## Neighbors in this run

Publishing alongside wavenet, mean-average-precision, typo-robustness,
sports-illustrated-ai-authors. No overlap. The in-library neighbor to differentiate
from is instrumental-convergence (a mechanism lesson: four subgoals, four
experiments). This lesson is about Carlsmith's assembled probabilistic argument and its
revision. If the draft starts re-deriving convergence, it has drifted.

## Recent habits not to inherit

From the last several What Could Go Wrong lessons (normal-accidents, suffering-risks,
recommender-radicalization, deskilling, capability-elicitation, alignment-faking):

- The desk has a strong recent run of "the evidence is thinner than the claim" pieces
  whose deks land on "researchers concede X" or "rests on simulations / a built demo."
  This lesson steelmans a quantified forecast; do not force it into that debunking
  mold or borrow that dek clause. The honest finding here is about a probability and a
  method, not a demo that was rigged.
- The heading mold "The [noun] that [verb]" ("The cascade that actually happened",
  "The corner where accidents are normal") recurs. Vary construction.
- Do not close on a generic "how worried to be" moral. The takeaway names what the
  reader now knows about this specific argument and its number.

## Source policy

Floor: at least 8 sources total, at least 4 primary and at least 1 secondary. Primary
means Carlsmith's report, his 2022 revision, and the published reviews (each reviewer
is a primary for their own stated view). Contested figures (the probabilities) need the
primary. Meet the floor with sources that change the interpretation.
