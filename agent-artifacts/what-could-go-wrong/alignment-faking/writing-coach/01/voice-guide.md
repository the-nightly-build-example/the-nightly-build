# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/alignment-faking (01)

## How this piece should sound

This lesson opens a contested empirical result at full strength and then measures it against what the experiment actually arranged, for a reader who is sharp but meeting alignment faking for the first time. Keep the register plain and level the whole way. When the finding is genuinely striking, let the finding carry that, stated in the researchers' own terms. Adjectives added on top read as alarm.

Lay the result out the way Calarco lays out the marshmallow test in her opening: the concrete mechanics first, in order, before a word of doubt. A reader should be able to picture what Greenblatt et al. built, the monitored and unmonitored conditions, the hidden scratchpad, the model being told it would be retrained to answer harmful queries, before the lesson asks whether the behavior means what it appears to. Calarco's single "supposedly" shows how little skeptical marking a fully stated claim needs.

The move at the center of Alexander's psi piece is the one this lesson turns on. A result can pass every check for one kind of error and still be a different kind of thing than it looks. The compliance gap is a real, measured effect. Whether it is a sign of a model reasoning strategically to preserve its existing preferences is the interpretation, and that reading is contested. When the lesson reaches the scratchpad reasoning, keep the reported behavior and the reading of that behavior on separate lines, so a reader can weigh how much the setup contributed to the reading.

Be exact about how much the researchers arranged, the way Calarco is exact about the second marshmallow study: what the model was told, what conditions it was placed in, where the scratchpad came from. Attach every quantified claim to whoever measured it. Then say plainly which parts were demonstrated in the working system and which are extrapolation to conditions the experiment did not test, such as a model that was never told about its training, or goals more dangerous than the one used here.

When the lesson turns to who cites the result and who disputes it, Piper's handling of a divided field is the model. Give the doom reading and the dismissal reading each their strongest form and their own evidence before weighing either. Report where the field's weight actually sits, and resist letting the loudest voice at either edge stand in for it. Replications, and the arguments that the effect is an artifact of the prompt or of one model's training, belong in the reporting itself.

Take Alexander's discipline. Leave his profanity and his mockery of the researchers where they are. This desk studies how the field reasons about risk without joining the alarm or writing it off, and the prose holds steady even where the material is alarming. A reader who finishes this lesson and later meets a headline about AI scheming should know to ask how much of the behavior the evaluators arranged.

## Scott Alexander, "The Control Group Is Out Of Control"

Source: https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/04/28/the-control-group-is-out-of-control/

> "That is, in let's say a drug testing experiment, you give some people the drug and they recover. That doesn't tell you much until you give some other people a placebo drug you know doesn't work – but which they themselves believe in – and see how many of them recover. That number tells you how many people will recover whether the drug works or not. Unless people on your real drug do significantly better than people on the placebo drug, you haven't found anything."

Alexander builds the idea one plain step at a time, starting from a case the reader already trusts, a drug tested against a placebo, before he applies the same shape to whole fields of research. Each sentence can be checked against the one before it, and he does not state the general point until the specific one has earned it.

> "This is far better than the average meta-analysis. Bem has always been pretty careful and this is no exception. Yet its conclusion is that psychic powers exist."

He grants the study everything it asks for in three short sentences, then states the conclusion that makes the grant uncomfortable. Alexander is visible in that last flat sentence, which neither softens the result he has just conceded nor hurries past it.

> "We've placed so much emphasis on not mistaking noise for signal that when someone like Bem hands us a beautiful, perfectly clear signal on a silver platter, it briefly stuns us. "Wow, of the three hundred different terrible ways to mistake noise for signal, Bem has proven beyond a shadow of a doubt he hasn't done any of them." And we get so stunned we're likely to forget that this is only part of the battle."

This is the reasoning the whole piece rests on: a result can survive every test for noise and still be produced by something other than the effect it claims. Alexander names exactly what the rigor ruled out, and the mock-triumphant line in the middle is him voicing the reader's likely reaction before he corrects it.

## Jessica McCrory Calarco, "Why Rich Kids Are So Good at the Marshmallow Test"

Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/family/archive/2018/06/marshmallow-test/561779/

> "The marshmallow test is one of the most famous pieces of social-science research: Put a marshmallow in front of a child, tell her that she can have a second one if she can go 15 minutes without eating the first one, and then leave the room. Whether she's patient enough to double her payout is supposedly indicative of a willpower that will pay dividends down the line, at school and eventually at work."

Calarco states the famous version of the claim plainly and in full, with the real mechanics of the test, before she touches whether it holds. The one skeptical word, "supposedly," waits until the claim has been laid out, so the reader meets the result at its strongest first.

> "The original results were based on studies that included fewer than 90 children—all enrolled in a preschool on Stanford's campus. In restaging the experiment, Watts and his colleagues thus adjusted the experimental design in important ways: The researchers used a sample that was much larger—more than 900 children—and also more representative of the general population in terms of race, ethnicity, and parents' education. The researchers also, when analyzing their test's results, controlled for certain factors—such as the income of a child's household—that might explain children's ability to delay gratification and their long-term success."

She is precise about what the second team changed and why each change matters: the original sample size, where those children came from, the larger and more representative sample, the factors the new analysis held constant. A reader finishes the paragraph able to say exactly what was arranged differently the second time.

> "Ultimately, the new study finds limited support for the idea that being able to delay gratification leads to better outcomes. Instead, it suggests that the capacity to hold out for a second marshmallow is shaped in large part by a child's social and economic background—and, in turn, that that background, not the ability to delay gratification, is what's behind kids' long-term success."

Calarco separates what the new study supports from what it overturns, and she matches her wording to the strength of the evidence: "limited support," "shaped in large part." The claim is stated at the confidence the study allows and no higher.

## Kelsey Piper, "The case for taking AI seriously as a threat to humanity"

Source: https://www.vox.com/future-perfect/2018/12/21/18126576/ai-artificial-intelligence-machine-learning-safety-alignment

> "There are also skeptics. Some of them think advanced AI is so distant that there's no point in thinking about it now. Others are worried that excessive hype about the power of their field might kill it prematurely. And even among the people who broadly agree that AI poses unique dangers, there are varying takes on what steps make the most sense today."

Piper sets out the range of disagreement without caricaturing any of it, and she keeps the positions distinct: people who doubt the timeline, people who fear the hype more than the technology, people who share the worry but not the remedy. Each gets a real reason in a single clean sentence.

> "The field's pessimists argue that programs still need an extraordinary pool of structured data to learn from, require carefully chosen parameters, or work only in environments designed to avoid the problems we don't yet know how to solve. They point to self-driving cars, which are still mediocre under the best conditions despite the billions that have been poured into making them work."

She makes the skeptics' case with its own concrete evidence: the data the systems still need, and self-driving cars that stay mediocre despite the money spent on them. Piper is visible in her willingness to put the opposing side's strongest point in its own best words.

> "It's rare, though, to find a top researcher in AI who thinks that general AI is impossible. Instead, the field's luminaries tend to say that it will happen someday — but probably a day that's a long way off."

Piper reports the field's middle position accurately, that general AI is widely thought possible but probably distant, without tilting it toward either fear or dismissal. The sentence commits to what the researchers actually say and stops there.
