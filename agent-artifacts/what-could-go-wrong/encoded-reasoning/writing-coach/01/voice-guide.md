# Voice guide: what-could-go-wrong/encoded-reasoning (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a lesson that has to do two jobs at once, and the writers below show it can be done without splitting the difference. It has to make a careful reader take seriously the worry that a model could bury its real reasoning in text that reads as ordinary, so that anyone checking the model's words for signs of trouble would see nothing wrong. And it has to show that same reader exactly how far the evidence for the worry actually reaches. Hold both in view. Do not let the fair hearing tip into alarm, and do not let the accounting of the evidence tip into dismissal.

Open the worry at full strength, in the terms the people who hold it would use, before a word of testing. Scott Alexander states the doom scenario in a single clean sentence and then says he will grant its assumptions. Holden Karnofsky, before defending his claim, fixes exactly what he means by it and rules out the softer readings a reader might have settled for. The claim about hidden reasoning deserves the same treatment: say plainly what it would mean for a model to keep its real reasoning out of the words an overseer reads, and say it well enough that a reader who came in skeptical understands why serious people are worried.

Attribute the worry to the people who actually hold it. Piper hands her reframing to a named researcher and reports the skeptics by their different reasons; Alexander lists the forecasters and their numbers before giving his own. When the article says who is worried and why, the reader can weigh the worry instead of weighing the article. The same care applies to whoever pushes back. Give the skeptic a real reason, the way Piper gives one skeptic the fear that hype will kill the field, a reason that has nothing to do with thinking the risk is small.

Where the case rests on something a working system has been shown to do, and where it rests on analogy about systems that do not exist yet, are different kinds of claim, and a reader should be able to tell which is which at every step. Alexander marks the exact point where his argument stops being settled and names the debate rather than closing it. Karnofsky labels his own scenario as speculative in his own voice, then says which smaller claim he will not give up. When the lesson reaches the crux the whole worry turns on, name it, and if the evidence does not settle it, say so rather than writing past it.

Prefer the premise that is hardest to dispute over the one that sounds most frightening. Karnofsky gives up the dramatic version of his argument and rests it on the weaker assumption, because he would rather win the smaller claim cleanly than leave a larger one open to dispute. If the point about hidden reasoning can be made without assuming the most capable system anyone has imagined, it is stronger for being made that way.

Keep the prose plain and concrete, and let the subject's own particulars carry the weight. None of these writers reaches for a grand word to signal that the subject is serious; in each piece the seriousness comes from stating the claim exactly and handling the evidence honestly. Where a figure or a real case would make an abstract point land, use it. And where the article is sure, it can say so. Alexander commits to a number while admitting he cannot fully defend it, which is what lets a reader trust him. Commit where the evidence lets you, and name the gap where confidence, whether doom or dismissal, has run ahead of what has been shown.

## Holden Karnofsky, "AI Could Defeat All Of Us Combined"

Source: https://www.cold-takes.com/ai-could-defeat-all-of-us-combined/

> "As a first step, this post will emphasize an unoriginal but extremely important point: the kind of AI I've discussed could defeat all of humanity combined, if (for whatever reason) it were pointed toward that goal. By "defeat," I don't mean "subtly manipulate us" or "make us less informed" or something like that - I mean a literal "defeat" in the sense that we could all be killed, enslaved or forcibly contained."

He fixes the exact meaning of his strong claim before defending it, and does it by ruling out the weaker readings a reader might have settled for. The plain "I mean a literal defeat" and the concrete list, killed, enslaved or forcibly contained, leave no room to quietly soften the claim into something safer. Karnofsky is visible in the refusal to let his own word do less work than he means by it.

> "But I want to be clear that I don't think the danger relies on the idea of "cognitive superpowers" or "superintelligence" - both of which refer to capabilities vastly beyond those of humans. I think we still have a problem even if we assume that AIs will basically have similar capabilities to humans, and not be fundamentally or drastically more intelligent or capable. I'll cover that next."

Karnofsky deliberately gives up the more dramatic version of the argument and rests his case on the weaker premise that AIs are only about as capable as people. Naming "cognitive superpowers" and "superintelligence" and then setting them aside shows a writer choosing the assumption that is hardest to dispute over the one that sounds most frightening. The move is his: he would rather carry a smaller claim cleanly than a larger one on contested ground.

> "It's necessarily speculative, and should be taken in the spirit of giving examples of how this might work - for me, the high-level concern is that a huge, coordinating population of AIs with similar capabilities to humans would be a threat to human civilization, and that we shouldn't count on any particular way of stopping it such as shutting down servers."

He marks the speculative part as speculative in his own voice, then says exactly which smaller claim he is not willing to give up. Separating "examples of how this might work" from "the high-level concern" lets a reader see where the concrete detail ends and the claim he actually relies on begins. Karnofsky shows here in his willingness to label his own guesswork before a critic can do it for him.

## Kelsey Piper, "The case for taking AI seriously as a threat to humanity"

Source: https://www.vox.com/future-perfect/2018/12/21/18126576/ai-artificial-intelligence-machine-learning-safety-alignment

> "Rosie Campbell at UC Berkeley's Center for Human-Compatible AI argues that these are examples, writ small, of the big worry experts have about general AI in the future. The difficulties we're wrestling with today with narrow AI don't come from the systems turning on us or wanting revenge or considering us inferior. Rather, they come from the disconnect between what we tell our systems to do and what we actually want them to do."

Piper hands the reframing to a named researcher rather than asserting it herself, and states it in flat, everyday words. She names the revenge-and-hostility picture a reader arrives with and then replaces it with the plain mechanism, so the correction lands against a belief the reader actually holds rather than one she invented to knock down. Her care to attribute, "Rosie Campbell ... argues," is how she keeps her own guess out of the reporting.

> "It's immediately clear how nuclear bombs will kill us. No one working on mitigating nuclear risk has to start by explaining why it'd be a bad thing if we had a nuclear war.
>
> The case that AI could pose an existential risk to humanity is more complicated and harder to grasp. So many of the people who are working to build safe AI systems have to start by explaining why AI systems, by default, are dangerous."

She anchors an unfamiliar risk to one the reader already understands, and then admits outright that the AI case is harder to explain. Conceding "more complicated and harder to grasp" costs her nothing and makes a reader more willing to follow the explanation that comes next. Piper is visible in the decision to name the difficulty of her own subject instead of talking past it.

> "There are also skeptics. Some of them think advanced AI is so distant that there's no point in thinking about it now. Others are worried that excessive hype about the power of their field might kill it prematurely. And even among the people who broadly agree that AI poses unique dangers, there are varying takes on what steps make the most sense today."

Piper lays out the people who disagree and gives each camp a real reason, including one, the fear that hype will kill the field, that has nothing to do with thinking the risk is small. Sorting the skeptics by motive rather than lumping them together is what keeps the piece from reading as advocacy. The evenness is hers: she reports the disagreement as carefully as she reports the worry.

## Scott Alexander, "Why I Am Not (As Much Of) A Doomer (As Some People)"

Source: https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/why-i-am-not-as-much-of-a-doomer

> "I go back and forth more than I can really justify, but if you force me to give an estimate it's probably around 33%; I think it's very plausible that we die, but more likely that we survive (at least for a little while). Here's my argument, and some reasons other people are more pessimistic."

Alexander gives a number and, at the same time, admits he cannot fully defend it and moves around a lot. Putting "I go back and forth more than I can really justify" next to a specific 33% shows a writer who will commit to a figure without pretending to more certainty than he has. The candor is his, and it is what earns the reader's trust in the argument that follows.

> "The usual scenario for unaligned AI destroying us all: some superintelligence with inhuman values is monomaniacally focused on something far from human values (eg paperclips) becomes smart enough to invent superweapons that kill all humans, then does so. Suppose we accept the assumptions of this argument:"

Before he argues against the doom case, Alexander states it at full strength and in one clean sentence, then says he will grant its assumptions. Laying out "the usual scenario" fairly, paperclips and superweapons included, means the reader meets the strongest form of the worry before any reply to it. He is visible in the discipline of stating the other side's case well enough that it could pass as their own words.

> "Nanomachines are definitely possible in some sense - biological enzymes and some microorganisms sort of count - but there's a lot of debate over exactly what a manufactured nanomachine could vs. couldn't do outside of the ecosystem of complex inter-reacting molecules that make up biological life. The most plausible proposals involve using living systems to create proteins that can (in a controlled environment) create preliminary nano-assembly machines, which can make more advanced nano-assembly machines, and so on, until they have machines capable of leaving the controlled environment (or creating other environments).
>
> Some scientists think this is just actually impossible - God Himself could not do it - see eg the Drexler-Smalley debate for some thoughts along these lines."

Alexander marks the exact point where the argument stops being settled: nanomachines are possible in some loose sense, but what a built one could do is genuinely contested, and he points to the debate rather than resolving it. The aside "God Himself could not do it" carries a real position, that some scientists think it is flatly impossible, without softening it. His refusal to hide that the crux the whole case turns on is unsettled is the move worth seeing.
