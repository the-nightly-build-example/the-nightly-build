# Voice guide: the-mechanics/reading-images (01)

## Directive

Register is the house plain-claim voice, pointed at one job this article has that
the desk default does not force: descend. This is not a survey of how vision
models are built. It is a single trace from one behavior the reader has seen
down to the part that produces it, and the writing has to feel like going down
one level at a time. Every section should answer the "why does it do that" the
last section opened, then open the next one. If a paragraph could be lifted to a
different position and read the same, it is describing rather than descending;
cut or move it.

Two things carry this article that the default does not hand you for free.

First, the honest handoff from settled to open. The series ground rule says mark
each step as settled engineering or open question. Do that as a stance, not a
disclaimer. When the mechanism is known, say it flatly and move on. When even the
people who build these systems do not fully know something, say that plainly and
without apology, in the same voice you used for the settled parts. The reader
should be able to tell, at every step, whether they are standing on rock or on a
question, and should trust that you are not blurring the two.

Second, the worked count is the tactile center of the piece, not a stat. Let the
reader do the arithmetic with you: a square image, a grid of small fixed squares
across and down, multiply, and that is where hundreds of image tokens come from.
Give it room to breathe over a few sentences. It is the moment the abstract claim
("an image becomes tokens") turns into something the reader can feel counting.

Keep the reader in the operation. Where a step is a thing the model does to the
image, write it as an action with a real object, not as a named stage. Prefer the
concrete verb over the term of art on first contact, then name the term once the
reader has watched it happen.

The failures land at the bottom, as consequence. Trace the mechanism first,
without foreshadowing the miscount and the misread small text. When the reader
reaches ground, the failures should already be explained by what they now
understand, so they read as "of course," not as a reveal you set up.

## Licenses

form: second-person present address to the actual reader ("you paste the photo",
  "count fourteen across")
move: Ciechanowski keeps the reader's hands on the mechanism ("you can drag",
  "you can use the slider") so the part is watched, not lectured. Alammar does the
  same with the reader holding the numbers.
bar:  each use must put the reader inside the operation being explained at that
  moment. If the sentence would survive with the "you" deleted, it is decoration
  and the "you" comes out. Never address a hypothetical reader's reaction; the
  ban on "a reader will notice" still holds.

form: the worked count walked out loud, arithmetic spread across sentences rather
  than compressed to a result
move: Alammar makes a vector tangible by counting it out ("five numbers") before
  any formula; the number is the intuition, not a citation.
bar:  the figures must be the real patch accounting from a cited paper or model
  card, and the count must do the explaining (why an image costs hundreds of
  tokens, why detail below the grid is gone). A number that only impresses is cut.

form: naming an open question as open, in the settled voice
move: Evans states plainly when a common concept has no clean answer ("I had a
  harder time explaining it than I expected") and treats the fuzziness as the real
  subject rather than a failure.
bar:  the sentence must say which of two specific things it means: settled
  engineering the reader can rely on, or a question still open to the people
  building these systems. A vague "it's complicated" fails the bar; the sentence
  has to locate the uncertainty.

## Recently used, do not reuse

- The dek's "reads/writes" antithesis, and its family of two-verb reversals
  ("reads X but writes Y"). The recent the-mechanics deks are single plain
  declaratives and that plainness is right; the mirrored-verb structure is spent.
  Match the register, not the shape.
- Heading cadence built on a comma joining two clauses with "and" ("The scale,
  and what it is compounding against"). Vary the heading shape across the piece.
- The "X is not Y; it is Z" correction mold and colon-subtitle headline. Standard
  house bans, flagged here because a vision piece invites both ("It doesn't see
  pixels: how a model reads an image").

## Bartosz Ciechanowski, "Mechanical Watch"
Source: https://ciechanow.ski/mechanical-watch/
Craft:
- cadence: declarative statements interleaved with short direct invitations to
  act; the reader is never left reading three abstract sentences in a row.
- argument: build from the whole to each part, establishing what the object does
  before opening how, so every component arrives with a job already waiting.
- evidence: the mechanism itself, watched in motion; each claim is something the
  reader can see happen rather than take on faith.
- stance: guide-companion, exhaustive but unhurried, explicitly telling the reader
  they need not memorize what they are seeing.
- notice: what deserves attention is made noticeable ("the real stars of the
  show") rather than announced as important.
- diction: accessible words for sophisticated content; no term of art before the
  thing it names has been shown.
- reader: an explorer with hands on the mechanism, not a pupil taking dictation.
- the important move: he introduces each part only at the moment the mechanism
  needs it, so the reader never holds an unexplained name in memory.

## Jay Alammar, "The Illustrated Word2vec"
Source: https://jalammar.github.io/illustrated-word2vec/
Craft:
- cadence: questions that invite the reader in, answered by short declaratives
  once the intuition is set.
- argument: intuition before terminology; a proxy example (personality traits as
  a vector) teaches the shape of the idea before the real term arrives.
- evidence: concrete small numbers made tangible ("five numbers"), shown as a
  grid the reader can read directly.
- stance: shared discovery, "we can now say", the reader arriving at the claim
  rather than receiving it.
- notice: grounds an intimidating abstraction in tools the reader already uses,
  so the concept feels familiar before it is named.
- diction: technical terms emerge only after the groundwork, never imposed early.
- reader: a collaborator walked to the idea, trusted to hold the numbers.
- the important move: he lets the reader do the small computation themselves, so
  the mechanism is felt rather than asserted. This is the model for the patch
  count.

## Julia Evans, "'server' is hard to define"
Source: https://jvns.ca/blog/2019/12/26/whats-a-server/
Craft:
- cadence: short honest bursts, exploratory rather than concluding.
- argument: names the ambiguity as the subject instead of papering over it, then
  works through where the word actually lands.
- evidence: tiny recognizable examples and exchanges, each one specific and
  concrete.
- stance: fellow-puzzler; admits her own difficulty up front and does not pretend
  to mastery she lacks.
- notice: treats a fuzzy or unsettled concept as a legitimate problem worth
  sitting with, not a failure to hide.
- diction: plain Anglo-Saxon words, "short and wide", "looks like a computer".
- reader: someone puzzling alongside her, addressed as "we".
- the important move: she distinguishes clearly between what is known and what
  resists a clean answer, and says which is which without embarrassment. This is
  the model for marking settled versus open at the bottom of the trace.
