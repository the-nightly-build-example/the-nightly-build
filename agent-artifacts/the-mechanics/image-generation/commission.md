## The behavior and why it

You type a sentence and a system hands back a picture that no one drew: a photo of
a thing that never existed, in the style you named. Anyone who has used an image
generator has seen it, and most users have no model at all of what happens between
the prompt and the picture. This desk answers how it actually does that. The
lesson works backward from the finished image to the mechanism that produced it,
one real step at a time, in plain words and with no code.

## The angle

The picture is built by removing noise, not by drawing. That is the spine of the
lesson, and every step should serve it. Work backward from the behavior:

- What comes out and what went in: a coherent image from a short text prompt and,
  underneath, a field of random noise the reader never sees.
- What the model was trained to do: given an image with a known amount of noise
  added, predict the noise (equivalently, predict a slightly cleaner version). It
  learns this on many image-and-caption pairs, so the prediction can be steered by
  text.
- How a picture appears at generation time: start from pure noise and apply the
  learned denoiser many times in small steps, each step producing a slightly
  cleaner image, until an image remains. There is no canvas and no object list;
  the image is what is left after repeated denoising.
- How the words steer it: the prompt is encoded by a separate text model and fed
  into the denoiser at every step, so each step is nudged toward an image that
  matches the text. Guidance strength (the reader may know it as a slider)
  controls how hard the prompt is enforced.
- One efficiency step worth teaching because it explains the seams: modern systems
  denoise in a compressed latent space and decode to pixels at the end, rather
  than denoising full-resolution pixels.

Keep going down until the reader hits ground: a step where nothing below it would
change the answer, which here is that the network only ever learned to predict
noise. Mark which steps are settled engineering (the denoising process, text
conditioning, latent-space diffusion) and which are open or only partly
understood (why specific failures happen, such as garbled text in images, wrong
finger counts, or muddled spatial relations). By the end the reader can explain
why the system behaves as it does and can tell when someone else's explanation
skips a step, for instance anyone who describes the model as "drawing" or
"searching for" an image.

## Sources

Source floor for this series: at least 8 sources, at least 4 primary, at least 1
secondary. Primary here is each cited paper's own authors reporting their own
method.

Direct the researcher to read, at minimum:
- Jonathan Ho, Ajay Jain, and Pieter Abbeel, "Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic
  Models" (2020), primary for the train-to-predict-noise procedure and the
  step-by-step reverse (denoising) process.
- Robin Rombach and colleagues, "High-Resolution Image Synthesis with Latent
  Diffusion Models" (2022), primary for denoising in a compressed latent space and
  decoding to pixels.
- Jonathan Ho and Tim Salimans, "Classifier-Free Diffusion Guidance" (2022),
  primary for how prompt adherence is strengthened and what the guidance control
  does.
- A primary source for text conditioning: Alec Radford and colleagues on CLIP
  (2021) for image-text alignment, and/or Chitwan Saharia and colleagues on Imagen
  (2022) for a large text encoder driving the denoiser. Read how the encoded
  prompt enters the model.
- At least one independent secondary source that describes the everyday behavior
  or the user-facing controls, for grounding the phenomenon a reader has seen.

Every claim the mechanism rests on is traced to the paper that owns it. Where
systems differ (pixel-space versus latent diffusion, CLIP versus a large text
encoder), record the difference rather than flattening it. Note honestly which
steps the sources treat as settled and which they treat as open.
