# Voice guide: the-evidence/emergent-abilities-mirage

## How this piece should sound

This lesson reads one paper for a reader who is smart and widely read but has never opened it, and who has met the idea the paper disputes only as a claim someone else asserted. Two demands sit on the prose at once. It has to teach a measurement point that runs against a newcomer's intuition, and it has to hold a live disagreement between the paper and the people who have pushed back on it, without the reader being able to tell partway through which way the writer has already decided. The register is plain and unhurried, closer to Harford and Luu below than to a review essay: short claims, real numbers, and no reach for effect.

The teaching sections have to keep two things apart that a reader new to the subject will want to collapse into one: a quantity that moves smoothly and a score built on top of it that moves in steps. Harford's blood-test paragraph shows the pace this needs. He states the answer intuition offers, says plainly that it is wrong, and then walks a hundred imagined cases through the calculation until the reader can see the result for themselves, rather than asserting it and moving on. His bacon paragraph shows the other half: he takes a figure that sounds alarming and resizes it against a base rate in the same breath. When this lesson handles an accuracy figure or a percentage that a newcomer cannot scale on their own, that is the move to reach for. Give the number the comparison that makes it mean something, and do not leave the impressive-sounding version standing alone.

The disagreement is where the sound matters most. Yong's replication piece lays out the skeptics' objections in full and in their own order before he weighs any of them, and only then tests the strongest one against the evidence, quoting a researcher in plain words rather than paraphrasing him into a tidy point. Build the two positions here with that much care: the paper's claim and its critics' claim each stated as its own holder would state it, each given its strongest version, before any weighing starts. Luu's opening does the same on a smaller scale, granting the other party its due in the sentence before he states his own contrary claim. Where this lesson reaches a judgment about what the paper does and does not establish, it can be stated directly, the way Yong states "a serious blow," so long as the evidence for it has already been shown on the page.

Holding both sides fairly does not require the prose to stay neutral about the evidence. Luu concedes the real failures by name, in specifics, in the middle of his own argument, and the concession costs him nothing because it is true; the reader trusts the rest more for it. When this lesson marks what the paper leaves open, or what its critics get right, name the specific task family or metric at issue, the way Luu names HoloLens and mobile, rather than gesturing at limits in general. Luu's venture-capital paragraph shows the discipline the scale question needs. Settle what the right measure is before arguing about the verdict, so that a list of examples is never mistaken for a count of them.

The vocabulary of the subject stays exact throughout. A discontinuous, all-or-nothing metric and a linear, partial-credit one are different things, and a per-token probability is not an exact-match score. Set each name once and reuse it, so that a synonym reached for variety does not read as a new idea the reader now has to track. The reader should finish able to follow, in plain terms, the measurement point at the center of the paper, and able to say where the people who study this still disagree.

## Tim Harford, "How to Make Good Guesses"

Source: https://timharford.com/2016/03/how-to-make-good-guesses/

> "Base rates are not just a forecasting aid. They're vital in clearly understanding and communicating all manner of risks. We routinely hear claims of the form that eating two rashers of bacon a day raises the risk of bowel cancer by 18 per cent. But without a base rate (how common is bowel cancer?) this information is not very useful. As it happens, in the UK, bowel cancer affects six out of 100 people; a bacon-rich diet would cause one additional case of bowel cancer per 100 people."

Harford takes a figure that sounds frightening, 18 per cent, and in the same short paragraph gives the base rate that resizes it, so the reader ends holding the absolute number that actually matters, one case in a hundred. The paragraph moves one step at a time: the claim, then a question in parentheses, then the recalculated figure. Harford is visible in the refusal to let the scary version stand and in trusting small round numbers to carry the point.

> "Imagine a blood test for a dangerous disease that is 75 per cent accurate: if an infected person takes the test, it will detect the infection 75 per cent of the time but it will also give a false positive 25 per cent of the time for an uninfected person. Now, let's say that a random person takes the test and seems to be infected. What is the chance that he really does have the disease? The intuitive answer is 75 per cent. But the correct answer is: we don't know, because we don't know the base rate."

He names the answer intuition offers, says flatly that it is wrong, and stops there, leaving the reader inside the puzzle before he resolves it. The example is built from whole imagined people rather than probabilities, which is what makes a counterintuitive result reachable for someone meeting it for the first time. The patience is the Harford move: he sets the wrong answer down and then earns the right one instead of asserting it.

## Ed Yong, "Psychology's Replication Crisis Is Running Out of Excuses"

Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/science/archive/2018/11/psychologys-replication-crisis-real/576223/

> "But skeptics have argued that the misleadingly named "crisis" has more mundane explanations. First, the replication attempts themselves might be too small. Second, the researchers involved might be incompetent, or lack the know-how to properly pull off the original experiments. Third, people vary, and two groups of scientists might end up with very different results if they do the same experiment on two different groups of volunteers."

Before he presses his own reading, Yong sets out the other side's three explanations in full and in order, each phrased as a serious person defending it would phrase it. He does not soften them or answer them here; he lets them stand as real objections the reader can weigh. The care he spends building the opposing case is what makes his later weighing credible rather than rigged.

> "It's worth dwelling on this because it's a serious blow to one of the most frequently cited criticisms of the "reproducibility crisis" rhetoric. Surely, skeptics argue, it's a fantasy to expect studies to replicate everywhere. "There's a massive deference to the sample," Nosek says. "Your replication attempt failed? It must be because you did it in Ohio and I did it in Virginia, and people are different. But these results suggest that we can't just wave those failures away very easily.""

Here Yong takes the strongest objection and tests it against the finding, quoting Nosek in the researcher's own plain words instead of summarizing him. The judgment is stated outright, "a serious blow," and then grounded, so the reader watches the reasoning rather than being asked to accept a verdict. Yong is visible in pairing a firm claim with a concrete quotation that carries its weight.

> "Sanjay Srivastava from the University of Oregon says the lack of variation in Many Labs 2 is actually a positive thing. Sure, it suggests that the large number of failed replications really might be due to sloppy science. But it also hints that the fundamental business of psychology—creating careful lab experiments to study the tricky, slippery, complicated world of the human mind—works pretty well."

Yong reports a source finding the non-obvious reading of the same result: the outcome that looks like bad news can also be read as reassurance about the method. He gives Srivastava room to make that case in his own sentences rather than compressing it into a single tidy line. The passage shows how to hold two honest readings of one finding side by side and let the reader keep both.

## Dan Luu, "Steve Ballmer was an underrated CEO"

Source: https://danluu.com/ballmer/

> "There's a common narrative that Microsoft was moribund under Steve Ballmer and then later saved by the miraculous leadership of Satya Nadella. This is the dominant narrative in every online discussion about the topic I've seen and it's a commonly expressed belief "in real life" as well. While I don't have anything negative to say about Nadella's leadership in this post, this narrative underrates Ballmer's role in Microsoft's success."

Luu names the received story in one sentence, grants the other party his due in the next, and states his own contrary claim plainly in the third. The concession to Nadella is genuine, and granting it is what earns Luu the room to disagree with the story around it. What shows Luu is the flat, unhedged claim set down right after a fair summary of the view he is about to argue against.

> "There are certainly plenty of big misses as well. From 2010-2015, HoloLens was one of Microsoft's biggest bets, behind only Azure and then Bing, but no one's big AR or VR bets have had good returns to date. Microsoft failed to capture the mobile market."

In the middle of his own case, Luu stops to list the genuine failures by name and in specifics, without shrinking them. A writer defending a position is steadier for conceding the strongest evidence against it before the reader thinks to raise it. Luu is visible in the specificity of the admission, HoloLens and mobile rather than "some misses," which is what keeps the concession from sounding like a formality.

> "When people point to a long list of failures like Bing, Zune, Windows Phone, and HoloLens as evidence that Ballmer was some kind of buffoon who was holding Microsoft back, this demonstrates a lack of understanding of the tech industry. This is like pointing to a list of failed companies a VC has funded as evidence the VC doesn't know what they're doing. But that's silly in a hits based industry like venture capital. If you want to claim the VC is bad, you need to point out poor total return or a lack of big successes, which would imply poor total return."

Luu reframes how the evidence should be weighed before he argues the verdict: in a business where one hit pays for many misses, a bag of failures proves little, so the real test is total return. The analogy does the analytic work in a sentence, and then he names the standard of proof he will hold both sides to. Luu is visible in the insistence on fixing the right measure first, so that counting examples cannot stand in for weighing them.
