# Voice guide: the-evidence/sparks-of-agi (01)

## How this piece should sound

This lesson reads one document against its own method, not against a verdict on GPT-4. Report what Bubeck's team actually did: which probes they ran, what they concluded from each one. Let the distance between that and how "sparks of AGI" gets cited now do the arguing. Newton's report on the Mechanical Turk study is the model for how to carry evidence and its limits in the same sentence: the sample size and the method sit right next to the finding, not three paragraphs later as a footnote. Every time this lesson states what the paper claims, put next to it, in the same breath, what kind of evidence is doing the claiming. A hand-built qualitative probe judged by the authors who wrote it is not a score against a held-out benchmark, and the reader should never have to wait to learn which one they are reading.

The reader has met "sparks of AGI" secondhand and never checked it against the source. Smith's habit of going straight to a field's own best examples, rather than trusting how often a claim gets repeated, is the one to teach here: walk the reader through one of the paper's own worked probes so they see, on the page, what the authors saw and what they concluded from it, not a summary of the paper's reputation. The worked example earns its place in the lesson only if the reader could, in principle, go argue with the transcript itself.

Dattani's habit of naming, before she judges a finding, exactly what she needed to see to trust it applies directly to the paper's definition of intelligence. Say where Bubeck's team got that definition, when it was written, and what work the choice does for the argument. A borrowed definition earns its weight in the lesson only once the reader can see what it let the authors claim that a different definition would not have.

State Microsoft's financial stake in OpenAI as a figure and move on, the way Newton quotes Altman's own hedge on GPT-4 and lets it stand without editorializing on top of it. This is a lesson, not a referendum on GPT-4's intelligence and not a takedown of the paper: it argues from what the paper's own method can and cannot support, not from excitement or alarm about what the phrase implies. The gap between what the paper measured and what the phrase now carries is the finding; state it and stop.

## Noah Smith, "The Two Paper Rule"

Source: https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/the-two-paper-rule

> "My solution to this problem is what I call the Two Paper Rule. If you want me to read the vast literature, cite me two papers that are exemplars and paragons of that literature. Foundational papers, key recent innovations - whatever you like (but no review papers or summaries). Just two. I will read them."

Smith turns a vague objection ("you haven't read enough") into a concrete, checkable test: two specific documents, read directly. The person is visible in the decisiveness: he doesn't hedge about how much reading is enough, he names a number and commits to it.

> "If these two papers are full of mistakes and bad reasoning, I will feel free to skip the rest of the vast literature. Because if that's the best you can do, I've seen enough."

The second sentence is short and flat on purpose, right after a longer one. It reads as a judgment already reached, not one being argued into existence.

> "In other words, if you suspect that a literature functions mainly as a mud moat, what you need to assess quickly is not what the literature claims to find, but whether those claims are generally credible. And that is why you need to see the best examples the literature has to offer."

This is Smith stating, plainly, what his own method is actually checking for: not the content of a claim but the quality of the reasoning behind it. He tells the reader exactly what he is doing and why, instead of leaving the method implicit.

## Saloni Dattani, "Big studies and very big findings"

Source: https://www.scientificdiscovery.dev/p/2-big-studies-and-very-big-findings

> "Sadly, it's been a while since I've had lectures on bacterial cell biology. But this was intriguing – and I had a newsletter to write! – so I thought about which points I needed to look for to judge this study and understand it, from a field outside of my own."

Dattani states her own distance from the field before she evaluates a claim in it. That admission is what licenses the reader to trust the checklist that follows: she is showing her work, not asserting authority she doesn't have.

> "Here's what I decided to look for. There should be clear evidence that these are single cell bacteria (not several cells clumped together) from reliable imaging techniques. There should be good evidence that this is a bacterium (not some other type of organism of that size) with evidence from its genome, cell structure, properties, and so on."

She names the standard before she applies it. A reader who disagrees with her conclusion can point to which item on this list they think fails, because the list came first and the verdict came after.

> "So, if you want to precisely estimate how well they protect people against these rarer risks, you either get a bigger sample or recruit people who are at higher risk to begin with. This meta-analysis combines both: their estimate is much more precise with a big pooled sample of elderly people."

Plain cause and effect, no jargon standing in for the mechanism: small sample, imprecise estimate; bigger sample, more precise one. The explanation and the specific number sit next to each other.

## Casey Newton, "The AI is eating itself"

Source: https://www.platformer.news/the-ai-is-eating-itself/

> "The first study, which had an admittedly small sample size, found that crowd-sourced workers on Amazon's Mechanical Turks platforms increasingly admit to using LLMs to perform text-based tasks. Studying the output of 44 workers, using 'a combination of keystroke detection and synthetic text classification,' researchers from EPFL write, they 'estimate that 33–46% of crowd workers used LLMs when completing the task.'"

The caveat about sample size comes in the same sentence as the finding, not after it. By the time the reader has the number, 44, they already know how much weight to put on it.

> "A second, more worrisome study comes from researchers at the University of Oxford, University of Cambridge, University of Toronto, and Imperial College London. It found that training AI systems on data generated by other AI systems — synthetic data, to use the industry's term — causes models to degrade and ultimately collapse."

Newton names every institution behind the study rather than writing "researchers." The claim and its source are stated as two separate, checkable facts in one sentence.

> "When GPT-4 came out in March, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman tweeted: 'it is still flawed, still limited, and it still seems more impressive on first use than it does after you spend more time with it.' The more we all use chatbots like his, the more this statement rings true."

Newton quotes the company's own hedge instead of writing his own. The follow-up sentence is short and adds only his own confirmation, without restating or inflating what Altman already said.
