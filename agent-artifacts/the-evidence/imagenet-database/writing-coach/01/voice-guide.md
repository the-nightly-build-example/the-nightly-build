# Voice guide: the-evidence/imagenet-database (01)

## How this piece should sound

This lesson reports the scale of one 2009 database paper, and the two-branch
move in Yglesias's demand passage is worth carrying over: he names exactly two
things that can happen and stops there. Nothing blurs into one general
impression of size. The hierarchy the paper set out to populate and what it had
actually populated by publication are two different figures, the way
"quantities" and "prices" are two different things demand can do. Naming them
as two separate numbers, side by side, does more than any adjective could to
show a reader what "at honest scale" means here.

The Mechanical Turk labeling and verification scheme is the paper's real
methodological content, and Lee's water-wave passage is a model for teaching
it: he describes what a mathematical breakdown looks like as a small scene
before he names the phenomenon. He does not open with the word "singularity"
and leave the reader to picture it unaided. The same move is available for the
agreement procedure behind a label: what one worker submits, what the check does with a
disagreement, what has to happen before a label counts as verified. A reader
who sees that sequence once does not need the word "quality control" defined
for them.

The correction at this piece's center is that the 2012 result people mean by
"ImageNet" belongs to a different paper and a different competition, three
years later, on a subset the 2009 paper does not itself claim. That is the
kind of claim a short, flat sentence carries better than a hedge does.
Yglesias's "This is nonsense" shows what the sentence can look like: it
commits, and the sentences on either side of it carry the reasoning that earns
the commitment. This piece does not need one big verdict on what ImageNet
"really is." It needs the plain fact, stated once the reporting in front of it
has shown it, the way Lee's line about OpenAI's prestige states a judgment as
the direct consequence of the specific facts just given. It is not a closer
tacked on for effect once the section has run out of things to say.

Where the piece has to hold several claims apart at once — what the 2009 paper
showed, what the verification procedure actually established about label
accuracy, what later audits found about the categories or the images
themselves — Yong's tally in the replication piece is worth the look: he
reports each result on its own terms, effect by effect. The study never gets
resolved into one summary verdict. Some of what the 2009 paper claims holds
up exactly at the scale it claims. Some of what gets attributed to "ImageNet"
in casual argument is a different paper's finding, wearing this one's name.
Keeping those separate, claim by claim, is more honest than either defending
the document wholesale or debunking it wholesale.

None of this needs dressing up. This piece has a hierarchy size, an image
count against a planned target, an agreement threshold, and a gap of years
between two papers doing two different things. A precise number, placed where
the reader can see what it is a number of, is doing more than any word for
"big" or "surprising" ever could.

## Matthew Yglesias, "Greedflation is still fake"

Source: https://www.slowboring.com/p/greedflation-is-still-fake

> "This is nonsense."
>
> "The whole point is that overstimulating the economy generates price
> increases and windfall profits, which is why ideally you wouldn't
> overstimulate the economy."

He rejects the opposing claim in three words with no cushioning, then spends
the next sentence saying exactly what his own account was instead. The blunt
sentence and the explanation that follows do different jobs, and neither one
hedges to soften the other.

> "Two things can happen if demand for the goods or services you sell goes up.
> One is that you can sell more stuff — an increase in quantities. The other
> is that you can raise prices."

He states a general mechanism by naming the two branches it splits into and
nothing else, with no scene-setting first. The reader has the mechanism in
hand before any application of it arrives, which is why the argument that
follows reads like a consequence and not a bare assertion.

> "What one hopes for in a stimulative policy is a large increase in
> quantities and a small increase in prices. And at a time of high
> unemployment, that's a very reasonable thing to hope for. After all, most
> companies should be able to relatively easily increase quantities sold by
> hiring unemployed people to do the extra work. That's the stimulus
> working."

Each sentence adds one new piece of the argument and the passage closes by
naming the term it has been building toward, rather than reaching for a
synonym. The reasoning is visible step by step; nothing is asserted as a
conclusion that the sentences before it have not already built.

## Timothy B. Lee, "OpenAI spent millions to solve this famous math problem — mathematicians are furious"

Source: https://www.understandingai.org/p/openai-spent-millions-to-solve-this

> "But I think that focusing on the details of the drama risks missing the
> larger point. Mathematics is as much about cultivating a community of
> experts as it is about solving individual problems."

He states his own judgment in the first person, directly after describing a
dispute he does not referee. The claim about what mathematics is "as much
about" gives a specific, checkable reason rather than gesturing at
significance in general terms.

> "OpenAI sought to gain prestige by solving a famous math problem. But the
> way it went about that has arguably undermined the community that made
> solving the problem prestigious in the first place."

The judgment arrives as a direct consequence of the specific facts he has just
laid out, not as a summarizing line tacked onto the end of the section.
"Arguably" is doing real work here: it marks the sentence as his read of the
situation rather than a settled fact he is reporting.

> "At first, we have a normal water wave. But as the simulation progresses,
> the leading edge gets so steep that water particles basically have to
> teleport in order to reach their positions in time. A real wave wouldn't
> behave like this."

He explains a mathematical breakdown by describing a picture instead of
naming the phenomenon. "Basically have to teleport" is an ordinary phrase
doing the work of making a technical failure mode vivid to a reader who is
not going to look at the equations.

## Ed Yong, "Welcome To The Era of Big Replication"

Source: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/welcome-to-the-era-of-big-replication

> "To address these concerns, a team of scientists from 36 different labs
> joined together, like some sort of verification Voltron, to replicate 13
> experiments from past psychological studies. They chose experiments that
> were simple and quick to do, and merged them into a single online package
> that volunteers could finish in just 15 minutes. They then delivered their
> experimental smorgasbord to 6,344 people from 36 different groups and 12
> different countries."

Every noun in this passage is a real number from the study: 36 labs, 13
experiments, 15 minutes, 6,344 people, 12 countries. The joke rides on the
actual mechanic of many separate teams combining into one effort, so it costs
the passage none of its precision.

> "Replications are really important, and there aren't enough of them in
> psychology. But single, one-off replications can add more heat than light."

He states what is at stake in the method, not in this particular result,
before walking through why one replication attempt is a weak test on its own.
The second sentence names the specific failure ("more heat than light")
instead of just asserting that a single replication is not enough.

> "It is very telling that everyone I spoke to praised the initiative,
> including the authors whose work did not replicate. There was none of the
> acrimony that has stained past debates. When something is done this well,
> it's pretty churlish to not accept the results."

The judgment ("done this well") arrives only after he has reported who
praised the project and why, including the people with the most reason to
object. "Pretty churlish" is a small, specific word standing in for what a
weaker draft would render as a much bigger, vaguer one.
