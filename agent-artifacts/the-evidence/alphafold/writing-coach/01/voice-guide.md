# Voice guide: the-evidence/alphafold

## Directive

Write this as a plain, confident explainer of one paper's numbers, not a
verdict about AI. Short declaratives carry the mechanism; a longer sentence
is earned only where the biology genuinely has a subordinate clause in it.
The reader is smart, has no biology, and wants the real figure before the
mood. The one habit to import: calibration lives inside the sentence that
carries the evidence, not in a separate hedge bolted on after. A threshold
arrives already turned into the judgment it licenses ("a score above X means
the model and the lab measurement agree about as often as two lab
measurements agree with each other") instead of a bare number followed by a
writerly "however." When the piece turns from what CASP14 showed to what
AlphaFold gets credited with, that turn is one sentence stating both halves
plainly. It does not need "but," "yet," or a semicolon to do the work; a flat
juxtaposition does it better and stays inside the house's earned-contrast
ceiling.

## Licenses

```text
form: the sourced hedge
move: Callaway and Heaven never let the writer's own qualifier soften a big
      number. The limit comes from CASP's or the paper's own stated scope,
      quoted or cited, sitting in the same paragraph as the figure it limits.
bar:  the hedge must trace to a citable line in a primary source named in
      this article, and it must appear within the paragraph that states the
      number it qualifies, not in a later "limitations" aside.
```

```text
form: the two-half verdict
move: Saplakoglu's "solved one part of the problem very cleverly, but not
      the way a scientist would" states what was demonstrated and what is
      credited in a single sentence, with no "but" carrying the turn between
      them.
bar:  usable once, at the pivot where the commission's separation (measured
      CASP14 result vs. the three things AlphaFold is credited with beyond
      it) lands, and only after both halves already have their own citation
      earlier in the piece.
```

```text
form: the numeric build
move: Quanta compresses a field's whole history into one clause of figures
      (seven solved structures in 1971, over 140,000 by 2020) so the scale
      of the foundation arrives before any claim about the result sits on
      top of it.
bar:  every figure in the compressed sentence needs its own citation, and
      the sentence must be sizing something the commission actually needs
      sized (CASP14's field, the training set, the database), never
      decoration for pace.
```

```text
form: definition by consequence
move: Heaven teaches GDT_TS by what a passing score lets a scientist stop
      doing, not by the formula: "a score above 90 means any difference
      could be down to experimental error." The number becomes usable in
      the same sentence that defines it.
bar:  the term is cashed out with the paper's or CASP's own number, in the
      sentence where it first appears, and the reader must be able to
      apply the threshold to the next number in the piece without
      rereading the definition.
```

## Recently used, do not reuse:

The series' "the paper never did X" headline mold and its numbers-forward
twin. The comma-triad dek and the semicolon-reversal dek. This piece finds
its own claim rather than a fifth instance of the reveal mold, and lands it
with the two-half verdict above instead of a dek built from clauses joined
by commas or a semicolon.

## Ewen Callaway, "'It will change everything': DeepMind's AI makes gigantic leap in solving protein structures"
Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-020-03348-4 (carried in full by Scientific American: https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/deepminds-ai-makes-gigantic-leap-in-solving-protein-structures/)
Craft:
- cadence: one flat declarative fact, then one attributed superlative; the two never stack unqualified.
- argument: the case that this matters is built entirely from what CASP's own scoring produced, never from the writer asserting significance.
- evidence: every threshold (two-thirds of predictions at lab-grade accuracy, ~90 vs. ~75 on the 100-point scale) carries a same-breath stakes clause, what the number lets a structural biologist stop doing.
- stance: relays CASP's verdict rather than campaigning for it, so the excitement reads earned, not promotional.
- notice: catches that the margin over the 2018 leaderboard (25 points, versus roughly six points last time) is itself news, not just the raw score.
- diction: plain nouns for the biology (spike protein, sequence, structure), and every figure given in a unit a lay reader already owns.
- reader: assumes curiosity and patience, no biology background, and earns each term before reusing it.
- the hedge is sourced, not asserted: when the piece needs to say "this isn't total," it finds a scientist's own qualifying sentence rather than inserting a writerly "however."

## Yasemin Saplakoglu, "How AI Revolutionized Protein Science, but Didn't End It"
Source: https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-ai-revolutionized-protein-science-but-didnt-end-it-20240626/
Craft:
- cadence: compresses decades into a paragraph, then slows to one sentence at the pivot, so the reader feels the acceleration the record shows.
- argument: separates what was attempted from what was achieved in a single controlled sentence, then spends the rest of the piece proving each half.
- evidence: sizes the field's infrastructure (seven solved structures in 1971, over 140,000 by 2020) before naming the achievement, so "big deal" already has a foundation under it.
- stance: sympathetic to the researchers whose years of bench work the tool displaces, without romanticizing the labor it replaced.
- notice: catches that "changed everything and nothing" is not a contradiction but a scope claim, and lets the sentence carry exactly that.
- diction: swaps a jargon term for a working image exactly once (a fold-switching protein as something that "flails about like an air dancer outside a car dealership"), then returns to the term.
- reader: trusts the reader to sit through the history if the payoff is a sharper verdict at the end, rather than writing for a reader hunting a shortcut.
- the "solved / not the way a scientist would" construction is the whole calibration in one sentence: it credits the result and denies the overclaim without a "but."

## Will Douglas Heaven, "DeepMind's protein-folding AI has solved a 50-year-old grand challenge of biology"
Source: https://www.technologyreview.com/2020/11/30/1012712/deepmind-protein-folding-ai-solved-biology-science-drugs-disease/
Craft:
- cadence: short declaratives carry mechanism; one longer sentence is spent only where a real subordinate relationship exists ("Demis Hassabis... has always stressed that these successes were just stepping stones toward a larger goal").
- argument: teaches a metric by the decision it lets the reader make, not by its formula.
- evidence: converts every score into a comparison the reader already owns (1.6 ångströms rendered as 0.16 nanometers, "atom-sized"); every superlative gets a same-paragraph number.
- stance: impressed and skeptical at once, and refuses to let either cancel the other; a scope caveat (structures known for only a quarter of human proteins) sits beside the triumph, not after it in a separate section.
- notice: catches that speed is a real limitation worth its own sentence, because a working scientist would ask about it before accuracy.
- diction: no term survives the sentence that introduces it without being cashed out ("GDT score... measures on a scale from 0 to 100 how close a predicted structure is to the actual shape").
- reader: a smart generalist who wants the real number, never told how to feel about a figure before seeing it.
- definition by consequence: a metric is taught by what a passing value lets a scientist conclude, so the very next number in the piece can be judged against it without rereading the definition.
