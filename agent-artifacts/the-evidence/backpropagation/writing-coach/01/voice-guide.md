# Voice guide: the-evidence/backpropagation

## How this piece should sound

This lesson puts one specific document from 1986 in front of a reader who has
never opened it, and reports what is actually on its pages against what the
field now says about it. All three writers below do that kind of reporting on
a technical primary source, and none of them softens the vocabulary to get
there: Wilczek keeps "wave function" and "complementarity," Lee keeps "query
vector" and "dot product." What makes the sentences readable is the sentence
built around the term, not the term's absence. This piece can hold the same
standard for whatever vocabulary the 1986 paper actually requires: hidden
units, the error signal, the generalized delta rule, whichever of those the
draft needs, get to stay exactly as named.

Grounding an abstraction in an object the reader already has in hand before
attaching the technical name to it, the way Lee's map coordinates stand in for
word vectors, or Wilczek's cakes stand in for a pair of undetermined quantum
states, is a move available wherever this piece has to make the paper's
central mechanical idea land before its name does.

Wolfram's habit of stating his own first reading of a document and then
correcting it against what the document actually says, on the page, is a
posture worth having available for the priority question this piece has to
answer. It lets a paragraph report what earlier work claimed and what the 1986
authors themselves credited as something checked against the record in front
of the reader, rather than a conclusion arrived at off-page and only announced.

Where this piece corrects an inflated claim about what the paper showed,
Wilczek's way of posing the misconception as a direct question and then
answering it in short, plain sentences, with no hedge around the answer
itself, is one shape that correction can take. The same goes for reporting
the paper's own stated limits: the 1986 authors named their limits themselves,
and stating them in those terms, plainly, is available rather than folding
them into a general caveat.

Lee's habit of pairing a moment of genuine notice with the exact evidence that
earns it — a named paper, a page count, a specific figure — rather than
asserting significance on its own, is the shape available for the places
where this piece has to make the paper's actual scale, or a later result
about it, register as more than an assertion.

## Stephen Wolfram, "Untangling the Tale of Ada Lovelace"

Source: https://writings.stephenwolfram.com/2015/12/untangling-the-tale-of-ada-lovelace/

> "In other words, she basically proposed to take on the role of CEO, with
> Babbage becoming CTO. It wasn't an easy pitch to make, especially given
> Babbage's personality. But she was skillful in making her case, and as part
> of it, she discussed their different motivation structures."

Wolfram takes a long, formal proposal from an 1843 letter and states its
function in one plain modern phrase, then moves straight on to why the pitch
was hard to make. The reader gets the point of the letter without first
working through its own diction, and the judgment about difficulty follows
immediately as a plain claim rather than sitting beside the analogy as
decoration.

> "When I first read this, I thought Babbage was saying that he basically
> ghostwrote all of Ada's Notes. But reading what he wrote again, I realize it
> actually says almost nothing, other than that he suggested things that Ada
> may or may not have used."

Here Wolfram narrates a specific wrong reading of a source and then corrects
it in the next sentence, in front of the reader, instead of presenting only
the corrected conclusion. The person is visible in the particular claim he
first made and the particular rereading that undid it, not in a general note
about being careful.

> "To me, there's little doubt about what happened: Ada had an idea of what
> the Analytical Engine should be capable of, and was asking Babbage questions
> about how it could be achieved. If my own experiences with hardware
> designers in modern times are anything to go by, the answers will often
> have been very detailed. Ada's achievement was to distill from these
> details a clear exposition of the abstract operation of the machine —
> something which Babbage never did."

Wolfram grounds a judgment about an 1840s correspondence in his own present-day
experience working with engineers, naming the comparison instead of asserting
the verdict outright. The verdict in the last sentence follows directly from
the comparison that came before it, so the reader can check the reasoning
rather than just accept the conclusion.

## Timothy B. Lee (with Sean Trott), "Large language models, explained with a minimum of math and jargon"

Source: https://www.understandingai.org/p/large-language-models-explained-with

> "This is useful for reasoning about spatial relationships. You can tell New
> York is close to Washington DC because 38.9 is close to 40.7 and 77 is close
> to 74. By the same token, Paris is close to London. But Paris is far from
> Washington DC."

Lee builds the technical point (a word vector encodes closeness in meaning)
out of an ordinary fact the reader already holds, map coordinates, before the
term "word vector" ever has to do any work. Each sentence adds exactly one
comparison and stops, which is where his hand is visible: nothing here is
padding out an idea that was already made.

> "You can think of the attention mechanism as a matchmaking service for
> words. Each word makes a checklist (called a query vector) describing the
> characteristics of words it is looking for. Each word also makes a
> checklist (called a key vector) describing its own characteristics. The
> network compares each key vector to each query vector (by computing a dot
> product) to find the words that are the best match."

The playful label ("matchmaking service") never substitutes for the real
terms; it sits next to "query vector," "key vector," and "dot product" in the
same paragraph. That pairing is Lee's signature move, and it is visible in the
order: image first, vocabulary immediately after, in the same breath.

> "We love this example because it illustrates just how difficult it will be
> to fully understand LLMs. The five-member Redwood team published a 25-page
> paper explaining how they identified and validated these attention heads.
> Yet even after they did all that work, we are still far from having a
> comprehensive explanation for why GPT-2 decided to predict Mary as the next
> word."

Lee states plainly that he finds something notable, then backs it in the next
two sentences with specifics: a named team, a page count, and an honest
statement of what even that much work failed to settle. The enthusiasm is
earned by what follows it rather than left to stand alone.

## Frank Wilczek, "Entanglement Made Simple"

Source: https://www.quantamagazine.org/entanglement-made-simple-20160428/

> "Entanglement arises in situations where we have partial knowledge of the
> state of two systems. For example, our systems can be two objects that
> we'll call c-ons. The "c" is meant to suggest "classical," but if you'd
> prefer to have something specific and pleasant in mind, you can think of
> our c-ons as cakes."

Wilczek introduces a new piece of vocabulary and, in the same breath, admits
the name is arbitrary and offers a plainer stand-in for it. The reader ends
up holding both the technical word and permission to think of it as something
ordinary, which is where a Nobel physicist's ease with the material shows.

> "But does it? Until I know the result you obtained, I don't know what to
> expect. I gain useful information when I learn the result you've measured,
> not at the moment you measure it. And any message revealing the result you
> measured must be transmitted in some concrete physical way, slower
> (presumably) than the speed of light."

Wilczek poses the question a reader is likely already asking (does entangled
measurement send information faster than light?) and then answers it in three
short sentences, each stating one fact, with no qualifying language wrapped
around the answer itself.

> "Nor is it paradoxical to find that distant events are correlated. After
> all, if I put each member of a pair of gloves in boxes, and mail them to
> opposite sides of the earth, I should not be surprised that by looking
> inside one box I can determine the handedness of the glove in the other."

He answers an abstract worry with a completely mundane physical scenario and
states the conclusion in the same sentence as the setup, rather than pausing
before delivering it. The gloves are his own choice of object; nothing about
the passage needed them to be gloves specifically, and that ordinariness is
the point.
