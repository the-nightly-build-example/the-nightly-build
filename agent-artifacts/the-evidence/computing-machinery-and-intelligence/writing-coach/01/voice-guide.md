# Voice guide: the-evidence/computing-machinery-and-intelligence

## How this piece should sound

This piece holds one document up next to how it gets quoted today, so the register that carries it is a person walking a reader through exactly what a paper said and exactly what a later claim said, and letting the two sit side by side. Scott Alexander's ivermectin piece opens this way: state what people believe happened, then go get the actual number. The lesson can take the same opening move for its own subject — most people who say "the Turing test" have not read the four conditions Turing actually set, so the piece earns its keep by going and getting them.

Turing's one number carries the whole lesson, and it deserves the discipline Alexander gives his own count of studies: "We've gone from 29 studies to 11, getting rid of 18 along the way. For the record, we eliminated 2/19 for fraud, 1/19 for severe preregistration violations, 10 for methodological problems, and 6 because Meyerowitz-Katz was suspicious of them." Every figure there is tied to a named reason, not folded into "most were excluded for quality issues." The storage figure, the 70 percent, the five minutes, and "average interrogator" can get the same treatment: state each once, exactly as Turing states it, and resist letting "roughly" or "well over half" stand in for a number the primary source already gives precisely. The same applies going the other direction, to whatever the modern claim's own participant count, duration, and pass rate turn out to be — get those exact figures from the primary study, not a rounded impression of them.

Where a figure needs translating for a reader who wasn't there, Timothy Lee's habit of converting it in the very next clause is worth borrowing: "When OpenAI released ChatGPT two years ago, it had a context window of just 8,192 tokens. This meant that if you fed it more than about 15 pages of text, it would 'forget' information from the beginning of its context." The translation isn't held for a later paragraph, and it reaches for something the reader already knows how to picture. Turing's 10^9 units of storage, and any modern figure the reader can't scale on their own, can get the same immediate anchor rather than being left to sit as a bare number.

The imitation game's own setup is a mechanism before it is a phrase, and Lee's habit of finding the ordinary version of a thing before the jargon arrives is the right model for introducing it: "I once attended a ballroom dancing class where couples stood in a ring around the edge of the room. After each dance, women would stay where they were while men would rotate to the next woman... Ring attention works on the same principle." The plain version of the situation comes first, and the vocabulary attaches to something the reader can already picture. Turing adapted the imitation game from a party game about telling a man from a woman by letter, conducted by teleprinter — the ordinary version of that setup can do the same work before "interrogator" and "average" become terms this piece leans on.

The piece's required contribution is to put Turing's four conditions beside a modern claim's own conditions and let the reader see what changed, and Derek Lowe's habit of stating a fact and its consequence back to back, with nothing argued in between, is the shape that needs: "The authors now say that they are unable to access the raw data used for their own study and are thus unable to have it confirmed by anyone else. Accordingly, the papers have been retracted." No adjective sits between the fact and what follows from it. Stated this way, three-party against two-party, five minutes against a longer session, and "average" interrogator against recruited participants don't need a verdict word attached — placed next to each other, plainly, they already show the reader what a modern "passed the Turing test" claim borrowed from Turing and what it quietly changed.

This is a history-of-one-document lesson, not a survey, so it has the room to slow down where the commission asks it to: on the exact substitution Turing makes when he sets aside "Can machines think?" for the game, and on the handful of the nine objections that actually carry the lesson rather than a march through all of them. The piece can spend that room instead of covering more ground thinly.

## Scott Alexander, "Ivermectin: Much More Than You Wanted To Know"

Source: https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/ivermectin-much-more-than-you-wanted

> "I know I'm two months late here. Everyone's already made up their mind and moved on to other things. But here's my pitch: this is one of the most carefully-pored-over scientific issues of our time. Dozens of teams published studies saying ivermectin definitely worked. Then most scientists concluded it didn't."

The opening states the two competing beliefs as flatly as possible before any evidence appears — one claim, then the opposite claim, no adjective on either yet. The "here's my pitch" is the one place the writer steps forward, and it does real work: it tells the reader why the question is worth reading on for, instead of assuming the appeal is obvious.

> "We've gone from 29 studies to 11, getting rid of 18 along the way. For the record, we eliminated 2/19 for fraud, 1/19 for severe preregistration violations, 10 for methodological problems, and 6 because Meyerowitz-Katz was suspicious of them."

Every number here is an actual count, not an impression of a count, and each is attached to a specific, named reason for the cut. A reader who wants to check the arithmetic can. Nothing is filed under a general label like "quality concerns."

> "As several doctors and researchers have pointed out... the most impressive studies come from places that are teeming with worms. Mahmud from Bangladesh, Ravakirti from East India, Lopez-Medina from Colombia, etc."

The finding lands on specific studies and specific places rather than a general descriptor like "certain regions." The writer trusts the named nouns to carry the claim instead of summarizing it into something vaguer and safer.

## Timothy B. Lee, "Why large language models struggle with long contexts"

Source: https://www.understandingai.org/p/why-large-language-models-struggle

> "When OpenAI released ChatGPT two years ago, it had a context window of just 8,192 tokens. This meant that if you fed it more than about 15 pages of text, it would 'forget' information from the beginning of its context."

The raw figure is translated into something a reader can picture without technical background, and the translation arrives in the very next sentence, not several paragraphs later. The reader is never asked to carry an unconverted number while they wait for it to matter.

> "I once attended a ballroom dancing class where couples stood in a ring around the edge of the room. After each dance, women would stay where they were while men would rotate to the next woman. Over time, every man got a chance to dance with every woman. Ring attention works on the same principle."

An ordinary, easily pictured scene does the explaining before any technical vocabulary shows up. The mapping from dancers to "query vectors" and "key vectors" only has to carry a little weight, because the plain version of the mechanism is already in the reader's head.

> "This is probably why Google charges twice as much, per token, for Gemini 1.5 Pro once the context gets longer than 128,000 tokens. Generating token number 128,001 requires comparisons with all 128,000 previous tokens, making it significantly more expensive than producing the first or 10th or 100th token."

An abstract property (attention cost grows quadratically) is tied to something checkable in the world (a pricing tier) and a specific number (token 128,001). The abstraction earns its place by immediately cashing out in a fact the reader could look up.

## Derek Lowe, "Surgisphere and Their Data (Updated)"

Source: https://www.science.org/content/blog-post/surgisphere-and-their-data-updated

> "It's worth noting that these papers go both ways about the efficacy of these treatments: the Ivermectin one suggested that it was a useful treatment for coronavirus patients, while the hydroxychloroquine one suggested that that drug wasn't."

The sentence corrects a real asymmetry in how the story gets remembered, using the studies' own verbs: one "suggested" a drug worked, the other "suggested" a different drug didn't. Neither is compressed into a label like "mixed findings."

> "The authors now say that they are unable to access the raw data used for their own study and are thus unable to have it confirmed by anyone else. Accordingly, the papers have been retracted."

Cause and consequence sit in two short sentences with nothing argued between them: the specific failure, then the concrete result. The writer lets the fact carry the verdict rather than telling the reader how damning it is.

> "…a bad study isn't just a zero. It's a negative number; bad medical papers can do actual harm, and in the case of coronavirus therapy recommendations that harm can be immediate. So let's have some answers as fast as we can get them."

The claim about stakes is stated once, plainly, and immediately followed by the specific harm rather than left to stand alone as a line to admire. The closing sentence is an instruction, not a flourish — it says what should happen next.
