# Commission: the-evidence/computing-machinery-and-intelligence

## The document

Alan Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," *Mind* LIX, no. 236
(October 1950), 433–460. The paper that proposed the imitation game, now
universally called the Turing test. This is the document the lesson reads.

## The angle

Readers invoke "the Turing test" constantly, and a 2025 preprint reporting that
GPT-4.5 was judged human most of the time revived the phrase in the press. Almost
no one has read the 1950 paper. The lesson puts the actual document in front of
the reader: what Turing actually proposed, the one concrete quantitative
prediction he made, what he was and was not claiming, and how far today's usage
has drifted from it.

Teach these, in an order the writer sets from the evidence:

- **What the imitation game actually is.** A three-party game Turing adapts from
  a party game about telling a man from a woman. An interrogator, communicating
  only by teleprinter (text), tries to tell a machine from a human. State the
  setup exactly as Turing sets it, including that it was conducted in writing,
  which is why the test is about conversation and not appearance or voice.
- **The one number in the paper.** Turing's prediction for "about fifty years"
  hence (i.e. around 2000): that a machine with about 10^9 units of storage could
  be programmed to play the imitation game so well that an average interrogator
  would have no more than a 70 percent chance of making the right identification
  after five minutes of questioning. Get the figures exactly from the primary:
  the storage figure, the 70 percent, the five minutes, the "average"
  interrogator. Show honestly how small and specific a claim this is, and that
  Turing framed it as a guess about when the *words* "machine" and "think" would
  have shifted in usage, not a threshold for consciousness.
- **What Turing was answering, and what he set aside.** He opens by refusing the
  question "Can machines think?" as "too meaningless" and replaces it with the
  game. Teach that substitution: the test is a deliberate operational dodge of the
  metaphysical question, not an answer to it. Cover, briefly and accurately, that
  the bulk of the paper is Turing rebutting nine named objections (the writer
  need not enumerate all nine; pick the ones that carry the lesson, e.g. the
  "Lady Lovelace" objection that a machine can only do what it is told).
- **How the document is used today, and where usage does not match it.** Recent
  claims that a system "passed the Turing test." Read the primary you cite for any
  such claim (e.g. the 2025 Jones & Bergen study) and state its exact setup and
  numbers, and how they compare to Turing's own conditions (three-party vs
  two-party, five minutes vs longer, "average" interrogators vs recruited
  participants). Draw the line the series demands: where today's "passed the
  Turing test" rests on conditions Turing did not set, say so plainly. Also state
  the standing criticism, older than the LLM era, that the test measures
  deception and human credulity rather than thought (the writer may cite a primary
  critic such as Hayes & Ford, or note it briefly if sourcing is thin).

## Required contribution

The evidence record and library carry the facts. This piece's own work is to set
Turing's actual 1950 prediction, in its exact numbers and conditions, beside a
present-day "passed the Turing test" claim in its exact numbers and conditions,
so the reader can see for themselves what the modern claim borrows from Turing
and what it quietly changes. The reader should leave able to hear "it passed the
Turing test" and ask the two questions that matter: under what conditions, and
against what bar.

## Boundaries

- One document: the 1950 paper. Later work enters only as present-day usage or as
  confirmation/correction, per the series prompt.
- Do not re-teach machinery covered elsewhere. Link, do not re-explain: the paper
  already covers how a chatbot predicts text and why it produces fluent, plausible
  wrong answers. If the writer needs "a model produces its most probable
  continuation," link an existing mechanics lesson in Background rather than
  teaching it. The researcher should surface the best existing library link.
- This is history-of-an-idea plus a present check. It is not a general essay on
  machine consciousness, not a survey of AI benchmarks, and not an opinion piece
  on whether LLMs think. No hype, no doom.
- Name no company as an authority. Report what a study did and what it measured.

## Source policy (the-evidence / lesson)

Floor: at least 6 sources, at least 3 primary, at least 1 secondary. The 1950
*Mind* paper is the anchor primary and must be read directly, not through
summary. Any modern "passed the test" claim must be read from its own paper or
preprint (primary), not from press coverage. Contested figures need a primary.
