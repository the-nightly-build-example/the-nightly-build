# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/syri-fraud

## How this piece should sound

This is a lesson about SyRI: a system the Dutch government used to score
households for welfare-fraud risk, which a court in The Hague struck down in
February 2020. The reader has never heard of SyRI and knows AI failures only
in the abstract. The piece has to put them inside the actual apparatus — what
data went in, what score came out, who acted on it — and inside the actual
people it touched, in the order the record supports, before it explains why
that shape of system tends to produce that shape of harm.

Open on a specific moment inside the system, not on a claim about algorithms
in general. Angwin, Larson, Mattu, and Kirchner's opening paragraph gives a
day, a bicycle, a scooter, a street, and nothing else — no claim yet about
what any of it means. A SyRI paragraph that opens on a specific address
flagged, a specific date a letter arrived, or the specific ward SyRI was run
in can do the same work: put the reader inside an ordinary moment before the
machinery around it becomes visible.

Where the record supports two comparable cases — a household flagged that
turned out clean, set beside one the system missed, or two households treated
differently for reasons the record can trace — setting them side by side and
letting the contradiction sit, the way Angwin et al. do with Prater and
Borden, argues harder than a sentence declaring the system unfair. This
depends on the record actually giving two comparable cases; where it doesn't,
a single flagged household or neighborhood, reported in full, can carry the
same weight alone.

Explain the mechanism in the same plain sentence shape used for the human
account, not in a separate technical register. Hill's paragraph on shadow
profiles defines the term in the sentence that introduces it and keeps the
same subject-verb-object rhythm she uses for her anecdotes. Where this piece
explains what SyRI's risk indicators were, how the model combined data across
agencies, or what the court's proportionality test asked of the state, that
explanation belongs in the same voice as the opening scene, not a shift into
a drier register that announces its own arrival.

Where the record holds an official document, a court's own language, or a
ministry memo that condemns the system on its own terms, quoting it and
stepping back does more work than a writer's adjective. Keefe's Blumenthal
letter and his one-sentence report of Purdue's guilty plea both let the
institution's own words carry the accusation, not his. The District Court's
2020 ruling on SyRI, and any ministry or parliamentary finding on the record,
can do the same here: state what was found, quote the reasoning where it is
sharp enough to stand on its own, and let it stand.

Close on a specific, sourced fact rather than a sentence that tells the
reader what SyRI means. All three exemplars end this way: Hill on a
stonewalled request, Angwin et al. on a rejected job application reported in
one plain quote, Keefe on a gate, a tree, and a groundskeeping routine. This
piece has the same option — what happened to the system after the ruling,
what a flagged household is doing now, what the government did or didn't do
next — reported flat, with nothing added that instructs the reader how to
feel about it.

Two decisions belong to this piece's own form rather than to any exemplar.
This is a lesson, not a magazine feature: the two bookends address the reader
directly and the body between them never does, so no direct address belongs
in the body, even where Hill briefly uses it in her own piece. And because
this is one lesson in a running desk on AI failures, this piece can avoid the
desk's own recent patterns: no "When you..." or "You have probably heard"
opener, no "By the end you'll know A, B, and C" closer, no dek built on a
"turned X into a Y" mold.

Calibration limit: Keefe's own sentences run longer and more atmospheric than
this desk's register — extended scene-setting, an ironic final image left
wordlessly to speak for itself. Take his discipline about letting the record
and an institution's own words carry the weight, and his refusal to
summarize; leave the length and the atmosphere behind. A 1,200–2,200-word
lesson for a general reader, written the way the press asks — plain claims,
stated directly — has no room for either.

## Julia Angwin, Jeff Larson, Surya Mattu, and Lauren Kirchner, "Machine Bias"

Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing

> "On a spring afternoon in 2014, Brisha Borden was running late to pick up
> her god-sister from school when she spotted an unlocked kid's blue Huffy
> bicycle and a silver Razor scooter. Borden and a friend grabbed the bike
> and scooter and tried to ride them down the street in the Fort Lauderdale
> suburb of Coral Springs."

Checked: https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing, retrieved 2026-09-26

The paragraph gives a day, two objects, a street, and nothing else — no word
yet signals that this will matter. The sentence that would tell the reader
what to feel about the theft is simply not there; the ordinariness of the
crime is left to do the setup on its own.

> "Yet something odd happened when Borden and Prater were booked into jail: A
> computer program spat out a score predicting the likelihood of each
> committing a future crime. Borden — who is black — was rated a high risk.
> Prater — who is white — was rated a low risk. Two years later, we know the
> computer algorithm got it exactly backward. Borden has not been charged
> with any new crimes. Prater is serving an eight-year prison term for
> subsequently breaking into a warehouse and stealing thousands of dollars'
> worth of electronics."

Checked: https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing, retrieved 2026-09-26

Two records are stated plainly, then the score each person got, then what
each person actually did next. No sentence here declares the system biased;
the contradiction between the score and the outcome is left to argue for
itself.

> "Jones, who had never been arrested before, was rated a medium risk. She
> completed probation and got the felony burglary charge reduced to
> misdemeanor trespassing, but she has still struggled to find work. 'I went
> to McDonald's and a dollar store, and they all said no because of my
> background,' she said. 'It's all kind of difficult and unnecessary.'"

Checked: https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing, retrieved 2026-09-26

The piece ends on Jones's own line, not on a sentence summarizing what her
struggle means. The paragraph right before it states the case's plain facts
— probation completed, charge reduced — without attaching a judgment word to
any of them.

## Kashmir Hill, "How Facebook Figures Out Everyone You've Ever Met"

Source: https://gizmodo.com/how-facebook-figures-out-everyone-youve-ever-met-1819822691

> "A man who years ago donated sperm to a couple, secretly, so they could
> have a child—only to have Facebook recommend the child as a person he
> should know. He still knows the couple but is not friends with them on
> Facebook."

Checked: https://gizmodo.com/how-facebook-figures-out-everyone-youve-ever-met-1819822691, retrieved 2026-09-26

Two sentences, no adjective describing how strange the situation is. The
facts alone — the secrecy, the algorithm's recommendation, the fact that he
still isn't friends with them — carry the strangeness.

> "Connections like these seem inexplicable if you assume Facebook only knows
> what you've told it about yourself. They're less mysterious if you know
> about the other file Facebook keeps on you—one that you can't see or
> control. Behind the Facebook profile you've built for yourself is another
> one, a shadow profile, built from the inboxes and smartphones of other
> Facebook users. Contact information you've never given the network gets
> associated with your account, making it easier for Facebook to more
> completely map your social connections."

Checked: https://gizmodo.com/how-facebook-figures-out-everyone-youve-ever-met-1819822691, retrieved 2026-09-26

Hill defines "shadow profile" inside the same sentence that introduces it,
in the same plain subject-verb-object shape she used for her anecdotes a
moment earlier. Nothing marks this as the technical section; the explanation
reads like the story that came before it.

> "Steinfeld also said that while Facebook doesn't currently 'offer a way for
> people to manage the contact information others have uploaded that might
> be related to them, this is something I've shared with the team.' As
> usual, I asked to speak with the People You May Know team directly, but was
> turned down."

Checked: https://gizmodo.com/how-facebook-figures-out-everyone-youve-ever-met-1819822691, retrieved 2026-09-26

The piece ends on a stonewalled request, reported flatly as "was turned
down" rather than flagged as evidence of anything. The last fact is set down
and the piece stops there.

## Patrick Radden Keefe, "The Family That Built an Empire of Pain"

Source: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2017/10/30/the-family-that-built-an-empire-of-pain

> "In July, 2001, Richard Blumenthal, who was then the attorney general of
> Connecticut, wrote to Richard Sackler. 'I have been increasingly dismayed
> and alarmed about the problems and escalating abuse of OxyContin,' he
> began, citing overdose deaths, addiction, pharmacy robberies, and 'the
> astonishing growth in state funding' that was being used to pay for
> OxyContin prescriptions through Medicaid and Medicare. Blumenthal
> acknowledged that other prescription drugs were also abused. 'But OxyContin
> is different,' he wrote. 'It is more powerful, more addictive, more widely
> sold, more illicitly available, and more publicized.' He urged Purdue to
> 'overhaul and reform' its marketing of OxyContin."

Checked: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2017/10/30/the-family-that-built-an-empire-of-pain, retrieved 2026-09-26

Keefe reports what an official wrote and to whom, then lets that official's
own words state the case against the company. He adds no adjective of his
own; the only choice visible here is which letter to quote and how much of
it.

> "In 2006, Purdue settled with Hanly's clients, for seventy-five million
> dollars. Shortly afterward, the company pleaded guilty, in a case brought
> by federal prosecutors in Virginia, to criminal charges of misbranding, and
> acknowledged that Purdue had marketed OxyContin 'with the intent to
> defraud or mislead.'"

Checked: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2017/10/30/the-family-that-built-an-empire-of-pain, retrieved 2026-09-26

One sentence carries the settlement figure, the criminal charge, and the
company's own admitted language, stacked without commentary between them.
The document's wording is doing the accusing, not the writer's.

> "We reached an ornamental wooden gate, beyond which was a yard dominated by
> a stately weeping willow. As I was admiring the tree, Jeff said that, for
> the people who maintained the grounds, it was 'a pain in the ass.' Whenever
> the wind picks up, he explained, branches break and scatter all over the
> lawn. 'But the place has to be flawless,' he said. 'There can't be a leaf
> on the ground.' So a crew comes by regularly, to clear away the mess."

Checked: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2017/10/30/the-family-that-built-an-empire-of-pain, retrieved 2026-09-26

The last image is a gate, a tree, and a groundskeeping habit. Keefe never
states the irony of a spotless lawn kept by money made from a company that
spread disorder; he sets the detail down and the piece ends there, with no
summarizing sentence at all.
