# Evidence record: when-ai-breaks/hirevue-facial-analysis (01)

The evidence firmly supports three of the commission's pillars: HireVue deployed AI
video-interview scoring at large scale, EPIC challenged it at the FTC in November 2019,
and HireVue removed the visual-analysis component in 2020 and announced it in January
2021. The scientific mechanism is well sourced: Barrett et al. (2019), the field's most
systematic review, concludes that facial configurations are not reliable and specific
enough to be read as diagnostic of any emotional state, which is the precondition
HireVue's facial scoring needed. Two things the writer must handle with care are also
well documented. First, how much facial analysis contributed to a score is contested
within the record itself: HireVue executives described facial input as 10–30 percent of a
score in 2019, but HireVue in 2021 said the same visual component added roughly 0.25
percent (up to 4 percent for customer-facing roles) of predictive power. Second, HireVue
never conceded the scientific-validity point. It framed the removal as a value decision
(language analysis had improved; visual "no longer significantly added value"), not as an
admission that facial scoring was baseless or biased. The record is thin, or blocked, on
the FTC's disposition of the complaint (no public enforcement outcome was reachable) and
on the primary weight figures at their original owners (the Washington Post and Fast
Company pages that first reported the 29 percent and 10–30 percent figures are
paywalled/bot-blocked; both were read inside EPIC's complaint, which quotes them). The
ORCAA audit, as reported, examined one narrow assessment and did not test the
facial-analysis construct at all.

```
URL:         https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1529100619832930 (DOI 10.1177/1529100619832930)
Kind:        Primary. The peer-reviewed scientific review; owns the field's assessment of
             the "common view." Read via the author-accepted version at the University of
             Glasgow open repository (eprints.gla.ac.uk/178385), which carries the same
             Executive Summary and Abstract as the published article.
Establishes: Barrett, Adolphs, Marsella, Martinez & Pollak (2019), "Emotional Expressions
             Reconsidered: Challenges to Inferring Emotion From Human Facial Movements,"
             Psychological Science in the Public Interest 20(1): 1–68. The review examined
             production and perception of facial movements across healthy adults in many
             cultures, infants and children, and congenitally blind people, for six
             emotion categories (anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, surprise). Its
             conclusion: people do move their faces in emotion-linked ways more than
             chance, but the configurations are too variable and context-dependent to be
             read as diagnostic of a specific emotional state, and perceivers do not infer
             emotion from configurations reliably and specifically either. The authors
             name automated emotion detection from faces, in industry and government, as
             an application built on the flawed common view. Caveat for this lesson: the
             review is about inferring EMOTION from faces. HireVue claimed to infer traits
             and job competence, a further and even less supported step; Barrett removes
             the necessary precondition (that faces reliably reveal inner states) rather
             than directly testing job-performance prediction.
Paraphrase:  The "common view" holds that a facial configuration maps one-to-one onto an
             emotional state. The review finds the evidence does not support treating
             smiles, scowls, or frowns as reliable and specific expressions of emotion; a
             given configuration such as a scowl "often communicates something other than
             an emotional state."
Locators:    Executive Summary (internal p.1); Abstract (internal p.3); author-accepted
             PDF read pp.6–9. Journal 20(1): 1–68.
Quote:       "instances of these emotion categories are NOT expressed with facial
             movements that are sufficiently reliable and specific across contexts,
             individuals, and cultures to be considered diagnostic displays of any
             emotional state. Nor do human perceivers, in fact, infer emotions from
             particular configurations of muscle movements in a sufficiently reliable and
             specific way that similarly generalizes." Also: "this common view continues
             to fuel commercial applications in industry and government (e.g., automated
             detection of emotions from faces)."
```

## Contradictions

- The central tension for the writer: how much facial analysis contributed to a score.
  In 2019, quoted by name, HireVue's CTO Loren Larsen put facial expressions at "10% to
  30%" of a score, and chief IO psychologist Nathan Mondragon put "Facial Action Units"
  at up to "29 percent" (EPIC complaint ¶¶28–29, quoting the Washington Post and Fast
  Company). In 2021, HireVue's CEO Kevin Parker said the same visual signal added "about
  0.25%" of predictive power in most cases and up to "4%" for customer-facing roles
  (Fortune). These are not obviously the same measurement — "share of a score" versus
  "marginal predictive power" can diverge — so the writer should present both with their
  dates and not collapse them. Do not assert facial analysis "determined" outcomes; the
  strongest defensible claim is that HireVue itself, before the controversy, described it
  as a substantial fraction of the score.

- Why HireVue removed it. HireVue's account (press release; Parker in Fortune) is that
  language analysis improved and visual analysis "no longer significantly added value" —
  a value/optics decision, made "proactively." EPIC and critics treat the removal as a
  response to the FTC complaint and public pressure. HireVue never conceded that facial
  scoring was scientifically baseless or biased. The invalidity case therefore rests on
  independent science (Barrett et al.) and EPIC's allegations, not on any HireVue
  admission. The writer should attribute the "baseless" judgment to the science and to
  EPIC, not present it as agreed.

- The audit does not support HireVue's broad implication. HireVue paired the removal with
  an audit it summarized as finding its assessments "work as advertised with regard to
  fairness and bias issues" (press release). But the ORCAA audit examined one early-career
  assessment, did not evaluate the tool's technical design, and sits behind an NDA (MIT
  Technology Review). It tested stakeholder-facing fairness process, not construct
  validity, and cannot bear the weight of a general "our AI is fair and works" claim.

- Scope of Barrett et al. The review refutes reliable, specific inference of EMOTION from
  facial configurations. HireVue claimed to infer traits and job competence — a further
  leap the review does not test directly. Barrett removes the foundation HireVue's facial
  claims needed; it does not itself run a job-performance study. State the mechanism at
  that altitude.

- Deployment figures come in two incompatible-looking pairs and must be dated. Late 2019
  (Washington Post via MIT Technology Review): 100+ companies, 1M+ applicants for the AI
  video assessment. January 2021 (HireVue's own release): 700+ customers, 18M+ video
  interviews, 114M chat engagements. EPIC (2019) cites "over 700 corporate customers" for
  HireVue overall. The likely reconciliation — total customer base versus the subset using
  AI-scored video — is not stated in the record, so do not merge the figures.
