## How this piece should sound

This lesson narrates the HireVue facial-analysis failure in order and then teaches
why the scoring behind it was invalid measurement, and the writing has to serve
both jobs. The first half needs the company, its leadership at the time, EPIC, the
dates, and the scale of deployment named. The second half teaches a reader who is
smart and widely read but holds none of this subject, so a term of art like facial
action unit, construct validity, or automation bias is defined in plain words where
it first appears, and the register stays calm and direct the whole way through.

The narration can run on concrete specifics and stay flat in tone. Kashmir Hill
opens on one person, a Thursday in January, an office, a driveway, and reports the
arrest with almost no adjectives, letting the handcuffs and the warrant paper carry
it. The HireVue story has its own specifics available: what the product was, how it
scored candidates on word choice, tone, and facial movement, the named enterprise
clients, the people who were ranked and filtered by it. Those details can do work
that adjectives would only dilute.

Where the account is contested, each side can be put at its strongest before the
piece weighs it. Richard A. Webster states the prosecution's defense of a discredited
technique in full, and Ed Yong lays out the skeptics' three objections in their own
logic, before either is answered. HireVue's own account of what facial analysis
contributed to a candidate's score, and the audit the company commissioned, can be
given that same fair statement, and the piece can say what evidence would settle a
point that stays disputed.

The teaching half can be made legible the way Yong makes a study legible. He says
what Many Labs 2 actually did, scales it against the studies it repeated, and tells
the reader why one finding matters before earning it with a plain example. Explaining
why a face-and-voice score fails as measurement can take the same care: what the
system measures, what it claims to measure, and what would have to be true for the
claim to hold. The editorial standard asks that reported fact, the underlying
science, and your own reading stay distinct, and the teaching section is where they
are easiest to blur.

A few smaller choices follow the same pattern. A deployment figure a reader cannot
scale on their own can be set beside one they already hold, the way Webster sets an
autopsy workload against a recommended annual maximum so the comparison does the
judging. Where an unvalidated score gated a real hiring decision, the distance
between how the score was described and how it was used can be shown from the record,
as Hill shows with the report line marking a result as an investigative lead only.
And when the scientific consensus enters, the cited source's own words can carry the
verdict while your voice stays flat, as Webster does with the National Academy of
Sciences finding.

The takeaway bookend is where the lesson lands its judgment, and a verdict is welcome
once the cited evidence has earned it. No hype and no doom: a grand word belongs only
after the argument that earns it, and the record here is strong enough that it rarely
will. The closing turn to where the same face-and-voice inference operates now can
name systems a reader actually meets instead of a general caution.

## Ed Yong, "Psychology's Replication Crisis Is Running Out of Excuses"

Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/science/archive/2018/11/psychologys-replication-crisis-real/576223/

> But skeptics have argued that the misleadingly named “crisis” has more mundane explanations. First, the replication attempts themselves might be too small. Second, the researchers involved might be incompetent, or lack the know-how to properly pull off the original experiments. Third, people vary, and two groups of scientists might end up with very different results if they do the same experiment on two different groups of volunteers.

Yong sets out the objection at full strength, in three plain numbered points, before he
answers any of it, so a reader meets the strongest case against his own view before the
reply. His own position is still present, folded into the two-word aside about the
misleadingly named crisis, which registers where he stands without letting him skip the
fair hearing.

> The Many Labs 2 project was specifically designed to address these criticisms. With 15,305 participants in total, the new experiments had, on average, 60 times as many volunteers as the studies they were attempting to replicate. The researchers involved worked with the scientists behind the original studies to vet and check every detail of the experiments beforehand. And they repeated those experiments many times over, with volunteers from 36 different countries, to see if the studies would replicate in some cultures and contexts but not others. “It’s been the biggest bear of a project,” says Brian Nosek from the Center for Open Science, who helped to coordinate it. “It’s 28 papers’ worth of stuff in one.”

The paragraph answers each objection by describing what the study actually did, and the
figure of 60 times as many volunteers is what makes the scale mean something to a reader.
Nosek is visible as a person because Yong lets him talk in ordinary words, the biggest
bear of a project, instead of paraphrasing him into method.

> It’s worth dwelling on this because it’s a serious blow to one of the most frequently cited criticisms of the “reproducibility crisis” rhetoric. Surely, skeptics argue, it’s a fantasy to expect studies to replicate everywhere. “There’s a massive deference to the sample,” Nosek says. “Your replication attempt failed? It must be because you did it in Ohio and I did it in Virginia, and people are different. But these results suggest that we can’t just wave those failures away very easily.”

Yong tells the reader why the point matters, that it is a serious blow to a common
criticism, and then earns the claim rather than leaving it as an assertion. The abstract
idea becomes checkable through Nosek's concrete example of Ohio and Virginia, and Nosek's
conversational voice is what carries it.

## Kashmir Hill, "Wrongfully Accused by an Algorithm"

Source: https://www.seattletimes.com/business/technology/wrongfully-accused-by-an-algorithm/ (New York Times report, read here in the Seattle Times reprint)

> On a Thursday afternoon in January, Robert Julian-Borchak Williams was in his office at an automotive supply company when he got a call from the Detroit Police Department telling him to come to the station to be arrested. He thought at first that it was a prank.
>
> An hour later, when he pulled into his driveway in a quiet subdivision in Farmington Hills, Michigan, a police car pulled up behind, blocking him in. Two officers got out and handcuffed Williams on his front lawn in front of his wife and two young daughters, who were distraught. Police wouldn’t say why he was being arrested, only showing him a piece of paper with his photo and the words “felony warrant” and “larceny.”

Hill opens on a named person and a specific afternoon, and reports the arrest almost
without adjectives, so the front lawn, the two daughters, and the warrant paper carry the
weight. Williams is a person on the page from the first line, and the restraint of the
telling is Hill's own presence in it.

> When one of these subcontractors develops an algorithm for recognizing faces, DataWorks attempts to judge its effectiveness by running searches using low-quality images of individuals it knows are present in a system. “We’ve tested a lot of garbage out there,” Pastorini said. These checks, he added, are not “scientific”; DataWorks does not formally measure the systems’ accuracy or bias.

Hill reports that the vendor does not formally measure how well its own tool works, and
lets the general manager admit it in his own words. The line about testing a lot of
garbage does more than a characterization would, and Pastorini is visible in his own
candor.

> “This document is not a positive identification,” the file says in bold capital letters at the top. “It is an investigative lead only and is not probable cause for arrest.”
>
> This is what technology providers and law enforcement always emphasize when defending facial recognition: It is only supposed to be a clue in the case, not a smoking gun. Before arresting Williams, investigators might have sought other evidence that he committed the theft, such as eyewitness testimony, location data from his phone or proof that he owned the clothing that the suspect was wearing.

Hill quotes the disclaimer printed on the report itself, then states plainly the gap
between how the tool is defended and how it was actually used. The point that a lead was
treated as proof comes from the record, from the file's own capitalized warning, and Hill
never has to tell the reader to be alarmed.

## Richard A. Webster, "Jimmie Duncan, Convicted on Allegedly Fabricated Bite Mark Analysis, May Be Executed by Louisiana Anyway"

Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/louisiana-jimmie-duncan-bite-mark-analysis-death-row-junk-science (Verite News, co-published with ProPublica)

> Louisiana prosecutors say they have no doubt Duncan is guilty and insist he be put to death without delay. In a Jan. 9 brief, they acknowledged questions surrounding the credibility of bite mark analysis but said there is no consensus on whether it is junk science. They also downplayed the importance of the evidence presented by the dentist, saying it was not needed to connect Duncan to the crime scene, despite his defense team’s argument that it was the only physical evidence linking Duncan to the child’s death.

Webster gives the prosecution its best case in its own terms, that there is no consensus
and that the disputed evidence was not needed anyway, and ties it to the specific brief it
came from. The opposing side is stated at full strength before the piece weighs it, and
the reader can see the prosecutors' own reasoning laid out, not a summary of it.

> On multiple occasions, Hayne claimed to be performing up to 90% of all autopsies in Mississippi and boasted that he completed 1,200 to 1,800 procedures in a single year. If true, that would far exceed the recommended annual maximum of 250 set by the National Association of Medical Examiners. When pathologists surpass that number, they risk engaging in shortcuts and making mistakes, according to the organization.

The workload figure means little on its own, so Webster sets 1,200 to 1,800 against a
recommended maximum of 250, and the comparison delivers the judgment without his having to
state it. The number is anchored to something the reader can hold, and the voice around it
stays flat and sourced.

> A year after Brooks and Brewer were freed, the National Academy of Sciences issued a damning report on bite mark analysis in which it stated there is “no evidence of an existing scientific basis for identifying an individual to the exclusion of all others.” Other reports found that skin cannot accurately hold the form of teeth, that there is no proof teeth provide unique individual markers and that analysts often have trouble determining if a bite mark is in fact a bite mark and if the source is even human.

Webster brings the scientific consensus in through the report's exact words, and keeps his
own sentence plain around the quotation so the authority carries the finding. The strongest
line in the paragraph is the one he lets the source say.
