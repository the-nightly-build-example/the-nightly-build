# Editorial review: when-ai-breaks/itutorgroup-age-discrimination (editor/01)

## Skeptic

Thesis: two failures share the "AI hiring bias" headline, and iTutorGroup is the
simple kind — an explicit date-of-birth rule a person wrote, which is exactly
why the EEOC could win the case by pointing at it — while the harder, more
common kind is the proxy a model learns on its own. The claims it stands on:
(1) iTutorGroup programmed its software to reject women 55 or older and men 60
or older, rejecting 200+ qualified applicants, caught when Wendy Pincus
reapplied a day later with a younger birth date; (2) the case settled for
$365,000 with no admission of liability; (3) the primary record describes an
explicit programmed rule, not AI/machine learning; (4) the same distinction is
now the live hinge in Mobley v. Workday.

I opened every cited source. Claims (1), (2), (4) held against the primaries.

- Every age, date, and dollar figure is exact. I read the complaint PDF: the
  operative allegation (para. 19b) says "female applicants age 55 or older and
  male applicants age 60 or older," and para. 19e says "more than 200 other ...
  applicants age 55 and over." The article uses "55 or older" / "60 or older"
  and the "age 55 and over" class for both sexes, correctly following the
  evidence record's resolution of the "over 55/60" wording in the complaint's
  recital. $365,000, filed May 5 2022, decree So-Ordered September 8 2023 by
  Judge Pamela K. Chen, March 29-30 2020, and the ADEA 40-and-older floor
  (defined at first use) all check out against the complaint, the decree, the
  ADEA page, and both EEOC releases.
- The settlement honesty held: no admission of liability, tutors disputed as
  employees, US business exited, forward obligations contingent on a future
  Resumption Date — all in the decree per the evidence record.
- Mobley held. I read the March 2026 order (Document 267, filed 03/06/26, Judge
  Rita F. Lin): it says "extensive work experience can be used as a proxy for
  age," exactly as the article renders it, and the case is live. The July 2024
  birthdate-filter-vs-participating-tool line matches the evidence quote.
- The "first workplace AI settlement" label is correctly attributed to the law
  firms (Greenberg Traurig, s6), never to the EEOC; Epstein Becker Green (s7)
  supports the deliberate-programming point. Both secondaries resolve.

One central claim BROKE. The mechanism section asserts (heading: "The record
never says 'AI'"; body: "nowhere in the EEOC's complaint, the consent decree, or
either of the agency's press releases do the words 'artificial intelligence,'
'AI,' 'machine learning,' or even 'algorithm' appear," cited to s1/s2/s4/s5).
I opened s4, the 2022 EEOC suit release, and it plainly contains all of that
language: Chair Burrows is quoted invoking the EEOC's "Artificial Intelligence
and Algorithmic Fairness Initiative," and the release's boilerplate names
"artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning, and other emerging
technologies." So the sweeping claim is false, and the section heading — display
text reaching every skimming reader — is a false claim. The complaint (I read
the PDF: zero AI/ML/algorithm hits), the decree, and the 2023 settlement release
(s5, verified) are clean, so the truth is narrower and still carries the lesson:
the EEOC never describes iTutorGroup's *software* as AI or machine learning; the
only AI language in the record is boilerplate about the agency's separate
initiative. The evidence record itself asserts the 2022 release "Uses no
AI/ML/algorithm language," so the record and the source I opened disagree. Per
the skill I route this rather than settle it: the researcher must correct the
evidence record and the writer must recast the heading and the claim. The
honest-mechanism framing survives; the overbroad factual assertion does not.

Data-nb-kind audit: 7 primary (s1-s5, s8, s9), 2 secondary (s6, s7). All labels
are correct — the two law-firm analyses are the only secondaries, and every
court/agency document is primary. Every citation href resolves to the document's
own host, not a reporter's rehost (clearinghouse.net hosts the actual
ECF-stamped filings; EEOC and govinfo are the agencies' own pages). One soft
miss: s8's href is the CourtListener docket index, though the entry labels it the
specific July 12 2024 order; the load-bearing birthdate-filter quote lives in
that order, so the link should point at the order document, not the docket.

## Cut

The prose is clean on slop. Sentences are concrete, sourced, and carried by
their nouns; the sequential narration holds the Krebs/Howe register the voice
guide sets. I found no empty-conclusion, puffery, or vague-attribution
sentences to delete. Zero body sentences failed the slop test.

Borderline edges I tested and kept because each carries a real, earned point:
"What the $365,000 settled was the past" (resolves the past-harm vs
contingent-future split the paragraph built); "the forbidden reason is stated
in the rule itself, not inferred from a pattern" (earned negative parallelism —
the piece names the inference-from-pattern misconception two sentences earlier);
"It would be easy to read this as a story about complicated technology. It is
the opposite" (a bookend, allowed to address the reader, and it resolves the
opener's setup). The dek stands in the case's own nouns (the reapplication, the
200, the $365,000) and does not copy a neighbor's construction. Headings are
concrete and varied — no "Background"/"What happened" scaffolding.

Furniture. Two pieces: a stat strip and the ¶19 source asset. The stat strip
(55+, 60+, 200+, $365,000) repeated four figures the reader had just met in the
dek and the three orientation paragraphs it sat directly beneath; it emphasized
nothing new and made the section read as prose plus a block. I cut it. The
recent-pattern note flagged over-furnishing on exactly these figures and named
the stat strip the likelier cut. The source asset does real work prose cannot —
it shows the rule in the government's filed words — so it stays.

## Reader

Read straight through as the paper's declared reader, the piece gives something
the sources alone do not: a diagnostic for reading any "AI hiring bias" story —
an explicit rule a person wrote at one end, a learned proxy at the other, with
the Mobley birthdate-filter line as the test for which kind you are looking at.
No single cited source hands the reader that continuum. The original-work
sentence claims the same thing, and the article delivers it. The prose sits
closer to the voice-guide exemplars than a median summary. The one thing that
undercuts the read is the false "never says AI" claim: a reader who clicks s4 to
check the article's own "read the actual record" invitation finds the article
wrong, which is why it must be fixed before publication.

## Edits

- Removed the stat strip in the orientation section (55+, 60+, 200+, $365,000):
  over-furnishing, every figure already carried by the dek and adjacent prose.

## Required work

  Chair Burrows' quote invoking the "Artificial Intelligence and Algorithmic
  Fairness Initiative" and the release's initiative boilerplate. The record's
  claim that this release "Uses no AI/ML/algorithm language" is wrong. The
  complaint, the decree, and the 2023 settlement release remain clean of that

- writer: Recast the section heading "The record never says 'AI'" and the
  sentence "nowhere in the EEOC's complaint, the consent decree, or either of
  the agency's press releases do the words ... appear." The accurate,
  still-load-bearing point: the EEOC never describes iTutorGroup's software as
  AI or machine learning — the documents call it software "programmed ... to
  automatically reject" — and the only AI language in the record is boilerplate
  about the agency's separate initiative, not a characterization of this system.
  Do not restore the sweeping "never appears" claim.
- writer: Point s8's href at the specific July 12 2024 Mobley order document
  rather than the docket index, so the birthdate-filter quote lands on the order
  a reader can verify.

## Decision

revise — the mechanism section's central claim, including its section heading,
is factually false against the 2022 EEOC release it cites; the honest-mechanism
lesson and every age, date, and dollar figure otherwise hold, so the fix is a
research correction and a recast of one heading and one sentence.
