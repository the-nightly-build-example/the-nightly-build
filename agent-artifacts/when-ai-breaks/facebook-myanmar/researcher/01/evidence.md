# Evidence: when-ai-breaks/facebook-myanmar (01)

The record firmly supports the article's core factual spine: Facebook became
effectively synonymous with the internet for Myanmar's ~20 million users as the
country came online after 2011; the company had almost no Burmese-language
moderation capacity during the years hate speech spread (one Burmese reviewer in
2014, ~60 by August 2018 for ~20 million users, moderation outsourced to Accenture
in Kuala Lumpur, no employees in the country); the UN Fact-Finding Mission (2018)
called Facebook "a useful instrument for those seeking to spread hate" and
attributed genocidal intent and crimes against humanity to the Tatmadaw; Facebook,
through its commissioned BSR assessment and its own statement, conceded it "wasn't
doing enough" to prevent the platform being used to "foment division and incite
offline violence," and removed 20 military-linked accounts and pages in August
2018; and litigation followed (the December 2021 ~$150bn class action; Amnesty's
2022 reparations report). The record is **strongest on the moderation gap and on
what each party said, and weakest exactly where the commission's angle is
sharpest**: no source in the record contains Myanmar-specific engagement telemetry
showing how much anti-Rohingya content the ranking/recommendation system
algorithmically boosted. The strongest algorithmic-amplification claim, Amnesty's
"proactively amplified," rests largely on general Facebook Papers evidence about
engagement ranking plus inference, not on Burmese-language distribution data, which
Facebook told the UN it could not provide. The military's primary responsibility is
uncontested across every source; the contested variable is the size and mechanism
of Facebook's contribution. Both are recorded fully in Contradictions.

## Contradictions

The commission's critical question -- how much of the atrocity is attributable to
Facebook's engagement-driven amplification versus the military's deliberate action
-- is genuinely disputed in the record, but along a narrower axis than "Facebook
vs the military." **No source disputes that the Tatmadaw bears primary
responsibility.** The UN attributes genocidal intent and crimes against humanity to
the military chain of command (A/HRC/39/64 paras 87-88); Amnesty attributes the
killings and ethnic cleansing to "the Myanmar security forces"; even the plaintiffs
plead Facebook as "a substantial cause," not the cause. The genuine disagreement is
about (a) the **size** of Facebook's contribution and (b) the **mechanism** -- an
engagement-ranking algorithm proactively amplifying, versus a platform's reach
being deliberately exploited by bad actors under near-absent moderation.

1. **Strongest platform-share claim (Amnesty, 2022):** Meta's "content-shaping
   algorithms proactively amplified and promoted" anti-Rohingya incitement; the
   company "substantially contributed" and was not a "passive and neutral platform."
   This is the load-bearing source for the commission's recommender-amplification
   angle. Its stake and its evidentiary basis both matter: Amnesty is an advocacy
   organization seeking reparations, and its algorithmic claim leans on general
   Facebook Papers evidence about how engagement ranking behaves plus inference,
   not Myanmar-specific data quantifying how much anti-Rohingya content the feed
   algorithmically boosted. The article can cite "proactively amplified" as
   Amnesty's conclusion; it should not present it as an established, quantified
   measurement of the platform's share.

2. **The careful middle (UN FFM, 2018):** "significant" role, "useful instrument"
   -- but the Mission expressly declined to assert the causal link, saying it "must
   be independently and thoroughly examined," and noted Facebook could not provide
   country-specific data. The UN flagged the platform; it did not adjudicate its
   share.

3. **The company-commissioned hedge (BSR, 2018):** frames the harm as "bad actors"
   and "a minority of users" exploiting the platform, and states plainly that "the
   actual relationship between content posted on Facebook and offline harm is not
   fully understood." Facebook's own admission is only that it "wasn't doing
   enough" to prevent misuse -- a failure-to-moderate framing, never an admission
   that its algorithm proactively amplified the incitement. The gap between BSR's
   "bad actors exploit the platform" and Amnesty's "the algorithm amplified" is the
   central mechanism dispute; do not let the writer collapse them.

4. **Explicit pushback on overstatement (Douek/Keller, 2018; Rio via Amnesty,
   2020):** the spread was "not merely organic" but covertly driven by ~700 military
   personnel, a deliberate-exploitation problem distinct from a purely structural
   algorithmic one; the counterfactual (how much would have spread without the
   military campaign) is hard to construct; and it is "difficult to tie what
   happened in Northern Rakhine in 2017 to individual pieces of Facebook content."

**What would settle it:** Myanmar-specific distribution data showing how the
ranking/recommendation system apportioned reach to anti-Rohingya content relative
to a neutral baseline (the very country-specific data Facebook told the UN it could
not provide), plus a defensible counterfactual for the campaign's trajectory absent
the platform. Absent that, the defensible synthesis the record supports: engagement
ranking amplified inflammatory content and near-absent Burmese moderation let it run
with little friction, materially widening the reach of a campaign the military
deliberately drove -- a substantial contribution of contested magnitude, not a
measured share and not the primary cause.

A minor factual contradiction to keep straight: Facebook's August 2018 action folds
together two different removals -- a content/human-rights ban of 20 named
military-linked individuals and organizations (Min Aung Hlaing, Myawady TV), and,
separately, takedowns of covert "coordinated inauthentic behavior" pages. Coverage
sometimes merges the counts; the newsroom post above is the primary for the
20-entity / 18-account / 52-page figures.
