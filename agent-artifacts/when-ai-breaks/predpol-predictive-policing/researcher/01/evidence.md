# Evidence record: when-ai-breaks/predpol-predictive-policing (01)

The record supports the commission's angle solidly: The Markup's 2023 investigation
(with its own published methodology) owns a precise, checkable accuracy figure for
Geolitica/PredPol in one department (Plainfield, NJ) — under half a percent of
23,631 predictions matched a later reported crime of the same type in the same
box during the same shift — and the founding papers, read firsthand, show the
model is literally an earthquake-aftershock model relabeled for crime. The two
feedback-loop papers (Lum & Isaac 2016; Ensign et al. 2018) were read in full and
formally demonstrate the mechanism the commission wants taught. The LAPD Office
of Inspector General's 2019 audit was read in full and independently confirms
that the department could not show PredPol reduced crime and that most tracked
"dosage" was drive-throughs of a few seconds. Where the record is thin: the
original 2015 Mohler et al. field-trial paper in JASA — the study that produced
the company's best affirmative evidence (a 7.4% crime-rate reduction) — sits
behind a paywall I could not open directly; its findings are triangulated
through two independent readers who did read it (a peer-reviewed paper
co-authored by two of PredPol's own founders, and the LAPD OIG audit, which
cites the same study and states the same figures), but I did not verify the
number against the primary source itself. Geolitica declined to comment on the
2023 accuracy findings specifically; the company's clearest on-the-record
defense of its methodology comes from a 2021 investigation into a different
question (racial bias), and from a co-designer of the algorithm defending the
2015 field trial, not from a response to the hit-rate critique itself. That gap
is flagged in Contradictions and should be named in the article rather than
papered over.

URL:         https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.09847 (also published as Ensign, D.,
             Friedler, S. A., Neville, S., Scheidegger, C. & Venkatasubramanian,

Kind:        primary — read the full paper.
Establishes: The formal, provable version of the feedback loop: a mathematical
             model (built explicitly around PredPol's own published equation)
             showing that letting police-discovered crime retrain the model
             makes the model converge to "which neighborhood we searched most"
             rather than "which neighborhood has the most crime" — regardless
             of the true crime rates — and a fix.
Paraphrase:  The paper builds directly on Lum & Isaac (2016) and explicitly
             writes out PredPol's own published crime-rate equation from
             Mohler et al. (2015) as the system it is modeling. It distinguishes
             "discovered incidents" (crime an officer finds only because they
             were sent to that neighborhood by the algorithm) from "reported
             incidents" (crime residents call in regardless of patrol
             assignment) and shows, using a generalized Pólya urn model, that
             if a system updates only on discovered incidents, it will
             converge to sending 100% of patrols to whichever neighborhood was
             favored first — even if the two neighborhoods' true crime rates
             are nearly identical (e.g., 10% vs 11%) — a "go with the winner"
             runaway effect proven formally (Lemma 4). Reported incidents
             alone would converge correctly, but any nonzero weight on
             discovered incidents biases the result away from the true rate;
             the paper derives the exact algebraic condition for how bad the
             bias gets. Empirically simulating actual PredPol code on Lum &
             Isaac's Oakland drug-crime data confirms the runaway effect
             (Figure 1/2: without correction, predicted deployment converges
             to sending nearly all patrols to one region regardless of true
             relative rates; with the paper's proposed correction — a
             rejection-sampling filter on discovered incidents — deployment
             converges to the correct true ratio). The paper also explicitly
             flags PredPol's design as conflating "amount of crime and
             incident data" (footnote 1) — i.e., the model predicts where
             incidents will be recorded, not where crime occurs.

### Contradictions

- **What the accuracy number measures, and what defenders say it misses.** The
  Markup's 2023 Plainfield analysis defines "accuracy" as hits ÷ total
  predictions issued (0.1%–0.6% by crime type), after excluding the small
  number of boxes officers actually visited, on the theory that a visited box
  where no crime occurred could reflect successful deterrence rather than a
  bad prediction. Geolitica's general public position (as characterized by The
  Markup, with a citation to outside deterrence research rather than a company
  statement) is that officer presence at a predicted location can itself
  prevent the predicted crime, which a simple hit/miss count would then score
  as a "miss." The Markup's own data undercuts how much that defense can
  explain in Plainfield specifically: officers were recorded visiting only 129
  of 23,760 predicted boxes (about half a percent) during the whole
  10-month period, and including those 129 back into the analysis did not
  materially change the results — there simply was not enough patrol
  "dosage" for a deterrence effect to be hiding the software's failures at
  scale in this dataset. What would settle it: a jurisdiction with complete,
  verified dosage logs across many more boxes (unlike Plainfield's sparse
  data), analyzed the same way, to see whether visited-but-quiet boxes really
  differ from unvisited-but-quiet boxes in a way consistent with deterrence
  rather than simply "most boxes are quiet because crime in any given box on
  any given shift is rare."

- **Company response to the accuracy exposé vs. the bias exposé.** Geolitica
  did not respond at all to The Markup's 2023 accuracy findings ("did not
  respond to multiple requests for comment," both before and after
  publication). Two years earlier, on the *bias* findings, CEO Brian MacDonald
  did respond at length, on the record, calling that dataset "incomplete" and
  "erroneous" (specific, checkable objections The Markup says it had already
  accounted for) while not disputing the predictions' authenticity, and
  defending the company's design choice not to fix a known racial-disparity
  problem its own founders had documented in 2018. The record therefore
  supports "the company disputed the bias critique on the record but did not
  contest the accuracy critique at all" — a real and notable asymmetry, not an
  inconsistency to smooth over.

- **The founders' bias defense (2018 RCT paper) vs. the 2021 nationwide bias
  finding.** Brantingham, Valasik & Mohler (2018), using real arrest data from
  the original randomized LAPD field trial, found no statistically significant
  difference in the racial breakdown of arrests between algorithm-directed and
  analyst-directed patrol days. The Markup/Gizmodo's 2021 analysis of actual
  nationwide predictions delivered to 38 departments found a strong,
  consistent relationship between predicted-box density and neighborhood
  race/income. These are not strictly contradictory — one measures whether
  arrests were disproportionately of one race *within a single controlled
  experiment comparing two targeting methods*, the other measures where
  predictions were geographically concentrated *across real, uncontrolled
  deployments nationwide* — but a reader could easily conflate them, and the
  founders' own 2018 conference paper (cited only secondhand here, via the
  2021 Markup piece, not read directly) already found the algorithm would
  target Black and Latino neighborhoods far more than White ones in a
  simulated deployment, which sits uneasily next to the RCT's arrest-parity
  finding. What would settle it: applying the RCT's box-level, per-crime
  arrest-rate methodology to a large multi-city deployment rather than three
  divisions of one department over about a year and a half.

- **Santa Cruz ban date.** Secondary sources disagree on the exact date of the
  city council's ban: Route Fifty states August 11, 2020; Governing states
  June 24, 2020 (unanimous); a separate summary found in early searching cited
  June 23, 2020. This looks like the difference between an initial unanimous
  vote/first reading (likely late June) and final ordinance adoption (likely
  August), which is a normal two-step municipal process, but I did not
  obtain the Santa Cruz municipal ordinance or council minutes themselves to
  confirm which date corresponds to which step. A writer should not commit to
  a single date without checking the city's own record or resolving which
  event each secondary source is describing.

- **LAPD's stated reason for dropping PredPol.** The city's own memo (via
  BuzzFeed News) attributes the April 2020 discontinuation to COVID-era
  budget cuts, not to the bias or accuracy criticism; Stop LAPD Spying
  Coalition organizer Hamid Khan is quoted disputing that account and
  crediting years of activist pressure instead. Both claims are attributed to
  a named party in a position to know (LAPD's own chief via memo; a named
  activist), so this is a genuine, unresolved dispute about motive, not a
  factual error — record it as competing accounts, not as settled fact either
  way.
