## How this piece should sound

This lesson explains why a predictive-policing tool can be both self-reinforcing
and barely accurate, for a reader who is smart and well-read but has never
opened a codebase. The press voice already sets the register: plain claims,
concrete stakes, no fuss, be understood over sounding good. Nothing below
should push the piece toward drama the material doesn't need. Predictive
policing sent real patrols into real neighborhoods on a record that turned out
to be close to noise, and that fact carries its own weight; the writing's job
is to lay out the mechanism so plainly that the reader can trace it
themselves, the way Yong lays out cause and effect below without ever pausing
to rate how bad the failure was.

Anchor the accuracy figure the way Angwin's team anchors theirs: give the exact
number, and set it against a comparison the reader already owns rather than a
bare percentage. A hit rate on its own is an abstraction; a hit rate set beside
a coin flip, or beside chance in a box that rarely sees a crime on a given
shift, is something the reader can immediately weigh. The same habit applies to
citing The Markup's own numbers — say what was held constant and over what
period in the same sentence that gives the figure, the way the mortgage-lending
piece folds its methodology into its finding instead of setting it off as a
footnote.

The feedback loop is this lesson's hardest idea and the one most likely to
default into an abstraction ("a self-reinforcing loop exists"). Build it the
way Yong builds a chain of compounding failure: one sentence, concrete nouns,
each clause causing the next, so the mechanism is visible in the syntax before
it's named. Patrols go to a box, recorded crime rises there, the model reads
that as confirmation, more patrols follow — that chain can carry the idea
before the term "feedback loop" is ever used, and the term then labels
something the reader has already watched happen.

When PredPol, Geolitica, or a defender of the software answers the accuracy
finding, give them a real quote and then say plainly what it does and doesn't
address, the way both exemplars below handle a company's or an industry
group's pushback. A rebuttal that disputes methodology without naming a flaw
is worth reporting in exactly that shape: quoted at length, then checked
against what it actually addresses.

Open on something specific before the aggregate figure — a box, a shift, a
department, a date — the way both accountability pieces below ground an
algorithmic claim in a person or place before the numbers arrive. This
article's record is about places and patrol shifts rather than a single
defendant, so the model to take is the anchoring itself, not the shape of a
biographical anecdote: name the block, the department, the year, before
naming the rate.

## Ed Yong, "How the Pandemic Defeated America"

Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2020/09/coronavirus-american-failure/614191/

> "In the first half of 2020, SARS‑CoV‑2—the new coronavirus behind the disease COVID‑19—infected 10 million people around the world and killed about half a million. But few countries have been as severely hit as the United States, which has just 4 percent of the world's population but a quarter of its confirmed COVID‑19 cases and deaths. These numbers are estimates. The actual toll, though undoubtedly higher, is unknown, because the richest country in the world still lacks sufficient testing to accurately count its sick citizens."

The paragraph gives the hard numbers first and only then tells the reader those numbers are a floor, not a ceiling. Yong doesn't hedge the whole passage with qualifiers; he states the count plainly, then states the limit of the count in its own sentence, so the reader knows exactly which part of the claim is solid and which is an admitted gap.

> "The CDC developed and distributed its own diagnostic tests in late January. These proved useless because of a faulty chemical component. Tests were in such short supply, and the criteria for getting them were so laughably stringent, that by the end of February, tens of thousands of Americans had likely been infected but only hundreds had been tested. The official data were so clearly wrong that The Atlantic developed its own volunteer-led initiative—the COVID Tracking Project—to count cases."

Four short sentences move from cause to effect to consequence without a single sentence announcing that a system failed. The failure is visible in what happened next: a faulty component, then a shortage, then an undercount so bad that the magazine built its own tracker. Yong is present in the choice of what to report, not in any comment on it.

> "Meanwhile, the United States underperformed across the board, and its errors compounded. The dearth of tests allowed unconfirmed cases to create still more cases, which flooded the hospitals, which ran out of masks, which are necessary to limit the virus's spread. Twitter amplified Trump's misleading messages, which raised fear and anxiety among people, which led them to spend more time scouring for information on Twitter. Even seasoned health experts underestimated these compounded risks."

The middle sentence is one clause chained into the next by "which," and each link is a plain, checkable fact rather than a judgment. The compounding is demonstrated by the sentence's own structure before the word "compounded" is even used again. That's a person choosing syntax to do the explaining, instead of a sentence that tells the reader a feedback existed.

## Julia Angwin, Jeff Larson, Surya Mattu and Lauren Kirchner, "Machine Bias"

Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing

> "On a spring afternoon in 2014, Brisha Borden was running late to pick up her god-sister from school when she spotted an unlocked kid's blue Huffy bicycle and a silver Razor scooter. Borden and a friend grabbed the bike and scooter and tried to ride them down the street in the Fort Lauderdale suburb of Coral Springs.
>
> Just as the 18-year-old girls were realizing they were too big for the tiny conveyances — which belonged to a 6-year-old boy — a woman came running after them saying, "That's my kid's stuff." Borden and her friend immediately dropped the bike and scooter and walked away."

The piece opens on a specific bike, a specific street, a specific age, before it ever mentions software. Every noun is concrete: a Huffy, a Razor scooter, a 6-year-old's stuff. The reporters are visible in what they chose to include — the detail that the girls dropped the stolen items and walked away — which sets up the contrast the whole piece is built on before the reader has been told there is one.

> "The score proved remarkably unreliable in forecasting violent crime: Only 20 percent of the people predicted to commit violent crimes actually went on to do so.
>
> When a full range of crimes were taken into account — including misdemeanors such as driving with an expired license — the algorithm was somewhat more accurate than a coin flip. Of those deemed likely to re-offend, 61 percent were arrested for any subsequent crimes within two years."

Both paragraphs give the number and immediately give the reader something to measure it against: a plain percentage for the narrow claim, then a coin flip for the broad one. Neither comparison needs a footnote to be understood. The writers trust the reader to do the comparing once they've been handed a benchmark, rather than telling the reader the number is bad.

> "The algorithm used to create the Florida risk scores is a product of a for-profit company, Northpointe. The company disputes our analysis.
>
> In a letter, it criticized ProPublica's methodology and defended the accuracy of its test: "Northpointe does not agree that the results of your analysis, or the claims being made based upon that analysis, are correct or that they accurately reflect the outcomes from the application of the model.""

The company's objection is quoted at its full length rather than paraphrased down to a clause, and it's placed right after the finding it disputes, not buried afterward. The reporters don't characterize the objection as weak or evasive; they let its own generality — disputing "the results" and "the claims" without naming what's wrong with either — speak for itself.

## Emmanuel Martinez and Lauren Kirchner, "The Secret Bias Hidden in Mortgage-Approval Algorithms"

Source: https://themarkup.org/denied/2021/08/25/the-secret-bias-hidden-in-mortgage-approval-algorithms

> "The new four-bedroom house in Charlotte, N.C., was Crystal Marie and Eskias McDaniels's personal American dream, the reason they had moved to this Southern town from pricey Los Angeles a few years ago. A lush, long lawn, 2,700 square feet of living space, a neighborhood pool and playground for their son, Nazret. All for $375,000.
>
> Prequalifying for the mortgage was a breeze. They said they had saved much more than they would need for the down payment, had very good credit—scores of 805 and 725—and earned roughly six figures each, she in marketing at a utility company and Eskias representing a pharmaceutical company. The monthly mortgage payment was less than they'd paid for rent in Los Angeles for years."

The couple's qualifications are given as figures — the square footage, the price, both credit scores, both jobs — before the piece ever says a word about denial or bias. Naming the numbers this early is what makes the later reversal legible: a reader who has just been told the credit scores can recognize exactly how strong the file was when it gets turned down.

> "Holding 17 different factors steady in a complex statistical analysis of more than two million conventional mortgage applications for home purchases, we found that lenders were 40 percent more likely to turn down Latino applicants for loans, 50 percent more likely to deny Asian/Pacific Islander applicants, and 70 percent more likely to deny Native American applicants than similar White applicants. Lenders were 80 percent more likely to reject Black applicants than similar White applicants. These are national rates."

The method is inside the same sentence as the result: how many factors were controlled, how many applications, what kind of loan. A reader never has to hunt for the caveat that makes the finding credible, because the reporters put it in the clause doing the reporting rather than setting it apart as a disclaimer.

> "We sent our complete analysis to industry representatives: The American Bankers Association, The Mortgage Bankers Association, The Community Home Lenders Association, and The Credit Union National Association. They all criticized it generally, saying the public data is not complete enough to draw conclusions, but did not point to any flaws in our computations."

The sentence names every group that was asked, then reports both halves of what happened — that they objected, and that the objection never located a specific error. Saying so directly, in one sentence, is how the piece stays fair to the industry's response without pretending the response undercut the finding.
