# Editorial review: when-ai-breaks/workday-hiring-screening (editor/01)

## Skeptic

Thesis: a federal court has let Workday, the maker of the software that screens
applicants, be sued as an employer's "agent" for discrimination, a genuinely new
turn, while the discrimination itself stays an unproven allegation still in the
pleadings. The piece stands on four claims.

1. **The ruling establishes procedure, not a finding.** The July 2024 order
   decided only that Mobley pleaded a plausible claim; it accepted his
   allegations as true at the 12(b)(6) stage and found nothing about whether
   Workday's tools discriminate. I pushed on the one place this could slip: the
   display text. The headline states only that a court let Workday be sued as an
   "agent" (the court's own term, Doc 80). The dek marks the discrimination as
   Mobley's claim ("says ... rejected him") and states the judge ruled on who can
   be sued, "not on whether it happened." Every timeline heading is procedural
   ("The agent theory survives," "Still in the pleadings"). The one verbatim
   holding sits in a note attributed to Judge Lin, and the quote itself says
   Workday's tools are "alleged to perform" the function. Nothing states or
   implies a discrimination finding. The gate holds.

2. **Disparate impact is what Mobley alleges, and the law forbids it without
   intent.** Checked the teaching against Griggs and the EEOC technical
   assistance. The plain definition (a neutral-looking screen that selects a
   protected group at a lower rate), the "no intent required" point, and the
   four-fifths worked example (48/80 = 60%, 12/40 = 30%, ratio 50% under the 80%
   line) all match the owning primaries. The table caption correctly calls the
   four-fifths rule a rule of thumb, not a legal boundary, as the EEOC document
   itself insists.

3. **The scale claim rests on Workday's own case-owned figure.** The closing turn
   anchors "nearly everywhere" on the 1.1 billion applications Workday told the
   court were rejected using its software (Doc 128), and the "hundreds of
   millions" collective ceiling is Workday's own estimate. The SHRM 64% figure is
   absent, correctly: its denominator is unverified in the record, and the
   particular instruction here forbids adding an unverified number. No arithmetic
   or denominator problem.

4. **The counter-material is carried, not defeated.** Workday's live defenses
   ("does not recommend, screen out, or otherwise assess..."; employers can toggle
   the AI; any disparity may be employer-driven) are stated as still standing and
   deferred to the merits, with the court's note that Workday's own website and
   discovery responses sit awkwardly against the "we don't recommend" claim. The
   agent theory is flagged as one trial judge's ruling, untested on appeal, that
   had to set aside an older rule (the Miller v. Maxwell's line) that individual
   agents are not liable. The ADEA-covers-applicants reading is flagged as
   contested, with two other appeals courts rejecting it. Each traces to its cited
   order.

Rita F. Lin, Derek Mobley, the EEOC, Workday), every date and docket step (2023

## Cut

The prose is already tight; the slop pass turned up nothing to delete. I ran the
placeholder test on every edge sentence and on each candidate that sounds like a
paragraph's best line.

The takeaway opens on a negative-parallelism construction ("The news ... is not
that a hiring algorithm was caught discriminating"). It survives the rule because
the misconception it corrects is real, named, and the whole article's subject: a
reader meeting this case would reasonably assume the news is a discrimination
finding, and the piece exists to separate that from what the ruling actually did.
That is the earned contrast the rule preserves, not the invented strawman it cuts.

"That ruling was one step in a longer case" leads into the timeline. It is a thin
line, but it orients the reader to the dated furniture that follows rather than
grading the article, and cutting it drops the timeline in without a turn from the
EEOC paragraph before it. Kept as a functional lead-in.

Checked the draft's distinctive phrasing against the voice guide's quoted
passages (Armstrong and Miller, Folkenflik, Totenberg). The article borrows their
moves, not their words: it orders events before weighing them, marks Mobley's
claim as a claim instead of leaning on "allegedly," and closes on what is not yet
settled. No lifted clause. No prompt leakage: the pervasiveness framing states a
reported fact about the reader's world, not a planning instruction, and the
bookends' direct address is the one allowance the lesson template grants.

Against the recent-pattern notes: the headline avoids both flagged molds (no
"<Company> never <did diligence>," no "<Actor> let an AI <do X> under cover") and
puts the real surprise, the vendor on the hook, in the opening words. The dek is
two plain sentences, none of the three banned dek molds, and carries no
"settled for $X without admitting" number. Furniture is chosen, not reflexive: the
timeline earns its place across five real procedural steps, and the single note
carries the verbatim holding for deliberate emphasis. Section headings are the
piece's own nouns and reconstruct the argument in order; none is scaffolding, none
repeats the comma-and-"and" mold. Punctuation is clean: no em-dashes or semicolons
in the body. No sentences failed the test.

## Reader

Read straight through as the paper's declared reader, I come away able to hold two
things apart that no single source hands me together: "the maker of the screen can
be sued as the employer's agent" (established, and new) versus "the maker was found
to discriminate" (unproven, still litigated). The article earns that by recasting
Mobley's zero-percent-across-100-plus-employers allegation as the reason the suit
points at the one thing those employers shared, Workday's screen, and using that
hinge to keep the procedural holding distinct from the discrimination the rulings
only assumed true. The original-work sentence in the handoff claims exactly this,
and it survives: the evidence record states the procedural line and the
cross-employer causation theory in separate places, and the article makes them one
argument. The prose sits with the voice-guide exemplars, not a median summary. The
headline, read last as the largest claim, states only established procedural fact
with its actors named.
