# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/workday-hiring-screening

## How this piece should sound

The discrimination in Mobley v. Workday is not proven; the agent-liability
ruling is. The piece has to hold both at once, and the exemplars below show
three ways to do it without hedging so much the piece goes limp.

Take the ordering from Armstrong and Miller: state what Mobley says happened,
then what the record actually shows happened next, in the sequence it
happened, before any of it gets weighed. The lesson's opening should tell what
the screening system was built to do, what Mobley says it did to him, and what
the court has and hasn't ruled, in that order, the way their dek states one
fact and then its reversal before asking the reader to judge either. Where the
lesson needs to report what Workday's software allegedly did to a specific
applicant, attribute the specific claim to the complaint the way Armstrong and
Miller put the detective's wrong conclusion inside his own quotation marks
rather than in their own voice — the claim stays a claim because it is marked
as someone's claim, not because the piece keeps saying "allegedly."

Folkenflik's paired construction — Fox accused, Dominion accused, "the
competing claims have not yet been addressed by the court" — is a model for
the discrimination itself: Mobley says the pattern in his 100-plus rejections
traces to race, age, and disability; Workday disputes it; no factfinder has
weighed either side yet. State both, then state plainly that neither has been
tested at trial. Use his other move for the legal theory itself: "the heart of
their claim" names what a plaintiff has to show without granting that they've
shown it. The lesson can use the same construction to explain what it takes to
make a software vendor count as an employer's "agent" — what Mobley's suit
has to establish, not what it has established — so the reader understands the
mechanism before learning whether it held.

Totenberg's paragraph on the Purdue ruling sorts a dry bankruptcy fight into
two camps before a single dollar figure lands, so every number that follows
already has somewhere to go. The "agent" ruling wants the same treatment: tell
the reader what the old rule was (only the employer answers for its hiring
decisions) and what this ruling lets a plaintiff argue instead (the vendor
that built the screen can answer too), before naming Workday, the collective
action, or any figure from the case. That ordering is what makes a
motion-to-dismiss ruling read as a real turn rather than a procedural footnote.
Disparate impact gets the same short, plain definition before it does any
work in the argument — a neutral-looking screen that still filters out a
protected group at a higher rate — stated once, in one sentence, the way
Totenberg gives the bankruptcy mechanism one clean sentence before the oral
argument details pile on.

Close the way Totenberg closes hers: on what is not yet settled, stated as a
fact about the state of the law, not a forecast dressed up as one. The
lesson's own turn to where this weakness lives today — the screening most
applicants now pass through — should say plainly what current law does and
does not resolve for other vendors, the way her last line says plainly that
the vote wasn't yet knowable, rather than reaching for a warning the piece
hasn't earned.

One direction the exemplars can't supply: the lesson template's two bookends
address the reader directly and say what the piece will teach and what it
taught. Nothing shown above does that, because none of these writers work in
that form — keep the bookends' directness there and nowhere else in the piece,
the way the template asks.

## Ken Armstrong and T. Christian Miller, "An Unbelievable Story of Rape"

Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/false-rape-accusations-an-unbelievable-story

> "An 18-year-old said she was attacked at knifepoint. Then she said she made
> it up. That's where our story begins."

This is the piece's dek, and it states two facts in strict sequence — the
claim, then its reversal — without telling the reader which one to believe.
Nothing here is the writers' voice; it is only what happened, in order. The
piece has committed to narrating events before judging them, in its first
sentence.

> "'Based on her answers and body language it was apparent that [Marie] was
> lying about the rape,' Rittgarn later wrote."

The writers never say Marie was lying. They report that a detective wrote
that conclusion, in his own words, and the whole piece is spent showing the
conclusion was wrong. Putting his judgment inside his own quotation marks,
rather than folding it into the narration, keeps the writers' voice out of a
verdict that belonged to someone else and later collapsed.

> "There are no firm statistics on how often police arrest women for making
> false rape reports, nor on how often prosecutors take such cases to court.
> Nobody collects such data. But leading law enforcement organizations urge
> caution in filing such charges. The International Association of Chiefs of
> Police and the FBI stress the need for a thorough investigation before
> discounting a report of rape. Cops must work as hard to prove a falsehood as
> they do to prove a truth."

The narrative stops here to teach a general mechanism — what departments are
supposed to do with a rape report, named organizations and all — before
returning to the story armed with the standard the detectives broke. Where the
number doesn't exist, the sentence says so directly ("Nobody collects such
data") instead of estimating one.

## David Folkenflik, "Rupert Murdoch's turn to face questions in $1.6 billion lawsuit against Fox News"

Source: https://www.npr.org/2022/12/09/1141688083/fox-news-rupert-murdoch-lawsuit-dominion

> "The last time Rupert Murdoch testified about a scandal involving his
> company, it played out in public. He sought to convey contrition about the
> monstrous activities that had come to light inside one of his newsrooms
> while skirting any responsibility for it."

Folkenflik opens on a past scandal that was already settled, not the pending
one, which lets him state a hard judgment plainly — "skirting any
responsibility" — on ground where nothing is still being litigated. The piece
earns its bluntness by spending it where the record already supports it.

> "Dominion's attorneys have accused Fox of destroying evidence from some of
> its most important figures: Fox News Media CEO Suzanne Scott, top stars Sean
> Hannity and Laura Ingraham, along with other stars, network executives and
> sources, presumably related to the claims of election fraud... Similarly,
> Fox has accused senior Dominion executives, including CEO John Poulos, of
> destroying private electronic messages relevant to the case. The competing
> claims have not yet been addressed by the court. Neither side would comment
> publicly on the allegations."

Both sides get the identical construction: accused, then the specifics, then
what hasn't happened yet. The symmetry is the device. Naming that the court
hasn't ruled, immediately after describing what each side wants it to find,
keeps every claim in the paragraph a claim.

> "Dominion lawyers want to show that the Murdochs had every reason to know
> they should prevent their stars from promoting such false claims of fraud
> and yet did not do so — the heart of their claim that the alleged defamation
> was orchestrated from the top."

The legal theory Dominion needs to win on gets explained entirely through what
the lawyers "want to show," never asserted as fact. "The heart of their
claim" names the mechanism a plaintiff has to prove without granting that
they've proven it.

## Nina Totenberg, "Supreme Court hears Purdue Pharma bankruptcy case that shields Sackler family"

Source: https://www.npr.org/2023/12/04/1217069753/purdue-sacklers-oxycontin-supreme-court

> "Basically, the issue before the court amounts to a battle between money and
> principle. On the money side is a bankruptcy deal approved by two lower
> courts that would provide $8 billion to state and local governments in
> dealing with the consequences of opioid addiction, as well as providing
> individual compensation to victims. Funding most of that settlement would be
> the Sackler family, who owned and ran Purdue Pharma, and agreed to pay $6
> billion into the compensation pot."

A dry bankruptcy dispute gets sorted into two camps before a single dollar
figure appears, so the reader has somewhere to put each fact as it arrives.
The figures come after the frame is built, not instead of it.

> "On the principle side are a relatively small number of victims, and the
> U.S. Trustee, who oversees bankruptcies. They object to the deal because it
> shields the Sacklers from any further lawsuits, and leaves the family with
> more than half their wealth, even though they were intimately involved in
> the aggressive and false marketing of OxyContin."

The objectors' reasoning is reported as their reasoning, in one unadorned
sentence, while the marketing history behind it is stated as settled fact —
because by this point in the case, it was. Totenberg doesn't say which side
has the better argument; the shape of the sentence does that work instead.

> "At the end of the day, it was unclear where the majority of the court is
> going, and whether the bankruptcy plan will survive."

The piece ends on what the justices' questions did not resolve, not on a
prediction dressed up as one. After several paragraphs of oral argument, the
plain, checkable fact is that the vote wasn't yet knowable, and the piece says
exactly that.
