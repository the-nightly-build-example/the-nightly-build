# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/houston-teacher-evaluation

## How this piece should sound

This is a lesson in the When AI Breaks course, for a reader who is quick and
widely read but has never been shown how a value-added model turns a classroom
of test scores into a single number for a teacher. Everything the field takes
for granted gets built up in plain words, and nothing is talked down to. The
register is the paper's: plain claims, concrete stakes, no fuss. When a sentence
could sound impressive or be understood, choose understood.

The lesson reconstructs a real institutional and legal failure from the record,
and its job is to explain a mechanism. The subject carries its own force, so the
prose can stay flat and let it. Gawande's verdict
paragraph on McAllen's quality shows the move: when you rule out an explanation,
do it by naming the yardstick and giving the figure, and mark the edge of what
the evidence supports the way he does with "so far as one can tell." A number
the reader cannot scale on their own wants a comparison beside it, as his
McAllen figure sits against El Paso.

Teach the structure before you judge it. Miller and Armstrong lay out how a
process is supposed to work, who decides and at which step, before they show it
failing, and the failure then reads as a failure of that structure. A
value-added score, the appeal a teacher would bring against it, the proprietary
model behind it: each is a step in a machine, and the reader needs the step
named plainly, in their own words, before the lesson can show what went wrong
there. Where a term of art first appears, define it in the sentence it arrives
in, as Fink defines triage, and separate it from the near thing it is easily
confused with.

The validity of these models is contested. The vendor defends them; the
teachers and the researchers pressed other points. When you carry a disputed
claim, attribute it to who said it and set the other side's strongest version
right beside it, the way Fink places a denial in the same breath as the account
it answers. Her triage paragraph is the model for the uncertainty at the center
of this lesson: where the experts genuinely disagree, or a prediction is
inexact, say so plainly instead of smoothing it into a cleaner story. Say
plainly, too, what the record does not settle.

Build any finding in the open, so the reader can see how it is known. Fink names
the documents her count rests on and states the number as a floor with a hedge;
a claim the argument leans on can show its source and its reach the same way.
When a plain mechanism needs to stick, Gawande's line about the doctor's pen
shows the kind of concrete image that does real work: it names where the thing
actually happens, and the sentences around it explain rather than decorate.
Reach for an image like that only when the mechanism is genuinely carried by it,
not to close a section on a quotable line.

Let a source's own plain words carry a point when they can, with flat
attribution, as Gawande does with the surgeon at the dinner table. The people in
this story, a teacher, an administrator defending the system, a judge, can often
say a thing more sharply than a paraphrase would, and the lesson can report it
rather than improve on it.

## Atul Gawande, "The Cost Conundrum"

Source: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2009/06/01/the-cost-conundrum

> "Nor does the care given in McAllen stand out for its quality. Medicare ranks hospitals on twenty-five metrics of care. On all but two of these, McAllen's five largest hospitals performed worse, on average, than El Paso's. McAllen costs Medicare seven thousand dollars more per person each year than does the average city in America. But not, so far as one can tell, because it's delivering better health care."

The paragraph rules out an explanation by counting. It names the yardstick, the
twenty-five Medicare metrics, gives the score against a second city, and states
the dollar figure, and only then reaches its judgment. Gawande's caution is on
the page in "so far as one can tell": he commits to what the data support and
marks the edge of it rather than rounding up to a stronger claim.

> "'Come on,' the general surgeon finally said. 'We all know these arguments are bullshit. There is overutilization here, pure and simple.' Doctors, he said, were racking up charges with extra tests, services, and procedures."

Gawande lets the surgeon say the thing in his own blunt words, then translates
it into plain terms in the next sentence. The attribution stays flat, "the
general surgeon said," "he said," so the source carries the claim and the writer
does not dress it up. You can hear a particular person at the table, impatient
with the polite explanations the other doctors were offering.

> "Health-care costs ultimately arise from the accumulation of individual decisions doctors make about which services and treatments to write an order for. The most expensive piece of medical equipment, as the saying goes, is a doctor's pen. And, as a rule, hospital executives don't own the pen caps. Doctors do."

A mechanism stated in one plain sentence, then fixed in the mind by a concrete
image that carries real content: the cost lives in who signs the order. The
image is not decoration, because the sentences after it, about who owns the pen,
do the explaining. Gawande the practicing surgeon is visible in the choice of
the pen as the object that spends the money.

## T. Christian Miller and Ken Armstrong, "An Unbelievable Story of Rape"

Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/false-rape-accusations-an-unbelievable-story

> "In that way, rape cases were unlike most other crimes. The credibility of the victim was often on trial as much as the guilt of the accused. And on the long, fraught trail between crime and conviction, the first triers of fact were the cops. An investigating officer had to figure out if the victim was telling the truth."

Four plain sentences explain how the process actually works before any judgment
about how it went wrong. "The first triers of fact were the cops" names a real
step in the machinery in the reader's own words. The writers teach the structure
first, which is what later lets a wrong result register as a failure of that
structure rather than of one person.

> "The fear of false rape accusations has a long history in the legal system. In the 1600s, England's chief justice, Matthew Hale, warned that rape 'is an accusation easily to be made and hard to be proved, and harder to be defended by the party accused.' Judges in the U.S. read the so-called Hale warning to juries until the 1980s. But most recent research suggests that false reporting is relatively rare. FBI figures show that police annually declare around 5 percent of rape cases unfounded, or baseless. Social scientists examining police records in detail and using methodologically rigorous standards cite similar, single-digit rates."

The passage states the old belief fairly, in its strongest words through the Hale
quotation, and then sets the evidence against it with a figure and a source. It
corrects a real, named belief rather than a strawman, and the correction rests
on the numbers, not on the writers' insistence. This is how the piece stays
even-handed on a contested point and still reaches a conclusion.

> "For Mason, his decision to file the citation required no complicated calculus. He was certain Marie had lied. The police had spent a lot of resources chasing that lie. The law said her lie was a crime. Really, it was as simple as that."

The institutional logic is laid out in short flat sentences, from inside the
detective's own view, with no adjective telling the reader how to feel. The
plainness is what indicts: the reader watches a chain of defensible-sounding
steps produce a wrong result. "Really, it was as simple as that" reports how
simple it looked to him, and the reader supplies the rest.

## Sheri Fink, "The Deadly Choices at Memorial"

Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/the-deadly-choices-at-memorial-826

> "Pou and her co-workers were performing triage, a word once used by the French in reference to the sorting of coffee beans and applied to the battlefield by Napoleon's chief surgeon, Baron Dominique-Jean Larrey. Today triage is used in accidents and disasters when the number of injured exceeds available resources. Surprisingly, perhaps, there is no consensus on how best to do this. Typically, medical workers try to divvy up care to achieve the greatest good for the greatest number of people. There is an ongoing debate about how to do this and what the 'greatest good' means. Is it the number of lives saved? Years of life saved? Best 'quality' years of life saved? Or something else?"

Fink defines the term of art in plain words, gives its origin in one concrete
line, and then says plainly that the experts do not agree on how to do it. The
closing questions are the field's real open questions, not rhetorical ones. She
teaches the reader enough to judge the decisions the story turns on, and she
does not hide the genuine uncertainty to make the account tidier.

> "It is now evident that more medical professionals were involved in the decision to inject patients — and far more patients were injected — than was previously understood. When the names on toxicology reports and autopsies are matched with recollections and documentation from the days after Katrina, it appears that at least 17 patients were injected with morphine or the sedative midazolam, or both, after a long-awaited rescue effort was at last emptying the hospital."

The finding is built in the open. Fink names the records it rests on, the
toxicology reports and autopsies, shows the step that produced it, matching
names across sources, and states the number as a floor with a hedge, "it appears
that at least 17." The reader can see how she knows it and how far she is willing
to go with it.

> "Mendez said she watched Pou struggle with what she was saying, telling investigators that Pou told her that 'the decision had been made to administer lethal doses' of morphine and other drugs. (Pou, through her lawyer, Richard Simmons Jr., denied mentioning 'lethal doses.') Were the LifeCare patients being singled out? Mendez asked. She knew there were other sick patients at Memorial. Mendez recalled that Pou said 'no' and that there was 'no telling how far' it would go."

A disputed account is attributed to the person who gave it and to where she gave
it, to investigators, and the denial sits right beside it in the same breath.
Fink does not resolve the conflict for the reader or quietly drop the side she
finds less convincing. That care with attribution is what lets a reader trust
the parts of the account that are not in dispute.
