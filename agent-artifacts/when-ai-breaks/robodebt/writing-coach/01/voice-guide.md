# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/robodebt

## How this piece should sound

This lesson tells the Robodebt scheme in order and then teaches why averaging a
year of tax-office income across fortnights, calling the result a debt, and
making the recipient disprove it fails at scale. The reader is smart and new to
it. So the register is plain: short declaratives a careful reader takes in on the
first pass, the way Patrick McKenzie explains why a bank behaves the way it does.
Income averaging and the reversed burden of proof are unfamiliar mechanics, and
the sentences that carry them should stay simple enough that the reader is never
doing two hard things at once. McKenzie's four-party sentence about who failed
the customer shows that a plain sentence can still have a point of view.

The mechanism is the transferable part, and it can be stated without deciding in
advance how loud to be. McKenzie names a failure and its scale in one breath:
extremely uncommon against all banking activity, happening every day, and falling
hardest on a few. The section on income averaging can do the same for how the
fortnightly figure is manufactured and how far the scheme reached, holding the
scale honestly rather than reaching for the alarming number or the reassuring
one.

The desk tells the incident in order, and Useem's opening shows the move: begin
inside a concrete scene, a date and the people deciding and the thing they built,
and let it mean something before it is explained. Where the Robodebt story turns
on a specific moment, a decision, a letter demanding repayment, a court date, it
can be shown at that grain instead of summarized. Useem also builds an
institution's character from particulars, patents and wings and a CFO's actual
words, not from adjectives; the calculation the scheme ran can be drawn the same
way, in its own arithmetic.

The documented harm is grave and the record supports it, which is exactly why it
asks for restraint. Davies states that care homes hold responsibility for the
most vulnerable people and that no one will own an urgent failure, plainly, at an
even volume. McKenzie ends on "Many of them will have done nothing wrong" and
stops. Where Robodebt's harm is on the record, naming who was hurt and what the
Royal Commission and the court establish will carry further than intensifying
language would, and it keeps the writer clear of overreach on contested harms.

On the disputed points, what was known about the scheme's lawfulness and when,
the attribution of specific harms, Davies models the fair-minded stance: he
starts from respect for the problem, granting that a failed arrangement once
looked like a solution, and asks why before he judges. That disposition keeps the
contested attributions labeled and the strongest version of each account intact.

## Patrick McKenzie (patio11), "Seeing like a Bank"

Source: https://www.bitsaboutmoney.com/archive/seeing-like-a-bank/

> "Banks are extremely good at tracking one kind of truth, ledgers. They are extremely bad at tracking certain other forms of truth, for structural reasons. In pathological cases, which are extremely uncommon relative to all banking activity but which nonetheless happen every day and which will impact some people extremely disproportionately, the bank will appear to lack object permanence."

The passage states a mechanism in flat declaratives and then bounds its scale in
the same sentence. McKenzie is visible in the refusal to round any of it off: he
holds "extremely uncommon", "happen every day", and "impact some people extremely
disproportionately" together instead of choosing the scary quantity or the
reassuring one. That is how a rare-but-real failure gets described accurately.

> "And so the customer will feel frustrated and they have been lied to, Tier 2 certainly doesn't feel like they've lied to anyone (they read the script, it's a Tuesday), Ops feels like the world is on fire because the world is always on fire, and senior bank management cannot detect this problem because no metric available to them is capable of disaggregating it from the complex monster that is the financial system."

One long sentence walks a single failure through four parties and gives each its
own true perspective, so no one is made the villain and the failure still
happened. McKenzie's plainness is in "they read the script, it's a Tuesday": he
explains why a person did nothing wrong and the harm landed anyway, without either
excusing or accusing.

> "Every future with 12 CFR § 21.11(k)(1) in it will include many Americans whose bank accounts are closed for no reason that can be disclosed to them. Many of them will have done nothing wrong."

Two short sentences name a real cost of a rule and stop. He says exactly who is
hurt, exactly what they are denied, and then the fact that matters most, that many
did nothing wrong. The restraint does the work; he does not raise his voice to make
the cost register.

## Dan Davies, "Taming the unaccountability machine"

Source: https://hypertext.niskanencenter.org/p/taming-the-unaccountability-machine

> "In pubs and golf clubs up and down the North Wales coast in the early 1990s, that was the joke made about the entrepreneurial practice of buying up an old hotel, converting it into a retirement home, and then filling it up all year round with widows from Liverpool and Manchester who treasured memories of childhood holidays in Rhyl or Prestatyn. Reforms to the social care system had given local authorities an obligation to support service provision through the "independent" sector, so it looked like free money."

Davies opens a systemic argument inside a concrete local scene, named towns and
real people, before any theory arrives. The dryness is his: "so it looked like free
money" delivers the incentive and the coming trouble in six words, with no
editorializing around them.

> "Care homes ought to be among the most closely supervised and regulated entities in society, as they are intimately responsible for some of the most vulnerable people. But in fact, oversight seems to be all but impossible. Not only are the authorities generally quite bad at proactively detecting mismanagement and dangerous practices, they are often surprisingly bad at responding to complaints when they are made. The business of trying to find anyone who is prepared to admit to being responsible for doing anything about an urgent, even life-threatening issue is confusing and frustrating, as many people with elderly relatives know."

The stakes here are grave, and Davies states them at an even volume. He names who
is at risk and grounds the abstraction in something the reader can check, "as many
people with elderly relatives know". He reports that no one will own a
life-threatening failure without reaching for outrage to do it.

> "But it is usually best to start with an attitude of respect for the problem. In large and complicated systems, something which presents as a failure today is very likely to have looked like a solution when it was first established."

Davies refuses the easy move of treating a failed system as self-evidently stupid.
The fairness is visible in the instruction he sets himself, "respect for the
problem", which fixes his analytic tone before he reaches any judgment. It is what
lets the piece explain a bad outcome without contempt for the people who built it.

## Jerry Useem, "The Long-Forgotten Flight That Sent Boeing Off Course"

Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/archive/2019/11/how-boeing-lost-its-bearings/602188/

> "The flight that put the Boeing Company on course for disaster lifted off a few hours after sunrise. It was good flying weather—temperatures in the mid-40s with a slight breeze out of the southeast—but oddly, no one knew where the 737 jetliner was headed. The crew had prepared three flight plans: one to Denver. One to Dallas. And one to Chicago."

Useem opens on a specific morning, the weather, three flight plans, a plane whose
destination is a secret, and lets the oddness of the scene carry meaning before he
explains it. The detail is exact and unforced. The writer is present in the choice
to show a corporate stunt and let it speak, not to announce that a company had lost its way.

> "For about 80 years, Boeing basically functioned as an association of engineers. Its executives held patents, designed wings, spoke the language of engineering and safety as a mother tongue. Finance wasn't a primary language. Even Boeing's bean counters didn't act the part. As late as the mid-'90s, the company's chief financial officer had minimal contact with Wall Street and answered colleagues' requests for basic financial data with a curt "Tell them not to worry.""

A company's character is established through particulars, patents held, wings
designed, a CFO who waves off a data request, rather than through adjectives. Useem
trusts the quoted detail to do the work, which is why the later change reads as a
real loss instead of a claim he is asserting.

> "The shift had started three years earlier, with Boeing's "reverse takeover" of McDonnell Douglas—so-called because it was McDonnell executives who perversely ended up in charge of the combined entity, and it was McDonnell's culture that became ascendant. "McDonnell Douglas bought Boeing with Boeing's money," went the joke around Seattle."

Useem explains who actually took control after a merger in one plain sentence, then
hands the point to a joke insiders told: "McDonnell Douglas bought Boeing with
Boeing's money." The wit is not his own flourish. It belongs to Seattle and rests
on the two companies' names, which is why it informs the reader instead of
decorating the paragraph.
