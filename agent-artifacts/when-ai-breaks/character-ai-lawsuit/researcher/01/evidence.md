# Evidence record: when-ai-breaks/character-ai-lawsuit (01)

The primaries carry the spine of the commission cleanly. The filed complaint
(Doc. 1) owns every allegation about Sewell Setzer III, the product, and the
founders; the court's order on the motions to dismiss (Doc. 115) owns what a
judge has actually decided; Character.AI's four dated blog posts own the
operator's announced changes; and the FTC's press release and model 6(b) order
own the regulatory action. The record is strong on chronology, parties, dates,
and the exact legal posture. It is deliberately thin in one place by design: the
care rules bar recording the method of death and the final messages, so this
record omits both and the article should teach the product failure without them.
The one place the commission's framing needs discipline is the May 2025 ruling.
It is a motion-to-dismiss order, not a finding: the court took the complaint's
facts as true and, on the First Amendment, declined to hold that the chatbot's
output is protected speech "at this stage" rather than holding it is not. A
later development the commission's "status as of the article date" requires:
Google and Character.AI agreed to settle Garcia and four related suits in
January 2026, on undisclosed terms and with no admission of wrongdoing. The
evidence supports the commissioned angle; it does not undermine it, but it does
bound how hard the ruling can be pressed.

Alleged-vs-confirmed is labeled throughout. Unless a line says a court or a
company confirmed something, every factual claim sourced to the complaint is an
allegation the defendants have not admitted.

## Contradictions

- **Co-founder's name spelling.** The complaint caption and the court order caption both read "Daniel De Frietas" (and the complaint caption adds "Adiwarsana"), but the body of each document spells it "Daniel De Freitas," and Tech Justice Law's case page gives the full "Daniel de Freitas Adiwarsana." The correct spelling is **Daniel De Freitas** (co-founder, with Noam Shazeer). Do not carry the caption's "Frietas" into the article; it is a filing typo repeated by the court.
- **Death-date typo in the order.** The court order's narrative says "On February 28, 2025 Sewell located his confiscated phone," which is a typo. The complaint, which owns the fact, states the death was **February 28, 2024** (¶11; DOB/death heading). Use the complaint's date.
- **What the ruling actually held on speech.** Widely repeated secondary framing says the judge "ruled that AI chatbot output is not protected speech" or that "chatbots have no First Amendment rights." The order is narrower: it declined to decide, holding the court "is not prepared to hold that Character A.I.'s output is speech" at the motion-to-dismiss stage (p.31). Also, a footnote separately notes the chatbot itself is not a "person" with Bill-of-Rights standing, but the operative holding on the output is the "not prepared to hold" one. This is the commission's "do not overstate a filing as a finding" risk; keep it exact.
- **Allegation vs. finding on Google.** Google's public position is that it and Character.AI are "entirely separate." The court did not contradict this; it held only that plaintiff sufficiently ALLEGED (plausibly pleaded) Google's liability as a component-part manufacturer and aider-abettor. Nothing in the record confirms those allegations as true. Two reported wordings of Google's statement exist (Reuters "entirely separate"; AP "completely separate, unrelated companies"); attribute as reported.
- **Confounders in the complaint's own facts.** The complaint states Sewell's therapist, unaware of his Character.AI use, attributed his decline to social media, and that he was diagnosed with anxiety and disruptive mood disorder. These are in the pleading and complicate any single-cause story; the order recites them too. They do not defeat the product-design theory (the court found a plausible duty and defect) but the article should not present causation as adjudicated.
- **Posture is unsettled, then mooted.** The order left the founders' personal jurisdiction open (refileable after discovery) and dismissed IIED. The January 2026 settlement then resolved the case before any trial or merits finding — so there is no judicial finding that the product caused the death. "Allowed to proceed" is the ceiling the record supports, plus a confidential settlement with no admission of wrongdoing.
