# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/character-ai-lawsuit

## How this piece should sound

This lesson reports a fourteen-year-old's death and the wrongful-death suit that
followed. The register that fits it is sober and exact. The facts of a companion
chatbot's design, a pattern of use, and a mother's complaint are heavy enough to
carry the piece on their own, so the writing does not press them. Sheri Fink's
Memorial reporting is the closest model: she states what happened in dated,
plain sentences and lets the reader feel the weight without being told to.

Calibrate certainty the way Fink does in the passage on who was injected. A
complaint alleges; a court rules; a company confirms or denies. Each of those is
a different kind of claim, and the piece can mark which is which in the sentence
that carries it, using her range from "it is now evident" to "it appears" to
"almost certainly, according to." When the strongest available source for a fact
about the chatbot's behavior is the filed complaint, the sentence can say so and
still report the fact.

Report the legal chronology as Amy Howe reports a ruling: say exactly what the
court did and, just as plainly, what it did not do. Her Howell sentence gives the
holding and its limit in one breath, that state courts "simply lack the
authority" to do a thing "even if it means" a specific person loses out. The May
2025 ruling let the case go past a motion to dismiss; that is what it did, and
the piece can state it at that size rather than as a verdict on the merits. The
October 2024 filing is a filing.

When many hands contributed to the failure, name them in order the way
Langewiesche does with the SabreTech chain, one plain clause per actor, and let
the sequence do the explaining rather than a single villain. The design incentive,
the missing guardrail, the company's later changes, the regulator's orders: each
is a step, and the reader who follows the steps understands the cause.

A hard judgment about the company's product or its response can be stated flatly.
Langewiesche sets a plain fact against an official's reassurance and lets the gap
show. If the record supports a judgment about what the design optimized for or
what the operator did after February 2024, it can be delivered in a level
sentence, without the indignation doing the work the evidence should do.

This is a lesson, so a clean distinction can teach. Langewiesche opens by sorting
accidents into three named kinds in plain words before he uses any of them. Where
this piece needs the reader to hold a distinction, between a scripted helpline
and a companion optimized for immersion, or between what is alleged and what is
found, it can lay the distinction out plainly first and then use it.

Define each term the smart general reader does not already hold, in the sentence
where it first appears, as all three writers define their statutes and their
procedures on first use. Keep the concrete detail; the exact figure, the exact
date, the exact wording of a policy change is what makes a sentence about this
incident and no other.

## William Langewiesche, "The Lessons of ValuJet 592"

Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/1998/03/the-lessons-of-valujet-592/306534/

> "Consider, for simplicity, that there are three kinds of airplane accidents. The most common ones might be called "procedural." They are those old-fashioned accidents that result from single obvious mistakes, that can immediately be understood in simple terms, and that have simple resolutions."

Langewiesche teaches by sorting. He names a category, defines it in plain words,
and only then builds on it. The tone is unhurried and unshowy, and the reader is
handed a tool they can use for the rest of the piece. This is a technical writer
who trusts a plain declarative to do the teaching.

> "We will never know everyone at fault in this story. ValuJet gave the order to replace oxygen generators on the MD-80s, most of which had come to the end of their licensed lifetimes. It provided SabreTech with explicit removal procedures and general warnings about the dangers of fire."

Here he reports a failure that had many contributors. He does not reach for one
villain; he lists each actor and what they did, one plain clause at a time, and
the causal chain carries the weight. The first sentence admits the limit of what
can be known and refuses the tidy assignment of blame, which is the honesty of a
reporter who has read the record and will not go past it.

> "The day after the ValuJet accident he had flown to Miami and made the incredible assertion that ValuJet was a safe airline—when for 110 people lying dead in a nearby swamp it very obviously was not. He also said, "I would fly on it," as if he believed that he had to reassure a nation of children. It was an insulting performance, and it was taken as evidence of the FAA's isolation and of its betrayal of the public's trust."

This is a hard judgment of an official's response, and Langewiesche lands it by
setting the claim against a plain fact, the count of the dead, and letting the
gap between them show. The verdict is stated flatly rather than shouted. A person
with a clear view of the record is visible here, judging without pleading.

## Sheri Fink, "The Deadly Choices at Memorial"

Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/the-deadly-choices-at-memorial-826

> "Investigators pored over the evidence, and in July 2006, nearly a year after Katrina, Louisiana Department of Justice agents arrested the doctor and the nurses in connection with the deaths of four patients. The physician, Anna Pou, defended herself on national television, saying her role was to "help" patients "through their pain," a position she maintains today. After a New Orleans grand jury declined to indict her on second-degree murder charges, the case faded from view."

Fink reports a legal chronology as a sequence of dated events, and she attributes
the doctor's account to the doctor without endorsing or dismissing it. The quoted
words are the subject's own defense, marked as such. This is restraint under
control: a story about a possible homicide told in level sentences, where the
reporter's care with attribution is itself the signal of how seriously she takes
it.

> "It is now evident that more medical professionals were involved in the decision to inject patients — and far more patients were injected — than was previously understood. When the names on toxicology reports and autopsies are matched with recollections and documentation from the days after Katrina, it appears that at least 17 patients were injected with morphine or the sedative midazolam, or both, after a long-awaited rescue effort was at last emptying the hospital. A number of these patients were extremely ill and might not have survived the evacuation. Several were almost certainly not near death when they were injected, according to medical professionals who treated them at Memorial and an internist's review of their charts and autopsies that was commissioned by investigators but never made public."

Every claim here is graded for how sure it is. "It is now evident," "it appears,"
"might not have survived," "almost certainly, according to": Fink moves through a
scale of certainty and tells the reader where on it each fact sits, and she names
the source of the hardest claim. This is how a careful reporter separates what
the evidence shows from what it suggests, in the middle of a grave story, without
ever flattening the two together.

> "On July 24, 2007, the jurors filed into Section E of Orleans Parish Criminal District Court, the building where Minyard survived Katrina. Judge Calvin Johnson read aloud the 10 counts of indictment. The grand jury did not indict Pou on any of them."

The outcome of the case gets three plain sentences and no editorial. The date,
the room, the counts, the result: Fink reports the legal turning point at exactly
its own size and stops. A reader learns what happened and is left to weigh it,
which is the discipline of a writer who knows the facts do not need her help.

## Amy Howe, "Opinion analysis: Unanimous court rules for veteran in family law case"

Source: https://www.scotusblog.com/2017/05/opinion-analysis-unanimous-court-rules-veteran-family-law-case/

> "Today's opinion in Howell v. Howell exemplifies the bread and butter of the Supreme Court's docket: It is a decision with significant real-world consequences that will nonetheless largely fly under the radar. The court unanimously handed a victory to Air Force veteran John Howell, who was divorced from his former wife, Sandra, over 25 years ago."

Howe opens by saying plainly what the decision is and how much it matters, and she
puts the actual holding, who won, in the second sentence. She writes for a reader
who is not a lawyer and defines the case in ordinary words. The precision is in the
sizing: she tells you this is consequential and quiet at once, and neither claim
is inflated.

> "The justices agreed with John, explaining that under federal law state courts simply lack the authority to divide up John's disability benefits, even if it means that Sandra winds up receiving less money than she might have originally expected. And the state can't get around the restrictions imposed by federal law by characterizing the award to Sandra as an order to John to "reimburse" her for the money that she no longer receives."

She states the holding and its limit in the same sentence, what the court decided
and the exact consequence it accepted. The reasoning is attributed to the justices,
not asserted by the writer. This is a legal explainer who has read the opinion and
reports what it holds and how far it reaches, with nothing added.

> "Breyer acknowledged that the federal statute and today's ruling could make things harder for former military spouses like Sandra. But he suggested that, going forward, state courts can try to account for the possibility that a veteran could later waive some part of retirement pay in favor of disability benefits, or they can recalculate spousal support based on later changes in circumstances. In this case, however, the state courts did not do that. Instead, the Supreme Court noted, the state courts' rulings "rested entirely upon the need to restore Sandra's lost portion.""

Howe distinguishes what the court decided from what it merely observed, and she
attributes each to its source, the justice or the court, quoting the opinion where
the exact words matter. The care here is in keeping the levels apart: a holding, a
suggestion, a factual note. A reporter who understands the ruling and will not let
its parts blur together is visible in every clause.
