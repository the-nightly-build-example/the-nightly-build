# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/cnet-ai-articles (01)

## How this piece should sound

This lesson tells a story about a newsroom that trusted a system it hadn't
tested, and it should sound like the writers below telling that kind of
story: plain sentences carrying real facts, no adjective doing the work a
fact should do.

Open the way Ben Smith opens the Ozy piece: state what the system was built
to do and why it looked like it was working, in sentences that carry names,
dates, and dollar figures, before any judgment enters. The reader should finish the opening paragraph knowing exactly what was
published, by whom, and why nobody caught it yet, with no signal that
something bad is coming. Smith earns his ending by refusing to tip it
early; do the same with the moment CNET's AI-generated articles first
looked fine.

Let the discovery arrive the way Smith lets the conference call turn: "the
man's voice began to sound strange," reported flatly, with the sourcing
attached in the same sentence, not built up as a reveal. When this lesson
narrates the moment the errors surfaced, whoever found them, on whatever
date, write that turn the same way: report it, source it, move on. The
piece has done its job if the reader feels the ground shift without being
told to.

When the piece turns from what happened to why fluent AI prose fooled
editors and readers, write the mechanism the way Angwin's team writes the
Northpointe statistics: short declarative sentences, each one a single
claim, none of them dressed up. "It makes the opposite mistake among
whites" is a sentence anyone can check against the number before it. Find
the equivalent sentence for how a language model gets a specific claim
wrong while sounding exactly as certain as it does when it is right. That
contrast is this lesson's actual subject, and it deserves the same flat
delivery, not a metaphor standing in for the mechanism.

Where the lesson needs to explain why a newsroom would publish
unsupervised AI copy at all, reach for Newton's move in the Cognizant
economics: put the two figures next to each other and let the gap argue
the case. Newton doesn't call outsourced moderation exploitative. He
writes the moderator's wage next to the median Facebook employee's
compensation and stops. If the piece can find the equivalent pair of
numbers for AI-generated content versus staff-written content, that pair
does more work than a paragraph of characterization.

One craft move belongs only to the exemplar, not the lesson: Newton
narrates as "I," interviewing named sources on the record. This lesson's
body speaks to no one and narrates itself to no one, so it can't take that
shape. It can still take Newton's discipline of putting a named person's
own words at the point where the mechanism turns, rather than paraphrasing
what they meant.

Close on an earned, plain verdict, not a moral. Smith's last line doesn't
announce that fraud is bad. It names the specific line that got crossed,
fooling Goldman Sachs, and stops. The takeaway bookend can do the
equivalent: name the specific thing about fluent, confident, wrong prose
that this lesson has actually shown, not a general warning about AI. If a
closing sentence would be true of any AI failure story, it hasn't found
this one yet.

Throughout, resist the temptation these three writers all resist: none of
them writes a sentence telling the reader how to feel about the newsroom's
decision to run unverified AI copy. Angwin's team states the score two
defendants got and what each of them did afterward; Newton sets the two
salaries next to each other; Smith names the exact line Ozy crossed. Do
the equivalent for CNET's editorial decisions and let the specific facts
sit there ungarnished.

## Julia Angwin, Jeff Larson, Surya Mattu and Lauren Kirchner, "Machine Bias"

Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing

> "Just as the 18-year-old girls were realizing they were too big for the
> tiny conveyances — which belonged to a 6-year-old boy — a woman came
> running after them saying, 'That's my kid's stuff.' Borden and her
> friend immediately dropped the bike and scooter and walked away."

This is the opening scene of a piece about algorithmic bias in criminal
sentencing, and it contains no mention of an algorithm. The writers trust
a small, specific, almost comic crime, two teenagers on a stolen kid's
scooter, to carry the whole piece's stakes once the reader learns what
happened to the girl next. Nothing here editorializes; the facts are
simply sequenced so the next section can land.

> "Yet something odd happened when Borden and Prater were booked into
> jail: A computer program spat out a score predicting the likelihood of
> each committing a future crime. Borden — who is black — was rated a
> high risk. Prater — who is white — was rated a low risk. Two years
> later, we know the computer algorithm got it exactly backward. Borden
> has not been charged with any new crimes. Prater is serving an
> eight-year prison term for subsequently breaking into a warehouse and
> stealing thousands of dollars' worth of electronics."

The word "odd" is the only editorial gesture in this passage, and it's
immediately paid for by four flat, sourced sentences. The writers don't
say the algorithm was racist; they lay the two outcomes next to each
other and let "got it exactly backward" be a description, not a verdict.
The restraint is visible in what's missing: no sentence telling the
reader this is unfair.

> "Overall, Northpointe's assessment tool correctly predicts recidivism
> 61 percent of the time. But blacks are almost twice as likely as whites
> to be labeled a higher risk but not actually re-offend. It makes the
> opposite mistake among whites: They are much more likely than blacks to
> be labeled lower risk but go on to commit other crimes."

This is the piece's actual finding, and it's delivered in the plainest
sentences in the piece. Each sentence states one comparison. There's no
throat-clearing before the number and no adjective after it. The pattern
of the bias is left to do the work that a word like "alarming" would
otherwise be asked to do.

## Casey Newton, "The Trauma Floor"

Source: https://www.theverge.com/2019/2/25/18229714/cognizant-facebook-content-moderator-interviews-trauma-working-conditions-arizona

> "The panic attacks started after Chloe watched a man die. She spent the
> past three and a half weeks in training, trying to harden herself
> against the daily onslaught of disturbing posts: the hate speech, the
> violent attacks, the graphic pornography. In a few more days, she will
> become a full-time Facebook content moderator, or what the company she
> works for, a professional services vendor named Cognizant, opaquely
> calls a 'process executive.'"

Newton opens on a person, in the present tense, weeks before the reader
learns any of the institutional detail. The corporate euphemism, "process
executive," arrives only after the reader has already watched what the
job actually is, so the word does the work of showing the gap between the
language and the reality instead of Newton having to state it.

> "The use of contract labor also has a practical benefit for Facebook:
> it is radically cheaper. The median Facebook employee earns $240,000
> annually in salary, bonuses, and stock options. A content moderator
> working for Cognizant in Arizona, on the other hand, will earn just
> $28,800 per year. The arrangement helps Facebook maintain a high profit
> margin. In its most recent quarter, the company earned $6.9 billion in
> profits, on $16.9 billion in revenue."

This is the piece's economic argument, made entirely out of figures set
side by side. Newton never calls the arrangement exploitative; the
$240,000 next to the $28,800 makes the case, and the profit figures that
follow show why the company had no incentive to change it. It's a model
for explaining an incentive structure without narrating an opinion about
it.

> "That people don't know there are human beings doing this work is, of
> course, by design. Facebook would rather talk about its advancements in
> artificial intelligence, and dangle the prospect that its reliance on
> human moderators will decline over time."

The stepping-back sentence at the close of the piece names a mechanism,
concealment as a design choice, rather than delivering a verdict about
the company's character. "Of course" is doing real work here: it tells
the reader this follows from everything already shown, rather than
announcing a new judgment.

## Ben Smith, "Goldman Sachs, Ozy Media and a $40 Million Conference Call Gone Wrong"

Source: https://www.nytimes.com/2021/09/26/business/media/ozy-media-goldman-sachs.html

> "This past winter, Goldman Sachs was closing in on a $40 million
> investment in Ozy, a digital media company founded in 2013, and there
> seemed to be a lot of reasons to do the deal. Ozy boasted of a large
> audience for its general interest website, its newsletters and its
> videos, and the company had a charismatic chief executive, Carlos
> Watson, a onetime cable news anchor who had worked at Goldman Sachs
> early in his career. And, crucially, Ozy said it had a great
> relationship with YouTube, where many of its videos attracted more than
> a million views."

Three sentences establish the deal, the money, the executive, and the
specific claim that was about to be tested, with no signal yet that
anything is wrong. "There seemed to be a lot of reasons to do the deal"
is the only hint, and it's understated enough that a reader who doesn't
already know the story wouldn't catch it.

> "Once everyone had made the switch to an old-fashioned conference call,
> the guest told the bankers what they had been wanting to hear: that Ozy
> was a great success on YouTube, racking up significant views and ad
> dollars, and that Mr. Watson was as good a leader as he seemed to be.
> As he spoke, however, the man's voice began to sound strange to the
> Goldman Sachs team, as though it might have been digitally altered, the
> four people said."

The turn happens inside a single sentence, reported as a physical
observation, a voice sounding strange, with its sourcing attached
immediately ("the four people said"). Smith doesn't dramatize the moment;
he just reports what the people on the call noticed, in the order they
noticed it.

> "One thing is evident, though: Trying to fool the world's most famous
> bank, even by the hype-ridden standards of the media business, is way
> over the line."

The column's last line names the specific thing that was crossed, fooling
this particular bank, instead of a general statement that fraud is wrong.
"Even by the hype-ridden standards of the media business" is doing the
real argument: it concedes the industry's normal looseness first, so the
sentence can show exactly how far past it this went.
