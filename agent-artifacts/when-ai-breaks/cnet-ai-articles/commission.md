# Commission: when-ai-breaks/cnet-ai-articles

## The incident
Between late 2022 and January 2023, the technology-news site CNET quietly
published dozens of personal-finance explainer articles generated by an in-house
AI tool, bylined "CNET Money Staff" with only a faint disclosure. In January 2023,
Futurism reported that the pieces were AI-written and contained factual errors;
CNET's own review then found that a majority of the roughly 77 AI-generated
articles required corrections, some for substantive financial/math errors. CNET
paused the program, added disclosures and corrections, and the episode became the
reference case for undisclosed AI-generated journalism. A system was deployed (an
AI content engine on a major publisher), failed publicly, did harm (wrong
financial guidance to readers; damaged trust; staff fallout), and left a record.

## Why this incident, now
The reader now meets AI-written text everywhere and is told it is reliable. When
AI Breaks teaches the incident in order, then explains why that kind of system
fails that way: fluent, authoritative prose can be confidently wrong, and fluency
is exactly what makes the errors hard to catch. It also teaches the disclosure and
trust failure that turned an efficiency experiment into a credibility crisis.

## The angle (in order, then why, then where it lives now)
1. Tell it in order, named and dated: what the system was (CNET's AI content
   engine, later described by the company; used on CNET and Bankrate finance
   explainers), what it actually did (produced articles with factual errors —
   verify the specific documented ones, e.g., a compound-interest miscalculation),
   how it was disclosed (the faint "CNET Money Staff" byline), who caught it
   (Futurism's reporting, January 2023), who was affected (readers, CNET's
   newsroom and its unionizing staff, the CNET brand and its owner Red Ventures),
   and what the operator did afterward (editor's note by Connie Guglielmo, the
   audit finding a majority needed corrections, the pause, appended corrections).
2. Explain why that kind of system fails that way, teaching the mechanism or
   linking where the course taught it: a language model generates the most
   probable continuation, which reads fluent and authoritative whether or not it
   is correct; there is no step that checks a computed figure or a factual claim,
   and the polish suppresses the scrutiny a rough draft would draw. Link the
   course's existing lessons (the-mechanics/hallucination, and any lesson on why
   models get arithmetic wrong or sound confident) rather than re-teaching them.
3. Close on where the same weakness lives today: AI-generated content across
   publishing and search, the disclosure question, and what actually guards
   against it (human verification and transparency, not trust in fluency).

## Boundaries and continuity
Work from the record: CNET's own editor's notes and corrections (the operator's
primary account), the appended article corrections, and the reporting that broke
and stood up (Futurism, The Verge, Washington Post). Where a cause or count is
disputed (exact number of articles, how many needed corrections, how severe),
present what the record supports and say what is uncertain. Differentiate from
published neighbors and link rather than overlap:
- when-ai-breaks/ai-overviews, nyc-mycity-chatbot, air-canada-chatbot,
  mata-v-avianca: those are chatbots answering a user's question wrong (or a
  filing). This is different — a publisher passing AI-generated articles to the
  public as vetted editorial, undisclosed, with errors. The distinct failure is
  editorial/trust plus fluency-masking-error, not a chatbot Q&A.
No verdict block in the body; the takeaway lands the judgment.

- Production policy (balanced): researcher high/capable, coach low/capable,
