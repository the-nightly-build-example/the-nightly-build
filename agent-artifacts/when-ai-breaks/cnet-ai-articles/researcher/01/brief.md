The incident under examination: CNET's undisclosed AI-generated personal-finance
articles (late 2022-January 2023) and the corrections that followed. Work from the
record.

Research questions the evidence record must answer, each traced to the owning
primary (give locators):
- The facts in order, from primary/documented sources: when CNET began publishing
  AI-generated finance articles, on which sites (CNET, and whether Bankrate/other
  Red Ventures sites), under what byline ("CNET Money Staff") and disclosure, and
  the AI tool involved as CNET later described it.
- The numbers, verified and scoped: how many AI-generated articles were published,
  and how many were found to need corrections (the widely cited "more than half of
  ~77" figure) — attribute to CNET's own account/editor's note and note any
  uncertainty. Do not repeat a number you cannot source to a primary.
- The specific documented errors: at least one concrete factual/math error the
  reporting or the correction identified (e.g., a compound-interest example that
  overstated earnings). Quote or paraphrase the correction precisely.
- The people and outlets with correct names/roles: Connie Guglielmo
  (CNET editor-in-chief at the time) and her editor's note; the Futurism reporters
  who broke it (Jon Christian / Frank Landymore); The Verge (James Vincent) and
  Washington Post coverage; Red Ventures as owner. Get titles right.
- The aftermath: the pause of the program, the added disclosures/corrections, and
  any related fallout (e.g., staff unionization concerns). Dates.
- The mechanism, from primary/technical sources: why fluent AI-generated prose can
  be confidently wrong (next-token generation optimizes plausibility, not truth;
  no step verifies a computed figure), and why fluency suppresses editorial
  scrutiny. Cite a primary/technical source for the hallucination/fabrication
  mechanism.

Where a count or cause is disputed, record each side in Contradictions and say
what the record settles.

Source policy: at least 8 sources, at least 4 primary, at least 1 secondary.
Primary = CNET's own editor's notes and article correction notes (the operator's
record) and technical primaries for the mechanism. Secondary = the reporting that
broke and confirmed the story. Classify each by authorship and stake. Confirm
every URL resolves to the document's own page.
