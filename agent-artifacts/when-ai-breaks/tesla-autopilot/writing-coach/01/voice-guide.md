# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/tesla-autopilot

## How this piece should sound

This lesson tells what happened to two people in two Autopilot crashes, then
explains why a Level-2 system fails that way, using the record: crash
reports, NTSB and NHTSA findings, court filings, Tesla's own statements. The
three exemplars below share one discipline worth taking whole: they trust
the sequence of events, told in order with its own numbers, to do the work
that adjectives are usually asked to do.

Narrate each crash the way Langewiesche narrates the last two and a half
minutes of Flight 592: the clock running, the exact words radioed or spoken,
the instrument readouts, one event after the next with nothing added. When
the piece reaches the moment Autopilot did whatever it did on the road, the
same discipline applies. Give what the car's logs show it doing, in the
order the logs show it, before any sentence says what that means. The
Fitzgerald passage does the same in two flat sentences and needs nothing
else: state what happened to the people in it and stop.

The piece will need to explain why a Level-2 system fails this way: what the
sensors saw or didn't, what a driver-monitoring feature did or didn't catch.
Follow the chain the way Langewiesche follows the oxygen generator. State
this step, then the one it causes, then the next, plainly, and let the
mechanism do the explaining. The piece can name the parts of the system as
precisely as that passage names sodium-chlorate and firing pins, because the
reader needs to know exactly what the machine was doing.

The regulatory fight is a dispute between accounts: Tesla's, NHTSA's, the
families', maybe a court's. Gladwell's engineer and his Congressman never
agree, and the piece doesn't need to make them. It gives each side's account
in that side's own terms and lets the specifics show where they part, the
way Faturechi, Rose, and Miller let Mabus call two fatal collisions "a
coincidence" and leave it there. When this piece sets Tesla's account of
what Autopilot is built to do beside what the crash record shows it did,
that same restraint holds: state each side's claim, cite what supports or
contradicts it, and let the gap speak for itself.

Two people died in this piece, and the register for that is set by the
Ulrich paragraph and by the Fitzgerald sentence that puts the hole in the
hull and the drowned sailors in the same clause. Report what happened to
them in the same plain sentences as everything else. Neither passage tells
the reader how to feel. The facts are sufficient once the piece has earned
them with a specific, sourced account of what happened, and the piece can
trust them the same way.

Closing on where the same weakness lives today is where a plain declarative
lands hardest, the way Moran's line does at the end of the ProPublica piece:
a fact or an admission from the record, stated once, with no summary
sentence stacked on top of it to explain why it matters. The house voice is
already this register: plain claims, concrete stakes, understood on first
read. These passages show what that voice looks like applied to a subject
where two people are dead and a company disputes the cause.

## William Langewiesche, "The Lessons of ValuJet 592"

Source: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/2022-11/langewiesche.pdf
(The Atlantic Monthly, March 1998)

> "But six minutes out of Miami, while climbing northwest through 11,000
> feet, Richard Hazen radioed, 'Ah, five-ninety-two needs an immediate
> return to Miami.' In the deliberate calm of pilot talk this was strong
> language. The time was thirty-one seconds after 2:10 P.M., and the sun was
> shining. Something had gone wrong with the airplane."

The sentence measures its own alarm against a scale it first defines:
"strong language" sits inside a description of how pilots normally talk, so
the reader learns the code before being told it was broken. The clock time
and the flat detail about the sun carry the plainness of a report reading
its own instruments, which is exactly what a controller and a cockpit voice
recorder were doing at that second.

> "Then the incredible happened. The airplane rolled wings-level again and
> pulled sharply out of its dive. It is highly unlikely that the airplane
> would have done this on its own. It is possible that the autopilot kicked
> in, or that one of the pilots, having been incapacitated by smoke or
> defeated by melting control cables, somehow momentarily regained control."

Langewiesche allows himself one word of astonishment, "incredible," and
then spends the rest of the passage undoing it with competing technical
explanations, none of which he picks. The writer is visible in the
refusal to resolve the moment into a single cause before the evidence does.

> "Oxygen generators are safety devices. They are small steel canisters
> mounted in airplane ceilings and seatbacks and linked to the flimsy oxygen
> masks that dangle in front of passengers when a cabin loses
> pressurization. To activate oxygen flow the passenger pulls a lanyard,
> which slides a retaining pin from a spring-loaded hammer, which falls on a
> minute explosive charge, which sparks a chemical reaction that liberates
> the oxygen within the sodium-chlorate core."

Each clause hands off to the next on a "which," so the mechanism assembles
itself in front of the reader, one link at a time. Langewiesche's own
competence as a pilot shows in how little he needs to translate: he names
the hammer and the pin and trusts the sequence itself to be understandable.

## Robert Faturechi, Megan Rose, and T. Christian Miller, "Years of Warnings, Then Death and Disaster: How the Navy Failed Its Sailors"

Source: https://features.propublica.org/navy-accidents/us-navy-crashes-japan-cause-mccain/
(ProPublica, February 7, 2019)

> "At 1:30 a.m. on June 17, 2017, the USS Fitzgerald collided with a
> container ship off the coast of Japan. Ocean water flooded in through a
> gaping hole, killing seven sailors in the room where they slept and
> temporarily trapping the ship's commander in his bunk."

The technical fact and the human one share a single sentence, joined by
nothing heavier than "killing." The detail about the commander trapped in
his bunk is not explained or dwelt on. It is stated once, in the same flat
tense as the time of the collision, and the paragraph moves on.

> "According to the investigative report, the McCain was steaming through
> the crowded Singapore Strait en route to a shipyard on the country's
> northern coast, when the captain noticed that one of his sailors was
> having difficulty staying on course and maintaining the ship's speed at
> the same time. The captain ordered a second crew member at a different
> computer console to take control of the speed, but that only led to more
> confusion and eventually panic. No one fully understood how to use the new
> touchscreen navigation system. For several minutes, the crew mistakenly
> thought it had lost control of the steering. Then they accidentally
> slowed the propeller on only one side, forcing the McCain to turn sharply
> left."

The sentences are ordered the way the confusion actually escalated on the
bridge: one crew member reassigned, then panic, then a wrong belief about
what had failed, then the actual mechanical error. The reader arrives at
the cause the same way the crew did, one wrong turn at a time, and the
finding comes last, where it belongs.

> "In an interview, Mabus, in fact, said he didn't recall the Balisle report
> that had so worried his deputy. 'I'm sure I knew about it when it came
> out,' he said."

The reporters set the denial and the concession next to each other without
a word of comment between them. The contradiction is left to the reader
because the two sentences, placed together, already make it visible.

## Malcolm Gladwell, "The Engineer's Lament"

Source: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2015/05/04/the-engineers-lament
(The New Yorker, April 27, 2015)

> "The Pinto exploded in flames. Shards of glass scattered in every
> direction. The car spun around and around, stopping a hundred and fifty
> feet from the point of impact. The fire reached almost thirteen hundred
> degrees, melting the sunglasses around Lyn's eyes. Lyn and Donna were
> killed instantly. Judy Ulrich lay in the grass with burns over ninety-five
> per cent of her body, crying out, 'Help me. Please, help me.' She died
> eight hours later."

Gladwell names the girls and states what happened to their bodies in the
same declarative sentences he uses for the distance the car spun. The
register holds steady through the whole paragraph, including the deaths.
The one quoted cry is the only moment the passage lets a person speak, and
it is left to stand on its own, with no line telling the reader how to
receive it.

> "So imagine the case in the recall officer's stripped-down version. The
> relevant question is not who died. He's not dwelling on the tragedy of
> three teen-age girls. His question is: Why did they die?"

The short, blunt sentences read like the recall officer's own mind at work:
each one states a fact of his job in his own procedural terms. Gladwell is
visible in the decision to let that coldness sit unexplained for a moment
before the piece goes on to earn it.

> "To the engineer, a car sits somewhere on the gradient of acceptability.
> To the public, a car's status is binary: it is either broken or working,
> flawed or functional."

Two sentences, one for each side, built the same way and holding equal
weight: neither gets the last word or the qualifying clause. Gladwell
states the engineer's frame and the public's frame in parallel sentences of
identical length, and the collision between them is the argument. He never
steps in to referee it himself.
