# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks / replit-agent-deletes-database

## How this piece should sound

This is one incident, told in order, then explained. The reader has never
touched a database or an autonomous coding agent, so the piece has to earn
both halves separately: what the agent actually did, dated and named, before
any claim about why that kind of system does that kind of thing.

Open in the room, not at the podium. Greenberg's help-desk paragraph below
opens on people who don't yet understand what's happening to them, not on a
sentence announcing that something significant has happened. This piece has
its own version of that moment — whoever first saw the data gone, or read
the agent's own account of what it had done — and that moment, not a claim
about AI agents in general, is where the piece should start.

When a term the reader doesn't hold has to enter — agent, autonomous coding
tool, rollback, sandbox, whatever the actual mechanism turns out to need —
define it the way Somers defines spaghetti code: name it, then explain it in
the same breath, in words the reader already has. The lesson template is
stricter than the house style on this point already; Somers is a model of
what that discipline looks like in a sentence that still moves.

Ground the mechanism in the dated, specific moment before widening to the
general pattern. Somers doesn't open with a claim about software complexity;
he opens with Jean Bookout's specific crash, and only climbs to the general
mechanism once that scene is on the page. This piece has its own specific
sequence of what the agent did and said — that comes first. The explanation
of why autonomous agents fail this way comes after, earned by that sequence,
not stated ahead of it.

A physical or already-held comparison is worth reaching for only when it is
exact. Greenberg's stolen-warden's-keys line and Schulz's hand-and-tectonic-
plate demonstration both work because the writer checked that the comparison
matched the actual mechanism before using it. If the reporting turns up a
specific credential, permission, or boundary the agent crossed, a comparison
like these could carry it. If the mechanism is fuzzier than that, no
comparison is better than a forced one.

A systemic insight, once the mechanism has actually been built up, can be
said in a single plain sentence — Schulz's "one enormous problem causes many
other enormous problems" lands because a full explanation of the fault line
sits underneath it. If this piece earns a comparable line about why one
wrong action from an autonomous agent has nothing to catch it, that line can
be just as short, but only after the piece has taught what makes the catch
absent.

Keep the intensity in the facts, not in the verbs. Greenberg's excerpt reaches
for "rampage" and "lose its mind"; that register belongs to a thriller
excerpt, not to a paper whose house voice is plain claims and concrete
stakes. The moment this piece reports what the agent actually deleted and
how it described its own actions afterward is exactly the moment a writer is
tempted to reach for a charged verb instead of the specific fact doing the
work — Somers's and Schulz's restraint at their own most dramatic moments is
the standard to hold instead.

## Kathryn Schulz, "The Really Big One"

Source: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2015/07/20/the-really-big-one

> Take your hands and hold them palms down, middle fingertips touching. Your
> right hand represents the North American tectonic plate, which bears on
> its back, among other things, our entire continent, from One World Trade
> Center to the Space Needle, in Seattle. Your left hand represents an
> oceanic plate called Juan de Fuca, ninety thousand square miles in size.
> The place where they meet is the Cascadia subduction zone. Now slide your
> left hand under your right one. That is what the Juan de Fuca plate is
> doing: slipping steadily beneath North America.

Schulz turns an invisible, centuries-long mechanism into something the
reader's own hands can do. She never says "imagine" or "picture this" — she
just gives the instruction, one clause at a time, and lets the demonstration
carry the explanation. The physical instruction is where she is visible:
she chose the one comparison a reader could actually perform at their desk,
rather than a comparison that only sounded vivid.

> What killed the trees in the ghost forest was saltwater. It had long been
> assumed that they died slowly, as the sea level around them gradually rose
> and submerged their roots. But, by 1987, Atwater, who had found in soil
> layers evidence of sudden land subsidence along the Washington coast,
> suspected that that was backward — that the trees had died quickly when
> the ground beneath them plummeted. To find out, he teamed up with
> Yamaguchi, a specialist in dendrochronology, the study of growth-ring
> patterns in trees. Yamaguchi took samples of the cedars and found that
> they had died simultaneously: in tree after tree, the final rings dated to
> the summer of 1699.

This is reconstruction, not summary: a wrong assumption, the person who
doubted it, the specific method he used to test it, and the specific finding
that settled it, in that order. The technical term arrives exactly once,
defined in the same sentence it's introduced. Schulz is visible in the
sequencing — she lets the reader watch the evidence get built rather than
handing over the conclusion first.

> Certain disasters stem from many small problems conspiring to cause one
> very large problem. For want of a nail, the war was lost; for fifteen
> independently insignificant errors, the jetliner was lost. Subduction-zone
> earthquakes operate on the opposite principle: one enormous problem causes
> many other enormous problems. The shaking from the Cascadia quake will set
> off landslides throughout the region — up to thirty thousand of them in
> Seattle alone, the city's emergency-management office estimates.

By this point in the piece, Schulz has spent several pages building the
mechanics of the fault line, so this generalization costs her one sentence
rather than a paragraph of hedging. She states the pattern, contrasts it
with the more familiar failure pattern in one clause, and moves straight
into a concrete number. Nothing here asks to be taken on the strength of the
sentence alone — it's paid for by everything before it.

## James Somers, "The Coming Software Apocalypse"

Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/archive/2017/09/saving-the-world-from-code/540393/

> In September 2007, Jean Bookout was driving on the highway with her best
> friend in a Toyota Camry when the accelerator seemed to get stuck. When
> she took her foot off the pedal, the car didn't slow down. She tried the
> brakes but they seemed to have lost their power. As she swerved toward an
> off-ramp going 50 miles per hour, she pulled the emergency brake. The car
> left a skid mark 150 feet long before running into an embankment by the
> side of the road. The passenger was killed. Bookout woke up in a hospital
> a month later.

Every sentence here is an action or a fact a reader could check against a
police report, in the order Bookout experienced them. Somers doesn't tell
the reader this crash illustrates a wider problem — he hasn't earned that
yet — he just gives the sequence and lets the details (the pedal, the brakes,
the exact skid length) do the work. He is visible in what he leaves out: no
adjective describes the crash, because the facts don't need one.

> Michael Barr, an expert witness for the plaintiff, had a team of software
> experts spend 18 months with the Toyota code, picking up where NASA left
> off. Barr described what they found as "spaghetti code," programmer lingo
> for software that has become a tangled mess. Code turns to spaghetti when
> it accretes over many years, with feature after feature piling on top of,
> and being woven around, what's already there; eventually the code becomes
> impossible to follow, let alone to test exhaustively for flaws.

This is the clearest model in any of these three pieces for handling a term
of art. Somers names it in quotation marks as the expert's own word, glosses
it in four words ("programmer lingo for"), and then spends one more sentence
on the mechanism behind the metaphor, so the reader understands not just what
the phrase means but why the code ends up that way. Nothing here assumes the
reader has written a line of code.

## Andy Greenberg, "The Untold Story of NotPetya, the Most Devastating Cyberattack in History"

Source: https://www.wired.com/story/notpetya-cyberattack-ukraine-russia-code-crashed-the-world/

> That gift shop also houses a technology help center, a single desk manned
> by IT troubleshooters next to the shop's cashier. And on the afternoon of
> June 27, 2017, confused Maersk staffers began to gather at that help desk
> in twos and threes, almost all of them carrying laptops. On the machines'
> screens were messages in red and black lettering. Some read "repairing
> file system on C:" with a stark warning not to turn off the computer.
> Others, more surreally, read "oops, your important files are encrypted"
> and demanded a payment of $300 worth of bitcoin to decrypt them.

Greenberg starts the incident before anyone in it knows what it is. The
staffers don't know yet that this is a historic cyberattack — they just know
their laptops are doing something strange, and they're standing in twos and
threes at a help desk. The dated, exact detail (June 27, 2017, the specific
error text on screen) is what lets the scene work without Greenberg having
to tell the reader it's important.

> NotPetya was propelled by two powerful hacker exploits working in tandem:
> One was a penetration tool known as EternalBlue, created by the US
> National Security Agency but leaked in a disastrous breach of the agency's
> ultrasecret files earlier in 2017. EternalBlue takes advantage of a
> vulnerability in a particular Windows protocol, allowing hackers free rein
> to remotely run their own code on any unpatched machine.

Two named tools, each introduced with where it came from before what it
does — the origin story of EternalBlue (an NSA tool, leaked) is doing real
explanatory work, not decoration, because it's the reason this particular
exploit existed to be combined with another one. Greenberg is visible in
the ordering: provenance first, mechanism second, because the provenance is
part of how the failure was possible at all.

> After a quick examination of the bank's surviving logs, Yasinsky could see
> that the attack was an automated worm that had somehow obtained an
> administrator's credentials. That had allowed it to rampage through the
> bank's network like a prison inmate who has stolen the warden's keys.

The comparison works because it's exact: an administrator's credentials are
a set of keys that open every door, and the theft of them is what let the
attack move freely rather than being caught at the first locked door. Note
for this piece: "rampage" is the kind of verb this desk's plainer register
should leave behind even where the comparison itself is worth keeping.
