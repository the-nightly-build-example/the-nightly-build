# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/shotspotter-wrongful-arrest

## How this piece should sound

This lesson opens on a specific night in a specific car, and the Colloff and
Armstrong/Miller passages below show what it costs to stay that concrete for a
whole paragraph: exact addresses, exact times, exact ages, no adjective doing
the work a fact could do. Tell what the ShotSpotter alert said, what the
officers did, and what happened to Michael Williams in that register — dates,
distances, durations — the way Colloff clocks Mickey Bryan's murder to the
minute and the block. A reader should be able to reconstruct the sequence from
the nouns alone.

The company's dispute is exactly the kind of contested cause Colloff's flashlight
cross-examination handles by getting out of the way. She doesn't referee the
question of whether Charlie Blue planted the flashlight; she puts McDonald and
Blue's own exchange on the page and lets a reader watch it settle nothing. Where
this lesson gives ShotSpotter's own account of its accuracy and the oversight
findings that contest it, the strongest move is the same one: state each claim
in its source's own terms, side by side, rather than adjudicating in the
narrator's voice before the reader has seen both.

Armstrong and Miller's false-reporting paragraph is close to a template for the
base-rate section this lesson has to write: name the intuition first ("false
accusations are common," here it would be "an alert means a gunshot happened"),
then supply the actual rate, sourced, without an adjective standing in for the
number. Their closing statistic does the next thing this lesson will also need:
it anchors a rate to a comparison the reader already holds ("five times the
national average") instead of asserting that a figure is alarming and expecting
the reader to take the writer's word for it.

Gawande's cancer-cluster piece is the clearest model here for teaching the
base-rate mechanism itself, because he never states it as a rule. He hands the
reader a worked count — how many of five thousand census tracts would show a
"significant" cancer cluster by chance alone, given nothing but the number of
cancers tracked — and lets the reader watch the seemingly damning number turn out
to be exactly what randomness predicts. The gunshot-detector version of this
argument wants the same shape: a concrete count of how many alerts a system
tuned to catch a rare event will still flag when nothing happened, worked
through with the actual numbers this lesson has, not asserted as a general
truth about detectors.

Both investigative pieces below also earn their emotional weight without
supplying it directly. Colloff reports the naked, blood-soaked bedroom in flat
declaratives and lets the facts carry what horror there is; Armstrong and Miller
narrate what happened to Marie in short, sequential sentences with no aside
telling the reader how to feel about it. A wrongful eleven months in Cook
County jail carries its own weight the same way. State what happened to Williams
plainly and let the fact of eleven months do what an adjective would otherwise
be asked to do.

Because this is one lesson, not a multi-part investigation, it doesn't have the
room these two pieces use to roam through decades of institutional history. Pick
the scenes and sources that carry the mechanism this lesson has to teach —
detection, alert, arrest, dismissal, oversight finding — and let the rest go,
rather than borrowing their scope along with their register.

## Pamela Colloff, "Blood Will Tell" (Part One)

Source: https://features.propublica.org/blood-spatter/mickey-bryan-murder-blood-spatter-forensic-evidence/

> "Most mornings, the sky was still black when Mickey Bryan made the short drive
> from her house on Avenue O, through the small central Texas town of Clifton, to
> the elementary school. Sometimes her car was the only one on the road. The
> low-slung, red-brick school building sat just south of the junction of State
> Highway 6 and Farm to Market Road 219 — a crossroads that, until recent years,
> featured the town's sole traffic light. Mickey was always the first teacher to
> arrive, usually settling in at her desk by 7 a.m."

This is the opening of a thirty-year murder story, and it spends its first four
sentences on a school building and a traffic light. The road numbers and the
7 a.m. arrival time are doing the work a mood-setting adjective usually does:
they tell the reader this account has been checked against a map and a clock
before it was checked against anyone's feelings about it.

> "In the bedroom, blood was everywhere — spattered across the bed, the ceiling,
> all four walls. Daniels immediately took hold of Vera and instructed her and
> Otis to go to the living room. He did not step any farther into the bedroom,
> but as he stood in the doorway, he could tell that Mickey was dead.
>
> Her body lay across the length of the unmade bed, her outstretched legs
> dangling over the mattress's edge. Her pink nightgown was drawn up to the top
> of her thighs, and she was naked from the waist down."

The worst physical facts of the murder are reported in the same flat
declarative sentences as everything around them — no sentence here is built to
make the reader flinch. The horror is left entirely to the facts named, which is
what makes this a dry-eyed telling of a genuinely awful scene rather than a
restrained-sounding one.

> "'Have you ever thought about it — perhaps you have, Mr. Blue — how would you
> prove to this jury that you didn't put that flashlight in that box?' McDonald
> asked.
> 'I'm here to testify what I found,' Blue said.
> 'I'm asking you, sir, if you were called on — other than your word and perhaps
> the word of Mr. Saunders — how can you tell us and prove positively that you
> didn't put it in there?'
> 'I didn't,' Blue shot back. 'I found it in there.'"

On the single piece of evidence the whole case turns on, Colloff quotes the
cross-examination verbatim and stops. She doesn't tell the reader who to
believe or step in with a summarizing judgment; the disputed fact is left
exactly as unsettled on the page as it was left in the courtroom, attributed
line by line to the two men who actually said it.

## T. Christian Miller and Ken Armstrong, "An Unbelievable Story of Rape"

Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/false-rape-accusations-an-unbelievable-story

> "He moved deliberately. He tied her hands loosely behind her. From a large
> black bag, he took out thigh-high stockings, clear plastic high heels with
> pink ribbons, lubrication, a box of moist towelettes and bottled water. Over
> the next four hours, he raped her repeatedly. He documented the assault with a
> digital camera and threatened to post the pictures online if she contacted the
> police."

Five short sentences, each one an action and nothing else, carry a sequence
that would read as more sensational if any single sentence tried to carry more.
The plainness of "he moved deliberately" is doing more than a description of
his state of mind would, because it is a fact the rest of the paragraph then
proves rather than a claim the reader has to take on faith.

> "The fear of false rape accusations has a long history in the legal system. In
> the 1600s, England's chief justice, Matthew Hale, warned that rape 'is an
> accusation easily to be made and hard to be proved, and harder to be defended
> by the party accused.' Judges in the U.S. read the so-called Hale warning to
> juries until the 1980s. But most recent research suggests that false reporting
> is relatively rare. FBI figures show that police annually declare around 5
> percent of rape cases unfounded, or baseless. Social scientists examining
> police records in detail and using methodologically rigorous standards cite
> similar, single-digit rates."

This passage names the widespread intuition first, in a historical quotation
that shows exactly how old and how authoritative that intuition once was, before
turning to what the actual rate is. The figure arrives sourced to two different
kinds of records — FBI counts and academic studies — rather than to a single
number asserted on its own.

> "In 2008, Marie's case was one of four labeled unfounded by the Lynnwood
> police, according to statistics reported to the FBI. In the five years from
> 2008 to 2012, the department determined that 10 of 47 rapes reported to
> Lynnwood police were unfounded — 21.3 percent. That's five times the national
> average of 4.3 percent for agencies covering similar-sized populations during
> that same period."

The rate on its own — 21.3 percent — would mean little to a reader without a
baseline to measure it against. The sentence that follows gives that baseline
in the same breath, as a multiple of the number just stated, so the reader
never has to hold the figure in the abstract for more than one sentence before
learning what it is large or small compared with.

## Atul Gawande, "The Cancer-Cluster Myth"

Source: https://www.sandiegocounty.gov/hhsa/programs/phs/documents/PHS_Cancer_Concerns_Cancer_Cluster_Myth_Gawande99-NewYorker.pdf

> "Raymond Richard Neutra points out that, given a typical registry of eighty
> different cancers, you could expect twenty-seven hundred and fifty of
> California's five thousand census tracts to have statistically significant but
> perfectly random elevations of cancer. So if you check to see whether your
> neighborhood has an elevated rate of a specific cancer, chances are better
> than even that it does—and it almost certainly won't mean a thing."

This is the piece's whole argument compressed into two sentences, and it never
states the underlying principle in the abstract. Instead it runs the actual
count — how many of five thousand tracts chance alone would flag — so the
reader arrives at the counterintuitive conclusion by doing the arithmetic
along with the writer, not by being told to accept it.

> "During the Germans' intensive bombing of South London in the Second World
> War, a few areas were hit several times and others were not hit at all. The
> places that were not hit seemed to have been deliberately spared, and,
> Kahneman says, people became convinced that those places were where the
> Germans had their spies. When Feller analyzed the statistics of the bomb hits,
> however, he found that the distribution matched a random pattern."

A second worked example, unrelated to cancer entirely, shows the same illusion
operating on a completely different kind of data. Putting the human
explanation (spies) right next to the statistical one (a random distribution)
side by side, in that order, lets the reader feel the pull of the wrong
explanation before the piece corrects it.

> "Health officials who understand the fear and anger can have impressive
> success, as the ones in Minnesota have shown. But there are times when you
> cannot maintain public trust without acting on public concerns. Science alone
> won't put to rest questions like the one a McFarland mother posed to the Los
> Angeles Times: 'How many more of our children must die before something is
> done?'"

The piece's final line is someone else's real quoted question, not the
writer's own summarizing verdict. Ending on a fact the reporting turned up,
rather than on a sentence built to sound conclusive, is what keeps thirty-seven
inches of statistical argument from closing on a note that undercuts it.
