# Evidence: when-ai-breaks/shotspotter-wrongful-arrest (01)

The record strongly supports the commissioned account. Michael Williams was held
in Cook County Jail for roughly eleven months on a first-degree murder charge in
which a ShotSpotter alert was the anchoring evidence, and Cook County prosecutors
moved to dismiss the case in July 2021 for insufficient evidence. He was never
tried and never convicted. The two contested mechanics of the alert are on the
record from court documents as reported by the AP and are not denied by the
company, only reframed: ShotSpotter's system first labeled the sound a firecracker
and a company reviewer relabeled it a gunshot, and a company engineer changed the
alert's reported location to the intersection where Williams was driving, about a
mile from the address the system first assigned. The oversight figures are firm
and owned by their sources: the Chicago OIG found 9.1% of dispatched alerts
produced evidence of a gun-related offense over 17 months, and the MacArthur
Justice Center found 89% of alerts turned up no gun crime over 21 months. Chicago
let the contract lapse and decommissioned the system on September 22, 2024.

Where the record is thin or contested: the day of Williams's arrest is not pinned
to an exact date in the sources here (August 2020, "nearly a year" before the July
2021 dismissal); his age is reported inconsistently (63 in his own complaint, 65
in the AP); and neither side has produced an independent, controlled test of
whether the sensors can tell gunfire from firecrackers or backfires, which is the
one measurement that would settle the accuracy dispute. A 2026 development sharpens
the story rather than undermining it: Chicago agreed to pay Williams $500,000 to
settle his suit, and SoundThinking publicly claims that its own later analysis is
what freed him. That claim is addressed in Contradictions.

The evidence does not undermine the commissioned angle. It complicates one word of
it: SoundThinking's position is that its technology cleared Williams as well as
helped charge him, and the piece has to carry both without letting the company's
framing stand as the last word or ignoring it.

## Contradictions

- **What an alert measures.** The critics (OIG, MacArthur) and the company are not
  measuring the same thing. OIG and MJC count downstream outcomes: how often an
  alert led police to evidence of a gun crime (9.1% for OIG; 11% of alerts yielded
  gun-crime evidence by the company's own reading of MJC). The company says that
  test is wrong, because an alert is itself evidence that gunfire occurred, and
  that missing physical evidence reflects slow arrival, uncooperative witnesses, or
  offenders leaving, not a detection error. Neither the outcome figures nor the
  company's 97% settle the underlying question, which is detection accuracy:
  whether the sensors reliably tell gunfire from firecrackers, backfires, and
  fireworks. What would settle it: an independent, controlled test against ground
  truth. MJC states no such test has been published; the company's 97% is
  self-reported and customer-reported, and counts an alert as wrong only when
  police file a voluntary complaint, which MJC and the complaint say rarely happens.

- **Did ShotSpotter jail Williams or free him?** The AP account and the complaint
  frame the alert, the firecracker-to-gunshot relabel, and the one-mile relocation
  as what anchored the charge. SoundThinking's 2026 statement claims the opposite
  role: that it proactively told prosecutors the in-car theory was not supported by
  the acoustic evidence and that this secured his release. Both can hold: the
  initial alert and its human modifications helped build the charge in 2020, and
  the company's later analysis may have helped unwind it in 2021. The company also
  stresses the arrest came nearly three months after the alert, not at the scene,
  to distance the alert from the arrest. What would settle the timeline: the Cook
  County court record showing when the company contacted prosecutors and what
  moved the dismissal. The dismissal itself was for insufficient evidence, not an
  affirmative finding of innocence; the prosecution's own words were that the
  evidence could not meet its burden of proof.

- **The relocation: "moved" vs "corrected."** The AP reports engineer Walter
  Collier changed the alert's reported address to the street where Williams was
  driving, about a mile from the first-assigned location. ShotSpotter says Collier
  corrected the initial real-time address to match what its sensors had actually
  identified. The dispute is over intent and correctness, not over the fact that a
  human changed the location after the fact. What would settle it: the raw sensor
  data and the internal record of the change.

- **Williams's age.** His own complaint (MacArthur) says 63; the AP says 65; some
  coverage said 64. Prefer the complaint's 63 unless a court filing states
  otherwise, and attribute if used.

- **Court-use count.** AP's lede says ShotSpotter evidence has reached "some 200"
  courtrooms; the same article cites a company press release figure of 91 cases in
  four years. These are different measures over different windows. Do not merge
  them; if a figure is used, name its source and scope.
