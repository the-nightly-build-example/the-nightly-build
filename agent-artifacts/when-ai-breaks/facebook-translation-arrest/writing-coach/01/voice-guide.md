# Voice guide: the Facebook translation arrest

## How this piece should sound

This is one arrest, told in order, and then one mechanism, explained. The
Langewiesche piece below narrates a crash for pages before it ever mentions an
oxygen generator, and when it gets there the sentence that explains the
generator ("To activate oxygen flow the passenger pulls a lanyard, which
slides a retaining pin...") reads in the same voice as the sentence that
counted the dead. That is the register this lesson needs: the paragraph that
explains why a translation model outputs the wrong token should not sound like
a different article from the paragraph that describes a man being read his
rights. No throat-clearing transition, no "but how did this happen" pivot
sentence — the explanation just starts, the way Langewiesche's does.

Keep the facts doing the work the way Miller and Armstrong do. "Each ring of
the phone seemed to announce another friendship, lost" carries its weight
because it is a fact about a phone ringing, not a sentence about how painful
isolation is. This piece has a real person under arrest, in a real country, on
a real date; name the person, the officer or platform employee if named in the
record, the date, and let those specifics carry the stakes. Nothing needs a
sentence telling the reader that a wrongful arrest is serious — the brief
already asks for precision and no editorializing about the conflict, and the
discipline the exemplars show is exactly this: the narration states what
happened and stops.

Gawande's line — "Medicine today has entered its B-17 phase" — is what an
earned analogy looks like: it arrives after forty paragraphs of intensive-care
detail have built the case for it, and it is a claim, not a decoration. If this
lesson reaches for a comparison to explain what machine translation is doing
when it fails, it should be built the same way: set up by the specific case,
stated once, and put to work rather than admired. And Langewiesche's closing
judgment on ValuJet — "They were unlucky, and the system killed them" — earns
its bluntness only because a dozen preceding paragraphs of procedural detail
substantiate every word in it. This piece has room, given a 1200–2200 word
band and a single incident, for at most one sentence with that kind of
weight, and it belongs at the point where the mechanism explanation has just
finished paying for it, not before.

The series prompt asks for what the system was built to do, what it actually
did, who it was affected, and what the operator did afterward, in that order.
All three exemplars below withhold the mechanism until the incident has been
told in full — Miller and Armstrong do not explain touch DNA until detectives
are already using it; Langewiesche does not explain the oxygen generator until
the reader already knows 110 people are dead. Follow the same order here: tell
the arrest as it happened to the person it happened to, and only then open up
how the translation system produced the words that got him arrested. Once the
explanation opens, a term the lesson's reader will not already hold — a
translation model's confidence score, a false positive, whatever the specific
failure turns out to be — gets defined in plain words in the sentence that
introduces it, the way Gawande defines "touch DNA" and "line infection" the
moment each enters the piece, never before.

## William Langewiesche, "The Lessons of ValuJet 592"

Source: https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/2022-11/langewiesche.pdf
(The Atlantic, March 1998)

> "By the time Walton Little felt the shock wave, everyone aboard was
> dead — two pilots, three flight attendants, and 105 passengers. Their
> remains lay in a shallow, watery crater filled with liquid mud and grass.
> All that marked the surface was a fractured engine, a few dead fish, some
> jet fuel, and a scattering of personal papers, clothes, and twisted pieces
> of aluminum — the stuff of tragedy."

This is the death toll and the aftermath in one paragraph, and neither
sentence tells the reader how to feel about it. The horror is in the list —
fish, fuel, papers, clothes, aluminum — laid out with no adjective doing extra
work beyond "the stuff of tragedy," a phrase earned by everything that came
before it rather than substituting for it.

> "Oxygen generators are safety devices. They are small steel canisters
> mounted in airplane ceilings and seatbacks and linked to the flimsy oxygen
> masks that dangle in front of passengers when a cabin loses pressurization.
> To activate oxygen flow the passenger pulls a lanyard, which slides a
> retaining pin from a spring-loaded hammer, which falls on a minute
> explosive charge, which sparks a chemical reaction that liberates the
> oxygen within the sodium-chlorate core."

The technical explanation arrives with no signal that a different kind of
writing is starting — same short declaratives, same voice that just spent
five pages on a crash. The chain of "which... which... which" mimics the
actual mechanical sequence instead of summarizing it, so the reader follows
the failure step by step rather than being told a step was missed.

> "It was a routine moment in a routine day. The morning's pesky electrical
> problems had perhaps been resolved. The crew was calmly and rationally
> preparing the airplane for the next flight, a procedure that had always
> worked for them before. As a result the passengers' last line of defense
> folded. They were unlucky, and the system killed them."

This is the piece's hardest sentence — "the system killed them" — and it
survives because "routine" is repeated rather than varied, underlining that
nothing about the moment looked like a mistake while it was happening. The
verdict is short and comes last, after the routine has been established twice,
not before it.

## T. Christian Miller (ProPublica) and Ken Armstrong (The Marshall Project), "An Unbelievable Story of Rape"

Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/false-rape-accusations-an-unbelievable-story
(ProPublica / The Marshall Project, December 16, 2015)

> "Each ring of the phone seemed to announce another friendship, lost. A
> friend from 10th grade called to ask: How could you lie about something
> like that? Marie — that's her middle name, Marie — didn't say anything.
> She just listened, then hung up."

The devastation is delivered as a small, literal fact — a phone ringing — and
the sentence stops there. "Marie — that's her middle name, Marie" is the kind
of detail a writer keeps only because it is true and specific, not because it
makes a point; it tells the reader the name is being protected without saying
so.

> "The investigators decided to try to get a sample of O'Leary's DNA. Though
> the degraded DNA lifted from the crime scenes could not definitively match
> O'Leary's DNA, it could show that a male from his family line had most
> likely committed the crime. If detectives could eliminate O'Leary's male
> relatives, they could place O'Leary at the scene of the crimes with a high
> degree of certainty."

The forensic explanation is written in the same plain declarative sentences as
the arrest scenes around it — no shift into a more technical register, and no
hedge word dressed up as caution. "Could show," "most likely," and "high
degree of certainty" are the actual epistemic status of the evidence, stated
once and moved past.

> "Rittgarn asked Marie what was going on. Marie said she really had been
> raped — and began to cry, saying she was having visions of the man on top
> of her. She wanted to take a lie detector test. Rittgarn told Marie that if
> she took the test and failed, she would be booked into jail."

The interrogation is rendered almost entirely in reported action — who asked
what, who said what — with no commentary attached to any line. The reader
supplies the judgment; the sentence that would supply it for them is the one
this piece never writes.

## Atul Gawande, "The Checklist"

Source: https://lchc.ucsd.edu/cogn_150/Readings/gawande_checklist.pdf
(The New Yorker, December 10, 2007)

> "When he arrived in our I.C.U., at 1:30 A.M. on a Sunday, his ragged black
> hair was plastered to his sweaty forehead, his body was shaking, and his
> heart was racing at a hundred and fourteen beats a minute. He was delirious
> from fever, shock, and low oxygen levels. 'I need to get out!' he cried. 'I
> need to get out!' He clawed at his gown, his oxygen mask, the dressings
> covering his abdominal wound."

The patient's panic is rendered through what a bystander could actually see
and hear — hair, heart rate, a repeated line of dialogue, hands on a gown —
never through a word for how frightening the scene was. Gawande is the doctor
in the room and does not step outside the scene to say so.

> "This is the reality of intensive care: at any point, we are as apt to harm
> as we are to heal. Line infections are so common that they are considered
> a routine complication. I.C.U.s put five million lines into patients each
> year, and national statistics show that, after ten days, four per cent of
> those lines become infected."

The turn from scene to statistic happens inside one paragraph with no seam:
the number arrives the sentence after the claim it supports, doing the work of
proving "so common" rather than just asserting it. The register does not rise
for the data — it is the same voice that just described a man clawing at his
gown.

> "Medicine today has entered its B-17 phase. Substantial parts of what
> hospitals do — most notably, intensive care — are now too complex for
> clinicians to carry them out reliably from memory alone. I.C.U. life
> support has become too much medicine for one person to fly."

The analogy is stated once, in a single declarative sentence, after several
pages spent earning it with the Model 299 story. It is not decorated or
repeated for emphasis — Gawande trusts that the reader who followed the
aviation story will feel the sentence do its work without being told to.
