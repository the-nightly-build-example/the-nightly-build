# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/saferent-tenant-screening (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a lesson that walks through one tenant-screening failure in order and then teaches why a scoring system fails that way. The reader is sharp and widely read but has never seen the inside of a screening algorithm, so the piece has to carry them from what a tenant-screening score is to why this one denied the applicants it denied. Write it the way Matt Yglesias explains something he understands: plain claims, concrete stakes, and when a sentence could sound good or be understood, choose understood.

Report the human cost the way Langewiesche reports the crash. When the piece reaches the applicant who was screened out, name what happened to them in exact terms and stop, the way he writes "everyone aboard was dead" and then gives the count and lists what was left on the water. A denied application, a lost apartment, a housing voucher that expired unused are heavy facts on their own, and stating them plainly does more than any adjective telling the reader they are heavy. Fink's Memorial opening works the same way, a row of bodies reported one physical detail at a time.

Teach the mechanism concretely rather than in the abstract. A reader who does not know how a tenant-screening score is built needs the equivalent of Schulz flattening her hand to show a subduction zone, or Langewiesche walking one real crash through the "engineered" category he has just named. Where the piece explains what went into the score, or how a cutoff turned a number into a rejection, ground it in the actual inputs and the actual threshold, not in a general account of how algorithms work.

Hold the precision in the vocabulary. The SabreTech mechanics were undone by the gap between "expired" and "expended"; a tenant-screening story has its own terms that decide outcomes, and getting each one exactly right, and keeping it named the same way throughout, is part of the reporting. Where the record is confusing or contested, say so in plain words, the way Fink writes "Surprisingly, perhaps, there is no consensus." Showing a genuine uncertainty serves the reader better than smoothing it into a clean rule.

Give the people in the story the fairness Langewiesche gives the mechanics. A leasing agent, a property manager, or a landlord who ran an applicant through a vendor's screening product was usually following a routine that had worked before. The lesson lands harder when it locates the failure in how the system was built and operated than when it hunts for a single villain, and it can still say plainly where an operator was warned, knew better, and did nothing.

Anchor the figures the reader cannot scale. When a number appears, how many applications a score sorted, or how a rejected applicant compared with the cutoff, tie it to something the reader already holds, as Schulz sets the Cascadia rupture against the 2010 Haiti earthquake. Commit to the real quantity where the evidence supports it instead of reaching for "many" or "countless."

## William Langewiesche, "The Lessons of ValuJet 592"

Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/1998/03/the-lessons-of-valujet-592/306534/

> "By the time Walton Little felt the shock wave, everyone aboard was dead—two pilots, three flight attendants, and 105 passengers. Their remains lay in a shallow, watery crater filled with liquid mud and grass. All that marked the surface was a fractured engine, a few dead fish, some jet fuel, and a scattering of personal papers, clothes, and twisted pieces of aluminum—the stuff of tragedy."

The count is exact and the inventory is concrete, so the scale of the death registers without any word instructing the reader to feel it. Langewiesche is a working pilot writing about a crash, and the restraint is his choice: he names what was on the surface of the swamp and stops. "The stuff of tragedy" is the only editorial phrase, and it comes after the physical list, not in place of it.

> "The second kind of accident could be called "engineered." It consists of those surprising materials failures that should have been predicted by designers or discovered by test pilots but were not. Such failures at first defy understanding, but ultimately they yield to examination and result in tangible solutions. An American Eagle ATR turboprop dives into a frozen field in Roselawn, Indiana, because its de-icing boots did not protect its wings from freezing rain—and as a result new boots are designed, and the entire testing process undergoes review."

He names a category and then immediately spends one real crash on it, with the aircraft, the place, the cause, and the fix all in a single sentence. The reader learns what "engineered" means by watching it happen to a turboprop in Roselawn, Indiana, not from the definition alone. This is a writer teaching a taxonomy by worked example, which is what a lesson has to do.

> "This required a gang of hard-pressed mechanics to draw a verbal distinction between canisters that were "expired," meaning most of the ones they were removing, and canisters that were not "expended," meaning many of the same ones, loaded and ready to fire, on which they were expected to put nonexistent caps. Also involved were canisters that were expired and expended, and others that were not expired but were expended."

The precision sits in the vocabulary itself: he holds "expired" and "expended" apart and shows how one canister could be both, which is the confusion that put the fire in the hold. Langewiesche does not mock the mechanics for the tangle; he lays it out so the reader sees the words were the trap. The flat second sentence carries his judgment while stating only facts.

## Sheri Fink, "The Deadly Choices at Memorial"

Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/the-deadly-choices-at-memorial-826

> "The smell of death was overpowering the moment a relief worker cracked open one of the hospital chapel's wooden doors. Inside, more than a dozen bodies lay motionless on low cots and on the ground, shrouded in white sheets. Here, a wisp of gray hair peeked out. There, a knee was flung akimbo. A pallid hand reached across a blue gown."

The opening reports a horror in short, level sentences, each fixing one physical detail: the gray hair, the knee, the hand across the gown. Fink is both an investigative reporter and a physician, and the clinical eye shows in the decision to catalog the bodies exactly rather than reach for adjectives. The weight comes entirely from what is named, which is why it does not read as staged.

> "Today triage is used in accidents and disasters when the number of injured exceeds available resources. Surprisingly, perhaps, there is no consensus on how best to do this. Typically, medical workers try to divvy up care to achieve the greatest good for the greatest number of people."

She teaches a term the reader half-knows by stating plainly what it is for, and then admits that the field has not settled how to do it. "Surprisingly, perhaps, there is no consensus" is a reporter refusing to flatten a real disagreement into a tidy rule. The reader comes away holding both the concept and the fact that it is contested.

## Kathryn Schulz, "The Really Big One"

Source: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2015/07/20/the-really-big-one

> "When the next very big earthquake hits, the northwest edge of the continent, from California to Canada and the continental shelf to the Cascades, will drop by as much as six feet and rebound thirty to a hundred feet to the west—losing, within minutes, all the elevation and compression it has gained over centuries. Some of that shift will take place beneath the ocean, displacing a colossal quantity of seawater. (Watch what your fingertips do when you flatten your hand.)"

Schulz explains a geological mechanism in figures the reader can hold: six feet down, thirty to a hundred feet west, within minutes, after centuries. The parenthetical instruction to flatten a hand turns an abstraction into something the reader can do with their own body. She reaches for the concrete demonstration instead of an analogy that only sounds impressive.

> "In the Pacific Northwest, the area of impact will cover some hundred and forty thousand square miles, including Seattle, Tacoma, Portland, Eugene, Salem (the capital city of Oregon), Olympia (the capital of Washington), and some seven million people. When the next full-margin rupture happens, that region will suffer the worst natural disaster in the history of North America, outside of the 2010 Haiti earthquake, which killed upward of a hundred thousand people."

The scale is built from specifics: the square miles, the named cities, the seven million people, and then a comparison to a disaster the reader already has a number for. Schulz measures a figure too large to feel against the 2010 Haiti earthquake, which is how she makes it register without inflating it. She commits to real quantities where a weaker writer would say "unimaginable."
