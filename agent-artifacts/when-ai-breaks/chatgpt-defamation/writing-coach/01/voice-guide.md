# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/chatgpt-defamation (01)

## How this piece should sound

This is a lesson on the When AI Breaks desk about a chatbot that stated false,
defamatory facts about a named, living person, built around Mark Walters v.
OpenAI. The reader is smart and widely read and follows the news, but is not a
lawyer and not an engineer. The register that fits is the one Amy Howe holds in
her Supreme Court reporting and James Somers holds in "The Coming Software
Apocalypse": plain, exact, and calm about a serious thing. Somers writes about
failures that put people in danger without reaching for a single frightening
word, and the writing is the stronger for it.

The piece moves between three kinds of statement, and the reader should always
know which one they are reading: what happened, what someone alleged, and what a
court decided. Howe's account of Counterman keeps them apart with her verbs
alone. Counterman "argued" and "contended." The trial court "rejected that
argument." The appeals court "upheld." Where this lesson reports what OpenAI
said, what Walters claimed, and what the court ruled, that same discipline is
what keeps a claim from being read as a finding. White's note on the Palin
ruling shows the other half of it: when a court ruling is easy to overread,
saying plainly what it does not establish is part of reporting it accurately.

The lesson will reach legal machinery the reader does not carry, such as the
posture a court ruled on or the standard a public figure has to meet to win a
defamation claim. White's lawsplainer shows one way through. He puts each legal
step into the plain words a person would actually say, in quotation marks,
rather than handing over a definition. A term of art can enter as soon as the
lesson cannot proceed without it, explained just that plainly.

A real person, Mark Walters, was described in a false and damaging role, and the
writing can convey what that means without sensationalizing it. Howe reports the
harm to Coles Whalen in concrete facts and lets them stand: canceled shows, a
gun carried, a move across the country. Somers opens on the 911 outage through
one woman's night and the knife she picked up. Neither writer adds an adjective
the facts have not already earned. Where this lesson shows the effect of being
named falsely by a system many people trust, plain facts will carry it further
than heightened ones.

The harder half of the lesson explains why a chatbot produces a confident,
detailed, false biography of a person. Somers is the model for walking a general
reader through a technical failure: he follows the mechanism in order and in
concrete terms. He is also the model for stating what is and is not known. When
the NASA investigators had not found a software cause but could not rule one
out, he reported both in a single sentence rather than settling it. Where the
evidence about how these systems fail is itself partial or disputed, saying
exactly that is truer than a clean answer, and Somers shows it can still read
cleanly.

## Amy Howe, "Justices throw out Colorado man's stalking conviction in First Amendment dispute"

Source: https://www.scotusblog.com/2023/06/justices-throw-out-colorado-mans-stalking-conviction-in-first-amendment-dispute/

> "The Supreme Court on Tuesday threw out the conviction of Billy Raymond Counterman, a Colorado man who was sentenced to four-and-a-half years in prison for stalking based on his Facebook messages. By a vote of 7-2, the justices ruled that the state courts had applied the wrong test to determine whether Counterman's statements were "true threats," which are not protected by the First Amendment."

Howe states the outcome in the first two sentences: who, the sentence he was
serving, the vote, and the exact thing the court decided, which was that the
lower courts had used the wrong test. She does not call the ruling important or
dramatic, and the vote count and the precise question carry it without help. The
reporter is visible in the discipline of saying only what the court did.

> "When the messages became increasingly menacing – referring to having seen her in person and suggesting that she should die – Whalen's mental health declined. She canceled appearances, started to carry a gun, and eventually left Colorado for the east coast."

The harm to Whalen is real, and Howe reports it in plain facts: the messages,
the decline in her mental health, and the three things she did in response. No
adjective is doing work the facts have not already done. Howe is present in the
choice to let "canceled appearances, started to carry a gun, and eventually left
Colorado" stand on its own.

> "Prosecutors in Colorado charged Counterman with stalking. At trial, he argued that his messages to Whalen were not "true threats" because he didn't actually intend to harm Whalen; instead, he contended, they were speech protected by the First Amendment. The trial court rejected that argument and he was convicted and sentenced to four-and-a-half years in prison; a Colorado appeals court upheld his conviction in 2021."

Every claim here is pinned to whoever made it. Counterman "argued" and
"contended"; the trial court "rejected that argument"; the appeals court
"upheld." A reader can see at each point which statement is a party's position
and which is a court's decision. The care in those verbs is where Howe shows.

## Ken White, "Lawsplainer: Sarah Palin's Defamation Claim Against The New York Times Can Go To Trial"

Source: https://popehat.substack.com/p/lawsplainer-sarah-palins-defamation

> "A motion to dismiss (called a demurrer in some states) argues "judge, even if you take everything in the complaint is true, under the law plaintiff doesn't have a case." That happens at the start of a case, before discovery — before depositions, exchanges of documents, and so forth. By contrast, a motion for summary judgment typically happens after the parties have exchanged discovery and taken depositions. A motion for summary judgment argues "judge, even if you accept all of the other side's evidence as true, even if you believe all their witnesses, under the law they have no case.""

White explains two procedural steps by putting each side's argument into the
plain words a person would actually say, inside quotation marks, and then marks
when in a case each one happens. A reader who has never heard the phrase
"summary judgment" can follow it. White, a practicing lawyer writing for
non-lawyers, is visible in the choice to translate the step rather than define
it.

> "That's important because the media often describes summary judgment rulings misleadingly. A ruling like this one doesn't mean that Sarah Palin has a strong case. It means that she has evidence which, if a jury believed it, would be legally sufficient to win — but a jury may not believe it. A jury may see the same evidence a completely different way. A plaintiff prevailing on a summary judgment motion is no guarantee of victory at trial at all — but it does strongly compel the parties to consider settlement, because of the extreme expense and risks of trial."

White says what the ruling does not establish before anyone can overread it: a
win at this stage means a jury could credit the evidence, not that the plaintiff
is right, and not a verdict. He marks the limit of the finding in the same
breath as the finding. The lawyer correcting a common misreading is the person
on the page.

## James Somers, "The Coming Software Apocalypse"

Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/archive/2017/09/saving-the-world-from-code/540393/

> "There were six hours during the night of April 10, 2014, when the entire population of Washington State had no 911 service. People who called for help got a busy signal. One Seattle woman dialed 911 at least 37 times while a stranger was trying to break into her house. When he finally crawled into her living room through a window, she picked up a kitchen knife. The man fled."

Somers opens on a large failure through one person's night: the count of calls,
the 37 attempts, the knife, the man leaving. The stakes are as high as stakes
get, and he reaches for no high words to say so. Somers is visible in trusting
the concrete detail to carry weight that a summary could not.

> "The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration enlisted software experts from NASA to perform an intensive review of Toyota's code. After nearly 10 months, the NASA team hadn't found evidence that software was the cause—but said they couldn't prove it wasn't."

This is a finding stated without overclaiming. The NASA team did not find
evidence that software caused the problem, and Somers reports in the same
sentence that they also could not rule it out. He states the limit of what was
established rather than resolving it for the reader. The restraint is in that
second clause.

> "Barr's testimony made the case for the plaintiff, resulting in $3 million in damages for Bookout and her friend's family. According to The New York Times, it was the first of many similar cases against Toyota to bring to trial problems with the electronic throttle-control system, and the first time Toyota was found responsible by a jury for an accident involving unintended acceleration."

Somers gives the jury's award and the outcome in exact terms, and attributes the
wider context to its source with "According to The New York Times" instead of
asserting it in his own voice. He keeps what the jury did separate from what is
reported about the rest of the litigation. The habit of naming where a claim
comes from is visible throughout the piece.
