# Editorial review: when-ai-breaks/donotpay-ftc (editor/01)

## Skeptic

Thesis: DoNotPay sold an AI "robot lawyer" advertised as operating like a human
lawyer; the FTC found the company never tested that the service performed like a
lawyer and never retained attorneys, called the performance claims
unsubstantiated, and made the company pay $193,000 in monetary relief and notify
subscribers. The mechanism is that fluent legal prose is no evidence of legal
correctness, and the present-day close is that the same untested claim sells
across Operation AI Comply and ordinary product pages.

The claims it stands on, each tested by opening the cited source:

- **The marketing.** "World's first robot lawyer," "operated like a human
  lawyer," the "$200-billion-dollar legal industry" ambition, and the "Los
  Angeles Times" pull-quote that came from a high-schooler's op-ed on the
  paper's High School Insider user-generated platform. I opened the complaint
  PDF (s1) as the article prints the href; it downloaded as the real 3MB
  document, and the extracted text carries every one of these verbatim,
  including "user-generated content platform for young people." Held.
- **The core finding, held at its true size.** Complaint paragraph 20 extracted
  verbatim: "DoNotPay did not test whether the Service's law-related features
  operated like a human lawyer... has not employed attorneys and has not retained
  attorneys... to test the quality and accuracy." The article's core-finding
  blockquote matches word for word. The piece explicitly states the FTC did not
  rule any document legally wrong and found "something prior: the company had
  never checked." The one complaint gesture at bad output (para 21, "not fit for
  use") is an allegation of consumer complaints; the article does not cite it as
  a finding. Attribution size is correct. Held.
- **$193,000 as monetary relief, never a fine.** Decision and Order (s5) opened
  and extracted: the amount sits under the heading "III. Monetary Relief," routes
  to a consumer-redress fund with leftovers to the U.S. Treasury, and the article
  says outright the deception counts carry no civil penalty. Held.
- **Dates kept distinct.** September 2024 = the Operation AI Comply announcement
  of the proposed settlement (s4, confirmed). January 14, 2025 = the finalized
  Decision and Order (article's date matches the evidence record's
  primary-document date). The 5-0 finalization (s7, confirmed). The article does
  not conflate them. Held.
- **The courtroom earpiece episode, outside the FTC record.** The article states
  plainly it "never reached the Federal Trade Commission's file" and is
  "documented in contemporaneous reporting and Browder's own statements, not the
  Commission's findings." CBS (s2) and Fortune (s3) opened and confirmed: the
  Browder jail-threat quote is verbatim, and Fortune confirms the $1M offer, the
  AirPods, verbatim repetition, and the GPT-3 model. The two threads are kept
  apart exactly as the round's focus required. Held.
- **DoNotPay's response, in its own words, allegation as allegation.** ABA
  Journal (s8) opened; the spokesperson quote ("pleased to have worked
  constructively... without admitting liability"; "a few hundred customers... out
  of millions... services that have long been discontinued") is verbatim. The
  settlement is described as resolved without admitting or denying, and the
  mandated-notice blockquote (Attachment A, extracted from s5) carries "neither
  admits nor denies." Held.

Every citation href was opened as the article prints it. All eight resolve to
the source itself, including both FTC PDFs (the writer's flag that the FTC server
blocks automated fetches did not reproduce here: both PDFs downloaded as the real
documents). Source kinds audited: five primary (s1, s4, s5, s6, s7), three
secondary (s2, s3, s8); the earpiece episode is cited only to the two secondary
outlets, correct because it is outside the FTC record. Floor met.

Display text audited descriptor by descriptor. Headline "The FTC found DoNotPay
never tested its robot lawyer against a human one" is a fair compression of para
20 and holds at the finding's real size. The dek's "a claim the FTC says it never
backed with a single attorney or test" marks the FTC's position and matches the
no-attorney, no-test finding. Subheads each name a real step in the piece's own
nouns. No break found; no fix routed.

## Cut

A dedicated slop pass, then the edge pass, the cold-arrival pass, and the delete
test. Three sentences failed and were fixed directly:

- **Lecturing, body-voice-breaking opener.** "Set the marketing aside, because
  the underlying failure is not special to DoNotPay" opened the mechanism section
  with a second-person imperative. It is both the lecturing-opener tell
  (`spec/slop.md`) and a breach of the lesson template's rule that only the two
  bookends address the reader. Recast to the plain declarative "The underlying
  failure is not special to DoNotPay," which keeps the pivot and drops the
  address.
- **Self-grading signpost.** "This is where the FTC's finding is sharper than it
  first looks" announced significance without carrying a fact or reasoning step.
  Cut; the paragraph now opens on the concrete "The Commission did not rule that
  DoNotPay's documents were bad."
- **Unsupported attribution.** "what regulators have begun to call AI-washing"
  attributed the coinage to regulators, which the evidence record does not
  establish. Trimmed to "a clean example of AI-washing," which still defines the
  term in the same sentence via the colon clause.

The heading flagged by the review-brief, "Why a fluent legal document is not a
correct one," was judged against the negative-parallelism rule and stands: the
misconception it corrects — that the harm was defective output, that fluency
proves correctness — is real and named, and the section spends its full length
establishing it. That is the earned-contrast exception, not the invented-strawman
mold.

Edges checked against the recent-pattern notes. The dek is a single clause plus
an appositive, not the two-clause "and/but" default and none of the three banned
dek molds. The present-day close is titled "Where the untested claim is sold
now," varied from the desk's stamped "Where the same pattern lives today" and
written in the piece's own nouns. The four prose semicolons (pricing tiers, the
earpiece scene, the $200-billion/$193,000 contrast, "The technology varied; the
claim did not") are all parallel or antithesis constructions, tightly bound, none
a splice; left in place. The $200-billion-against-$193,000 juxtaposition is the
Dan Luu move of letting two real figures carry the judgment, and it is earned.

No borrowed clause from the voice-guide exemplars: the McKenzie separation (the
visible fluent letter versus the untested service underneath) and the Luu
question (what would count as evidence a service performs like a lawyer) are
applied as moves in the article's own words, not lifted phrasing. No prompt
leakage: the "product pages" and "AI that does X" framing reports the reader's
situation the commission describes, which is not a leak.

## Reader

Read straight through as the paper's declared reader: what I have that the
sources alone would not give me is the separation of the fluent document a
subscriber could see from the never-asked question of whether the service was
ever measured against a lawyer's work, used to hold the FTC's finding at its
narrow size and turned into a reusable test for the next "AI that does X" claim.
That synthesis is the article's own; the complaint owns the finding and the
hallucination lesson owns the mechanism, but neither owns their joining. It
survives, and it matches the draft-handoff original-work sentence. The prose sits
closer to the voice-guide exemplars than to a median summary. The headline, read
last as the largest claim, is true and sourced to paragraph 20.
