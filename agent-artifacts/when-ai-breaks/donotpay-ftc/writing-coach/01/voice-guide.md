# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/donotpay-ftc (01)

## How this piece should sound

This lesson narrates a documented enforcement case: a product sold for years as
"the world's first robot lawyer," the paying subscribers who bought AI-generated
legal letters and filings, and the Federal Trade Commission that found the
marketing had run ahead of anything the company tested. It is one lesson in a
running course for a reader who is sharp and widely read but has never worked
inside a language model, so the writing teaches while it narrates. The register
is plain and serious.

The hardest part of the body is explaining why a system like this fails the way
it did. The reader has to understand how a model produces fluent, confident
legal prose whose fluency does not establish that the law in it is right.
Patrick McKenzie builds a bank failure one plain sentence at a time, each step
earning the next. Where the DoNotPay mechanism can be built that way, the reader
will follow it; where it is compressed into a single abstract claim, the reader
will not. McKenzie's other habit may help as much: he pins a term to exactly
what it covers and will not let it drift. The FTC's finding is narrow and
specific, and the piece can hold it at that size rather than widen it into a
verdict on AI in general.

Somewhere the piece has to separate the fluent output a subscriber saw from the
question of whether the service was ever measured against a lawyer's work.
McKenzie's line about a bank run revealing an insolvency that was already there
shows the move: he names the event everyone watches, then the failure that was
underneath it the whole time. If the material supports that separation, stating
it plainly does more than stating it with force.

The judgment about the "robot lawyer" claim is only as strong as what the FTC
found and what DoNotPay did. Dan Luu lets revenue and market-cap figures carry an
unfashionable verdict about Ballmer; here the settlement terms, the amount, and
the specific claims the FTC called unsubstantiated are the particulars a judgment
can sit on. Luu's second move fits the mechanism directly. He asks what evidence
would actually settle a charge before he answers it, and the same question runs
under this case: what would count as showing that a service performs like a
lawyer, and whether anyone gathered it.

Some of what the piece reports is the FTC's allegation rather than an admitted
fact, and DoNotPay may have answered on the record. The ProPublica reporters
quote an official's justification in her own words and set it down for the reader
to weigh; where DoNotPay's response exists, the piece can do the same. They also
say plainly what their evidence does not prove before they land the point it does
support. That order is available here: mark what is alleged, say what is unknown
about how subscribers were affected, and let the accountable finding rest on what
the record carries. Give the figures the way they do, the count of subscribers or
the size of the payment, with the reach of each figure named rather than rounded
up.

The series closes on where the same weakness is sold now, which the reader meets
on ordinary product pages and across the rest of the FTC's Operation AI Comply
actions. That close is reporting. It works when it points at a specific, current
place the untested claim is being made, and it needs no closing line that grades
the lesson.

## Patrick McKenzie (patio11), "Deposit insurance"

Source: https://www.bitsaboutmoney.com/archive/deposit-insurance/

> Every insurance policy has a notion of "covered perils": it will pay out if
> and only if an event matching a limited description happens. Deposit insurance
> covers precisely one covered peril: the loss to depositors (1) caused by the
> failure (2) of an insured financial institution (3).

McKenzie defines a term by pinning it to exactly what it covers and nothing
more, and the numbered clauses show him refusing to let the definition drift.
The precision is the writing: the reader finishes the sentence knowing the one
thing the insurance pays for and, by implication, the many things it does not.
His turn of mind is visible in the choice to number the conditions rather than
round them into a phrase.

> Basically every effective process to grow loans introduces more correlation.
> You site your branches in places where you think they will get attractive
> business brought to them, and your loan book starts to concentrate in those
> neighborhoods. You hire loan officers who are good at getting deals done, and
> your loan book concentrates in the professional networks of those loan
> officers, of whom you probably have less than a few dozen.

He explains how a hidden risk accumulates by walking through the ordinary,
sensible decisions that produce it, one plain sentence at a time. Nothing is
asserted; each step earns the next, so the reader sees a loan book become
concentrated without anyone intending it to. McKenzie is visible in the patience
of the sequence and his refusal to jump to the conclusion.

> In most cases, if you're killed by a bank run, the damage was done long
> before. You earned your fate via years of diligent work making bad loans, and
> became insolvent. The bank run revealed the insolvency.

McKenzie separates the event everyone watches from the failure that had already
happened, in three short sentences. The blunt "You earned your fate" carries a
judgment the preceding explanation has earned the right to make. He is visible in
his willingness to state the unglamorous cause right after the dramatic one.

## Dan Luu, "Steve Ballmer was an underrated CEO"

Source: https://danluu.com/ballmer/

> When people point to a long list of failures like Bing, Zune, Windows Phone,
> and HoloLens as evidence that Ballmer was some kind of buffoon who was holding
> Microsoft back, this demonstrates a lack of understanding of the tech
> industry. This is like pointing to a list of failed companies a VC has funded
> as evidence the VC doesn't know what they're doing. But that's silly in a hits
> based industry like venture capital. If you want to claim the VC is bad, you
> need to point out poor total return or a lack of big successes, which would
> imply poor total return.

Luu takes a common charge and asks what evidence would actually support it, then
shows that the charge does not meet its own test. The venture-capital comparison
is one a general reader already holds, and it does the argument's work instead of
decorating it. Luu is visible in the move of fixing the right measure first and
answering the charge against that measure.

> Even Bing, widely considered a failure, on last reported revenue and current
> P/E ratio, would be 12th most valuable tech company in the world, between
> Tencent and ASML. When attacking Ballmer, people cite Bing as a failure that
> occurred on Ballmer's watch, which tells you something about the degree of
> success Ballmer had. Most companies would love to have their successes be as
> successful as Bing, let alone their failures.

Luu lands an unfashionable judgment by setting a concrete number beside it, and
the number is placed so the reader can size it without prior knowledge. He turns
the very thing cited as a failure into evidence of the opposite, and lets the
figure rather than an adjective carry the reversal. The dry last sentence shows
his preference for understatement over emphasis.

## Debbie Cenziper, Megan Rose, Brandon Roberts and Irena Hwang (ProPublica), "The Secret Gamble at the FDA That Exposed Americans to Risky Drugs"

Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/fda-drug-loophole-sun-pharma

> The reports cite about 70 hospitalizations and nine deaths. And those numbers
> are conservative. ProPublica limited its count to reports that linked problems
> to a single drug. However, the total number of complaints to the FDA that
> mention exempted drugs is in the thousands.

The reporters give the figures and then state exactly how far the figures reach,
marking what they counted and what they set aside. Naming the limit of a number
is what makes the number worth trusting. The care shows in the sentence that
calls their own count conservative and then explains why.

> In the hands of consumers, according to the FDA's longtime head of drug
> safety, the information would have caused "some kind of frenzy."
>
> "We felt we didn't have to make it a public thing," said Janet Woodcock, who
> spent nearly four decades at the agency.

The reporters let the official explain her decision in her own words and set the
quotation down without arguing with it. The plain attribution and the direct
quote leave the reader to weigh the justification. The restraint is the craft:
the passage reports what was said and trusts the reader to judge it.

> The outcomes described in the complaints may have no connection to the drug or
> could be unexpected side effects. In some cases, the FDA received complaints
> about the same drugs made by other manufacturers.
>
> Still, the seriousness of the reports involving exempted drugs did not
> galvanize the agency to investigate, leaving the public and the government with
> no way of knowing whether people were being harmed and, if so, how many.

The reporters say plainly what the complaints do not prove before they draw any
conclusion, and only then land the point the record does support. Keeping that
line, between what is shown and what is only alleged, is what lets the closing
judgment stand. The discipline is visible in the order: the caveat comes first,
the accountability second.
