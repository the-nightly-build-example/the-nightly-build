# Evidence: when-ai-breaks/arup-deepfake-fraud (01)

The record firmly supports the core incident. In end-January 2024 the Hong Kong
Police Force received a report that a finance-department employee at a
multinational firm had authorised transfers totalling about HK$200 million
(about US$25.6 million) after a deepfake "video conference" that impersonated the
firm's UK-based chief financial officer and other colleagues; the loss went out
in 15 transfers to five local Hong Kong bank accounts; the police report was
lodged on 29 January 2024; police described the case at a briefing in early
February 2024 through Acting Senior Superintendent Baron Chan Shun-ching; and in
May 2024 Arup confirmed to CNN that it was the target and that "fake voices and
images were used." Each figure is verified against a source that owns it.

The record is thin, and in one respect contradicts the commission, on whether
this was an *interactive, real-time* deepfake. The authoritative primary — the
Hong Kong government's own Legislative Council reply — states the video
conference was **pre-recorded**, that "there was actually no interaction between
the informant and the fraudster," and that the pressure to pay was applied
afterward over instant messaging. The commission's framing ("interactive,
multi-party video," "a live video image as proof of identity") describes the
victim's experience of watching what looked like a live meeting, but the police
account is that the deepfakes did not converse in real time. The general
capability the lesson wants to teach — real-time face and voice deepfakes cheap
and convincing enough to defeat a human "I saw them on the call" check — is real
and separately documented, but this specific incident is not clean proof of it.
That is the record's most important limitation and it is developed in
Contradictions below.

## Contradictions

- **Interactive/real-time vs. pre-recorded — the load-bearing one.** The
  commission frames the incident as "interactive, multi-party video used in
  enterprise fraud" and says "the failure was trusting a live video image as
  proof of identity." The Hong Kong government's own record (LCQ9, primary) and
  the police briefing as carried by AFP say the opposite about the mechanism: the
  video conference was **pre-recorded**, "there was actually no interaction
  between the informant and the fraudster," and the fraudster then "continued
  with the payment transfer instructions using instant messaging software." So
  the victim was fooled by watching convincing pre-recorded fakes of colleagues
  in a meeting he joined live, not by conversing with real-time deepfakes. The
  lesson's human-check point still holds ("everyone I saw and heard looked like
  my colleagues"), but two commission phrasings are not supported by the police
  account: that the deepfakes were *interactive*, and that a *live* video image
  was the proof of identity. The writer should say the meeting the victim joined
  was live but the deepfake participants were pre-recorded and non-interactive,
  and that the actual instructions to pay came afterward by message.

- **What the police confirmed vs. what is only reported.** Confirmed by police /
  Hong Kong government: the HK$200M loss, five bank accounts, 15 transfers, the
  UK-CFO impersonation, the pre-recorded non-interactive method, the 29 Jan
  report. Confirmed by Arup: that it was the target, that "fake voices and images
  were used," ~$25M, one employee, systems not compromised. Not established by
  either: exactly how the deepfake video/audio was produced beyond "downloaded
  online public video clips and voices" (no tool, cost, or real-time-rendering
  detail is on the record); the exact date of the police briefing (CNN says a
  Friday briefing, i.e. ~2 Feb; HKFP says police spoke "Sunday," 4 Feb — the
  briefing and its reporting span 2-4 Feb 2024); and whether the audio was
  simulated live (one RTHK-derived summary said voices were emulated "in real
  time" while video was pre-recorded, which sits awkwardly against LCQ9's flat
  "no interaction" — treat LCQ9 as authoritative and describe the meeting as
  pre-recorded and non-interactive).

- **One employee vs. "employees."** SCMP's lede says "employees at its Hong Kong
  branch were fooled... with one incident involving" the fake CFO, which could be
  read as multiple victims making up the HK$200M. This is resolved by two
  primaries: Arup ("one of its Hong Kong employees") and LCQ9 ("one fraud case,"
  "the informant," singular). Use the single-employee version; it is the
  primary-sourced one.

- **USD figure.** HK$200 million converts to about US$25.6 million (SCMP, CNN,
  RTHK, and the round figure Arup gives as "$25 million"); AFP/HKFP round it to
  "US$26 million." HK$200M is the hard number; give the USD as ~US$25.6M and note
  Arup's own "about $25 million."

- **PNAS scope.** Nightingale & Farid tested *static* AI-generated face images,
  not real-time video or voice. It grounds "a synthetic face can be more
  convincing than a real one," not "real-time video-call deepfakes are
  indistinguishable." Do not stretch it past still faces; pair it with the NSA
  and Europol material for the video/voice and real-time claims.
