# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/ai-overviews (01)

## Directive

Write this as a reconstruction, not an explainer. The house voice is plain
exposition; this article earns its keep by telling the incident in order
first, then turning that order into the lesson. Keep the Yglesias-plain
register the paper already sets. What changes here is shape: lead the reader
through a dated sequence of what a real system did, then let the "why" arrive
as the question that sequence forces.

Three moves will change sentences in this piece.

Tell it forward. The default lesson would state that retrieval has no trust
model and then illustrate it. Invert that. Put the reader at the launch, then
at the glue answer, then at the postmortem, in that order, each beat carrying a
name or a date. The mechanism is what the reader is ready to hear only after
they have watched it happen.

Report the absurd flat. The material is inherently funny: a search engine
telling people to put glue on pizza and eat a rock a day. The comedy is in the
fact, not in the writing. State it deadpan, at its plainest, and do not nudge
the reader toward the laugh. A wink here would break the serious register and,
worse, would let the writer off the hook of explaining why a real system did
this. The exemplars below describe horrors and farces in the same level voice;
match that control.

Pivot on the reader's own question. Do not announce the mechanism section. Move
into it on the question the story just raised: how did a real Reddit joke end up
stated as fact at the top of Google. Answer it with the machinery the course
has already taught, linked, not re-taught.

## Licenses

form: ordered narrative reconstruction
move: Somers opens inside the 911 outage and walks it forward before naming a
  cause; Cloudberg runs the event chronologically, then pivots to findings.
  Deploy it to carry the reader from launch through the failure to the
  postmortem in sequence, each beat anchored to a person, a company, or a date.
bar:  every narrative sentence advances the timeline and rests on the record
  (a confirmed artifact, a dated statement, a named actor). No reconstructed
  scene, no invented interiority, nothing that traces only to a faked
  screenshot.

form: deadpan statement of an absurd fact
move: the exemplars report grim or ludicrous particulars at the same even pitch
  as everything else and trust the fact to land. The restraint is the craft.
bar:  the sentence states the verified fact plainly and adds no editorializing
  adjective, no aside, no signal that the writer finds it funny. If deleting one
  word would make it drier and it still lands, that word was a nudge.

form: the investigative hinge question
move: Cloudberg turns from "what happened" to "why the system failed" by
  raising the question the narrative has made unavoidable, so the pivot feels
  earned rather than imposed. Deploy it once, at the seam between the incident
  and the mechanism.
bar:  the question names something the story has already shown the reader, and
  the paragraphs after it answer it directly. Not rhetorical, not a section
  label in disguise, and used once.

## Recently used, do not reuse

- The single-declarative dek naming a number and an actor
  (google-flu-trends: "overshot the CDC for 100 of 108 weeks"; epic-sepsis:
  "missed two-thirds of the patients it was built to catch"). Match that
  concrete register, but the line must be built from this incident's own figure
  or fact, not the same subject-verb-number cast reworn.
- Section headings that join two clauses with a comma and "and." The recent
  desk cadence leans on it; vary the shape here so the headings do not read
  stamped.
- Do not open by retelling the air-canada-chatbot or mata-v-avianca incidents to
  set up a contrast. Draw the hallucination-versus-retrieval distinction in a
  line and link them; they are a boundary, not a runway.

## James Somers, "The Coming Software Apocalypse"
Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/archive/2017/09/saving-the-world-from-code/540393/
Craft:
- cadence: short declaratives at the point of dread ("People who called for help
  got a busy signal"), lengthening into explanation once the stakes are set.
- argument: the concrete failure is a specimen of a general one; the reader
  meets the instance before the thesis.
- evidence: exact coordinates do the work of credibility. A date, a state, a
  town in Colorado, a named company, one un-named woman.
- stance: intelligent-layman address, neither alarmist nor knowing. He explains
  the counter and the threshold as plain facts, not revelations.
- notice: he lets one human detail (37 calls, a kitchen knife) make the systemic
  failure personal without milking it.
- diction: everyday words for technical objects ("counter," "threshold"); the
  code is demystified, never dressed up.
- reader: assumes curiosity, not expertise; earns each technical step off the
  narrative that needed it.
- the move the axes miss: he reveals the cause as an anticlimax. The programmers
  picked a number in the millions and the calls exceeded it. Stating the
  mechanism flatly, without drama, is what makes it frightening.

## Admiral Cloudberg (Kyra Dempsey), "System of Denial: The crash of Comair flight 3272"
Source: https://admiralcloudberg.medium.com/system-of-denial-the-crash-of-comair-flight-3272-8d2e2ad94fb4
Craft:
- cadence: clipped sentences through the event, longer analytical passages
  after it; pacing is controlled, never rushed even at the crash.
- argument: an accident is a chain, not a mistake; every piece "was already
  known to someone, somewhere," and the failure was assembling them too late.
- evidence: precise actors and dates (the manufacturer, the regulator, a draft
  report in one month, a revision distributed the next) build accountability.
- stance: forensic and calm, analytical even at the worst moment. No
  sensationalism; the gravity comes from precision.
- notice: she scaffolds a concept just before the moment it explains, so the
  reader holds the idea exactly when the narrative needs it.
- diction: defines the term of art first (the stall angle), then shows what
  disrupted it; plain comparisons ("sandpaper-like") over jargon.
- reader: general reader treated as capable of the real mechanism if it is built
  up in order.
- the move the axes miss: she transitions from what happened to why through an
  investigative question, making the pivot feel like the reader's own next
  thought rather than a lecture's next heading.

## William Langewiesche, "The Human Factor" (Air France 447)
Source: https://www.vanityfair.com/news/business/2014/09/air-france-flight-447-crash
Craft:
- cadence: patient sequential reconstruction; he holds the reader in the cockpit
  minute by minute as the situation degrades.
- argument: the disaster traces not to one fault but to a system that flew
  itself until the humans could not; automation removed the skill it depended
  on. (Read as craft, not as a thesis to import: this article's failure is a
  trust gap in retrieval, not deskilling.)
- evidence: named crew (Bonin), the physical trigger (ice in the airspeed
  sensors), the exact machine response (autopilot disengages, the fly-by-wire
  mode downgrades) told as plain cause and effect.
- stance: even and unhurried; he explains the stall and the misread inputs
  without contempt for the pilots, letting the mechanism carry the judgment.
- notice: he explains why an action that seems mysterious (pulling the nose up)
  followed from what the crew could and could not perceive.
- diction: aviation terms introduced in plain language at the moment they act,
  not front-loaded.
- reader: a magazine reader with no cockpit knowledge, walked through a technical
  chain step by step.
- the move the axes miss: he keeps narration and mechanism braided, explaining
  each system as it fails rather than saving the analysis for the end, so the
  reader understands the cause in the same beat they watch it bite.
