# Editorial review: when-ai-breaks/ai-overviews (editor/01)

## Skeptic

Thesis: an AI answer built by retrieving the web is only as trustworthy as the
page it happened to match, because the part of the system that fetches a page
cannot judge it. The load-bearing claims:

1. AI Overviews launched to everyone in the US on May 14, 2024, reaching
   hundreds of millions that week (s1). Confirmed against the launch
   announcement.
2. The glue answer reproduced a real eleven-year-old Reddit joke word for word
   (s2, s3). Confirmed. The exact quantity (⅛ cup, non-toxic, tackiness) matches
   the Daily Dot's reporting of u/fucksmith's comment.
3. The rocks answer surfaced Onion satire as a data void, via a copy republished
   on a geological-software site, not a direct theonion.com retrieval (s4). The
   piece states this correctly: paragraph one names the Onion origin of the
   content, paragraph two supplies Reid's data-void mechanism and the republished
   copy. It does not claim a direct Onion pull.
4. Google's counter-account (Reid: misinterpreting language / not enough good
   information; Pichai: hallucination is inherent) is real and on record (s6, s7,
   s8). Confirmed.

I pushed hardest on the claim I most wanted to keep, the dek's "had read its
source correctly." For glue it is decisive (verbatim reproduction). For rocks it
holds at the level of restatement, and the body is honest that Google reads the
rocks case as a data void rather than pure faithful retrieval. The piece does not
assert the retrieval thesis unopposed: it states Google's position at full
strength in the position card, grants the rocks/data-void case to Google, then
shows the glue case defeating the misinterpretation reading because comprehension
there was perfect. That weighing is the article's original work and it survives
the break attempt.

Display text, descriptor by descriptor. Headline is a defended claim (the piece
proves the glue joke was restated as fact). Dek carries concrete facts (eat a
rock a day; Reddit troll; The Onion) and an actor (Google's AI); it is not the
banned semicolon-reversal, suspended-question, or three-clause comma-triad mold,
and its shape differs from the recent single-declarative-with-number deks, which
is what the recent-pattern note wants. "Reddit troll" is not an unsupported
editorialization: the Daily Dot source itself frames the author as a "Reddit
troll," so the label rests on the record. Dates verified against the evidence and
the live sources: launch May 14, 2024; Daily Dot May 23; NBC "the next day" (May
24); postmortem May 30; Onion 2021. Reid's title in body prose ("the vice
president who runs Search") matches the evidence record's "VP, Search"; no title
appears in display text. The postmortem's stated title "AI Overviews: About last
week" is correct: the page's SEO title has since changed, but "About last week"
still appears throughout the live article, along with "data void," "How many
rocks," "geological software," "never appeared," and "less than one in [7
million]," so every postmortem-sourced claim resolves on the page.

Source-kind audit. s1 primary (Google launch, Google owns the claim); s2
secondary (Daily Dot original reporting); s3 secondary (NBC); s4 primary (Google
postmortem); s5 primary (the Onion artifact itself); s6 secondary (AP via PBS);
s7 primary (Pichai's own on-record words in the Verge transcript, per the
evidence record's authorship-not-domain rule); s8 secondary (CBS). All correct.
No citation points to a Reddit URL for the glue joke; its existence is attributed
to the secondary reporting and Google's acknowledgment, as required. The
Verge/Pichai page is cited at its own address in both the source list and the Go
deeper band, not swapped for a secondary. I opened all eight hrefs: each returns
200 at its own address and the ones I title-checked (Onion, AP/PBS, blog.google
postmortem, Daily Dot, NBC) land on the correct article.

Faked-screenshot boundary. The discarded fabrications (dogs in hot cars, smoking
in pregnancy, depression / Golden Gate Bridge) appear only inside Reid's "never
appeared" line, framed as fabrications, never presented as real Overviews. That
is the correct and intended use. Only glue and rocks are used as real examples.

Distinction from the desk's prior incidents. Air Canada's chatbot and the
mata-v-avianca brief are drawn as a boundary in a single mechanism paragraph with
links, not retold and not used as an opening runway. Correct per the voice guide.

## Cut

The prose is disciplined and the deadpan register holds; the absurd facts are
stated flat and trusted to land. Two cuts made.

The worst tell was in the Why-this-matters bookend: "This lesson walks through
what happened, in order, and then answers the question the sequence raises:" —
method narration and a signpost describing where the piece will go. I cut it and
let the lesson's question stand on its own, which the template licenses; the
bookend still poses the question the takeaway resolves.

The second was a mild repeated formula: "the course has already covered" appeared
twice. In the orientation the inline link to the retrieval lesson already carries
the taught-earlier signal, so I cut the phrase there ("That design has a name:
retrieval-augmented generation..."), leaving the single remaining use in the
mechanism section.

Checked but left standing: the scale sentence ("even a rate that low is a large
count of bad answers, and the disagreement is really about which figure to hold
in view...") runs long but stays in control and its clauses are correctly joined;
it is a weighed synthesis, not a run-on to break. "The most-used page on the
internet" recurs twice but reads as deliberate scale-setting on common ground,
not a formula. The furniture earns its place: the data-void note defines the term
the mechanism turns on, and the position card states Google's counter-account
once at full strength. No Verdict-style restatement block is present; judgment
lands in the takeaway bookend, as the press requires.

## Reader

Read straight through as the paper's declared reader, I come away with something
the sources alone do not hand me: the reason two "AI got it wrong" stories are the
opposite of the hallucination everyone assumed, and a way to tell faithful
retrieval of a bad source apart from a model making things up. The postmortem
supplies the data void, the reporting supplies the verbatim joke, and Pichai
supplies the hallucination framing, but none of them performs the weighing that
shows the glue case defeating that framing. The draft-handoff's original-work
sentence claims exactly this, and the article delivers it. The prose sits closer
to the voice-guide exemplars (Somers, Cloudberg) than to a median summary: dated
forward narration, flat statement of the absurd, one earned hinge question at the
seam. The headline, read as the largest claim, is one the body proves.
