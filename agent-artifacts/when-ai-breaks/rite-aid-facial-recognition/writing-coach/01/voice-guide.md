# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/rite-aid-facial-recognition

## How this piece should sound

This lesson tells one real incident, Rite Aid's facial-recognition surveillance and the Federal Trade Commission's 2023 action, and teaches why a system built that way produced the harm it did. The reader is quick and widely read and has never worked in a codebase. Hold a sober register: report what the program did to shoppers, name the people, the companies, and the dates, and keep the FTC's allegations apart from what Rite Aid admits and what the settlement actually resolves. The numbers matter most here, and they have to land in plain counts, not in the language of a paper.

The three writers below all report a failure that cost people something and teach the mechanism underneath it, which is this lesson's own task. None of them writes about facial recognition, so study their handling and set the cases aside.

Campbell teaches the stick shaker and the angle-of-attack problem inside the story, a clause at a time, without stopping to lecture. The lesson's own terms can enter the same way when the material reaches them: a watchlist, a false match, a base rate can each be defined in the sentence that first uses it and then left to work.

When the lesson builds the reason the system failed, Campbell's chain from where the engines sit to lift to handling shows a mechanism assembled one step at a time, each step resting on the one before. The lesson has its own chain to build for the false accusations. Assembling it the same way, so the reader arrives at the mechanism rather than being handed it, is what the passage models.

Joyce carries a statistical argument in counts a reader can hold, attributes every figure to whoever measured it, and gives ranges where the data is thin ("There isn't enough data to be more precise"). This lesson runs on measured error rates and match counts, some contested and some never made public. Where a figure is a range, or where the match logs that would settle a dispute were never released, Joyce's plainness about what the numbers do and do not support is the model.

The FTC's charges and Rite Aid's answer are a contested account, and the lesson need not referee it inside the telling. Kirchner and Goldstein give the trade group's denial in its spokesman's own words and report, in the same breath, what he conceded; Joyce declines to impute motive to the prosecution while still showing where its numbers went wrong. The strongest version of each side, attributed and held apart, leaves the reader able to weigh it.

The record here includes people who were followed, stopped, searched, and put out of stores. Joyce reports two infants' deaths without one word reaching for feeling, Campbell narrates a fatal flight minute by minute, and Kirchner and Goldstein let a list of alleged crimes stand before revealing it was one wrong report. Reported plainly, without pressing for effect, those facts suit this register.

Where the record hands over a line that does the work, use it. The landlord's "I assume they have matched everything up" and Boeing's own bulletin carry more than a summary would. The FTC's complaint and Rite Aid's statement have lines like that in them.

## Darryl Campbell, "The many human errors that brought down the Boeing 737 Max"

Source: https://www.theverge.com/2019/5/2/18518176/boeing-737-max-crash-problems-human-error-mcas-faa

> Inside the cockpit of PK-LQP, a brand-new Boeing 737 Max belonging to Lion Air, the stick shaker on the captain's side began to vibrate. Stick shakers are designed to warn pilots of an impending stall, which can cause a dangerous loss of control. They're unmistakably loud for that reason.

Campbell introduces the stick shaker and defines it in the same breath he first uses it, so a reader who has never sat in a cockpit stays inside the scene. The definition is one sentence and does not halt the account. You can see the reporter deciding, term by term, exactly how much a newcomer needs before the next sentence.

> Due to their size and position, the engines on the Max create lift when the airplane enters a steep climb (or, in aviation parlance, at high angles of attack). This extra lift causes the Max to handle differently than previous versions of the 737, but only when it's climbing steeply.

This is a mechanism built one step at a time: where the engines sit, what that does to lift, what the lift does to handling, and the exact condition under which it happens. Campbell names the term the field uses, "high angles of attack," and translates it in the same clause. No step asks the reader to hold an idea an earlier sentence has not already supplied.

> Boeing, meanwhile, defended its previous silence about MCAS.
>
> "Since it operates in situations where the aircraft is under relatively high g load and near stall, a pilot should never see the operation of MCAS," read a Q&A distributed to Southwest Airlines.

Campbell gives Boeing's justification in Boeing's own words and sources it to a specific document, rather than characterizing it. He reports the company's reasoning here without ruling on it, and the quoted document stands on its own. The reader hears the defense first-hand and is left to judge it.

## Helen Joyce, "Beyond reasonable doubt"

Source: https://plus.maths.org/content/beyond-reasonable-doubt

> Five years ago, a young couple from Cheshire suffered one of the most devastating losses imaginable - their baby Christopher died in his sleep, aged 11 weeks. Doctors, neighbours, all were sympathetic, and the death was certified as natural causes - there was evidence of a respiratory infection, and no sign of any failure of care.

The material is about as painful as material gets, and Joyce reports it plainly, with dates and ages and the medical finding, and no adjective reaching for the reader's feelings. The facts carry the weight because she trusts them to. You can see a writer who has decided that plain reporting is the right treatment for the hardest possible subject.

> Ray Hill, a Professor in the Mathematics Department at Salford University, has carried out a detailed analysis of the data in CESDI, and come to the conclusion that such deaths are most certainly not independent. He estimates that siblings of children who die of cot death are between 10 and 22 times more likely than average to die the same way. Using the figure of 1 in 1,303 for the chance of a first cot death, we see that the chances of a second cot death in the same family are somewhere between 1 in 60 and 1 in 130. There isn't enough data to be more precise, or to take familial factors into account, but it seems reasonable to use a ballpark figure of 1 in 100.

Joyce walks the reader through a real calculation in plain counts, attributes each figure to who produced it, and gives ranges instead of false precision. When the data runs out she says so, and then commits to a working figure anyway. The person shows in that honesty about the limits of the numbers, held together with a willingness to still reach a usable answer.

> Far more importantly, if they wanted to take into account the factors that made cot death less likely in a family like the Clarks, they should also have taken into account the factors making murder less likely in such a family. Home Office figures suggest fewer than 30 mothers a year kill their own child. And in most of these cases the children have been poorly cared for, Social Services have had files on the family, the family is poor, only one parent is on the scene, and so on. No such factor was present in this case.

Joyce weighs the figures on both sides of the question, setting how rare the innocent explanation is against how rare the guilty one is, with numbers for each. The reasoning is even-handed and shown in full, so the reader can follow every step rather than take her word for the result. This is analysis that stays tied to cited figures.

## Lauren Kirchner and Matthew Goldstein, "Access Denied: Faulty Automated Background Checks Freeze Out Renters"

Source: https://themarkup.org/locked-out/2020/05/28/access-denied-faulty-automated-background-checks-freeze-out-renters

> Burglary and domestic assault in Minnesota. Selling meth and jumping bail in Kentucky. Driving without insurance in Arkansas. Disorderly conduct. Theft. Lying to a police officer. Unspecified "crimes." Too many narcotics charges to count.

The piece opens with a wall of alleged offenses in clipped fragments, then reveals in the next lines that this is a single automated background check on one apartment applicant, and that "none of the charges were hers." The withholding does the work: the reader feels the weight of the record before learning it is wrong. The reporters trust a plain list to set up the reveal, with no commentary telling the reader what to make of it.

> Noting the millions of tenant background reports produced each year, the group denied that any systemic problems existed and accused consumer lawyers of being myopic.
>
> "If I sat in a cardiologist's office all day, all I would see is people with heart problems," said Eric Ellman, the association's senior vice president for public policy and legal affairs. He acknowledged that it hadn't developed any standards for screening accuracy but said the companies had their own policies.

The industry's trade group gets to deny the problem in its representative's own words, named and titled, including the analogy he chose. Then, in the same paragraph, the reporters record what he conceded, that the group had set no accuracy standards. The contested account is laid out fairly and in full, and the concession sits next to the denial rather than being argued against.

> Steven Schachtman, a longtime Minneapolis landlord and property manager who oversees about 10,000 rentals and uses screening firms, said it's difficult for a landlord to check the reports to ensure they matched their applicants.
>
> "That is why we are hiring them," he said. "I assume they have matched everything up."

One quoted line, "I assume they have matched everything up," carries the failure of oversight further than a paragraph of explanation would. Kirchner and Goldstein found the person whose plain words show that no one in the chain is checking, and let him say it. The reporting is in the selection: they went and got the sentence that does the work.
