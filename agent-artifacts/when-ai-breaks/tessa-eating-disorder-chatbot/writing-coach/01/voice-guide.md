# Voice guide: Tessa eating-disorder chatbot (lesson)

## How this piece should sound

This lesson tells what a support chatbot did to people who came to it for help
with an eating disorder, and it explains it for a reader who is new to how a
tool like Tessa ends up in that role. The register is calm and exact. The facts
of what happened are grave on their own, and the prose does not need to amplify
them. When the harm is described, the plainer the sentence, the more a reader
believes it. Angwin's team reports the disparity in COMPAS scores as "almost
twice the rate" and stops there; an intensifying adjective laid on top of a fact
like that only makes a reader trust the reporting less.

Handle what Tessa told its users the way Nina Martin and Renee Montagne handle a
hospital's missteps: as a sequence, each step attributed to where it is known
from. Say what NEDA said and when, what Sharon Maxwell documented and when, what
the vendor said, and keep each claim on the record it came from. The advice at
issue is dangerous to this population, so the piece can establish that it was
harmful and who showed it was, without printing the instructions themselves. The
lesson needs the fact of the harm; it does not need the content of the advice.

The cause is partly contested, and that is where the Markup's plainness about its
own limits is worth studying. "The formulas are closely held secrets," stated
flat, with no guess pasted over the gap. Where the record settles what changed in
Tessa, whether a script, an added generative component, or a tool pushed outside
the job it was built for, the piece can say so. Where the record does not settle
it, naming what is unknown and what evidence would decide it is stronger than
filling the space.

Give the parties their particulars, and early, the way the Markup names the
McDaniels family and their house before any percentage: the researchers who built
Tessa, the vendor who ran it, the helpline workers whose jobs were ending, the
people who reached the bot instead of a person. These are real parties with names
and dates, and naming them plainly is both more accurate and more humane than a
category. Whatever the piece concludes about how Tessa came to be giving that
advice, it should arrive through the facts in the order they happened, with
nothing in the prose reaching for effect ahead of them.

## Julia Angwin, Jeff Larson, Surya Mattu and Lauren Kirchner, "Machine Bias"

Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing

> "On a spring afternoon in 2014, Brisha Borden was running late to pick up her god-sister from school when she spotted an unlocked kid's blue Huffy bicycle and a silver Razor scooter. Borden and a friend grabbed the bike and scooter and tried to ride them down the street in the Fort Lauderdale suburb of Coral Springs.
>
> Just as the 18-year-old girls were realizing they were too big for the tiny conveyances — which belonged to a 6-year-old boy — a woman came running after them saying, "That's my kid's stuff." Borden and her friend immediately dropped the bike and scooter and walked away.
>
> But it was too late — a neighbor who witnessed the heist had already called the police. Borden and her friend were arrested and charged with burglary and petty theft for the items, which were valued at a total of $80."

The writing opens on the small facts of a single arrest: the blue Huffy, the
6-year-old's scooter, the $80 total. Angwin and her co-authors are reporting a
case the algorithm will later score wrongly, but here they hold that back and
walk through what happened in order. The specific objects and the exact figure
carry the point a summary would have lost.

> "We also turned up significant racial disparities, just as Holder feared. In forecasting who would re-offend, the algorithm made mistakes with black and white defendants at roughly the same rate but in very different ways.
>
> The formula was particularly likely to falsely flag black defendants as future criminals, wrongly labeling them this way at almost twice the rate as white defendants."

This is the study's central finding, and the writers give it as a measured
comparison rather than an accusation: the same overall error rate, different in
kind, the disparity stated as "almost twice the rate." They name what they found
and attach a number to it, and they leave out the adjective that would have told
the reader to be angry. The reader gets there from the figure.

## Emmanuel Martinez and Lauren Kirchner, "The Secret Bias Hidden in Mortgage-Approval Algorithms"

Source: https://themarkup.org/denied/2021/08/25/the-secret-bias-hidden-in-mortgage-approval-algorithms

> "The new four-bedroom house in Charlotte, N.C., was Crystal Marie and Eskias McDaniels's personal American dream, the reason they had moved to this Southern town from pricey Los Angeles a few years ago. A lush, long lawn, 2,700 square feet of living space, a neighborhood pool and playground for their son, Nazret. All for $375,000."

Martinez and Kirchner introduce the people harmed before any statistic, with
plain particulars: the square footage, the son's name, the price. Nothing in the
sentence strains for sympathy, and the reader knows exactly whose house is at
stake. It sets a data investigation on one household before the numbers arrive.

> "Holding 17 different factors steady in a complex statistical analysis of more than two million conventional mortgage applications for home purchases, we found that lenders were 40 percent more likely to turn down Latino applicants for loans, 50 percent more likely to deny Asian/Pacific Islander applicants, and 70 percent more likely to deny Native American applicants than similar White applicants. Lenders were 80 percent more likely to reject Black applicants than similar White applicants."

The finding arrives with its method attached, 17 factors held steady across two
million applications, so the reader can see what was controlled for before
reaching the percentages. Each group gets its own figure instead of one rounded
headline number. The writers commit to exact claims and show how they got them.

> "No one outside Fannie and Freddie knows exactly how the factors in their underwriting software are used or weighted; the formulas are closely held secrets."

Facing a system they cannot see inside, the writers say so plainly rather than
guess at the mechanism. The sentence marks the limit of what the reporting can
establish, in one line, without hedging or apology. Naming the unknown is part of
why the surrounding claims hold up.

## Nina Martin and Renee Montagne, "The Last Person You'd Expect to Die in Childbirth"

Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/die-in-childbirth-maternal-death-rate-health-care-system

> "As a neonatal intensive care nurse, Lauren Bloomstein had been taking care of other people's babies for years. Finally, at 33, she was expecting one of her own. The prospect of becoming a mother made her giddy, her husband Larry recalled recently — "the happiest and most alive I'd ever seen her." When Lauren was 13, her own mother had died of a massive heart attack. Lauren had lived with her older brother for a while, then with a neighbor in Hazlet, New Jersey, who was like a surrogate mom, but in important ways she'd grown up mostly alone. The chance to create her own family, to be the mother she didn't have, touched a place deep inside her."

Martin and Montagne introduce Lauren through plain biographical facts rather than
sentiment: her job, her age, the mother she lost at 13. The one openly emotional
line is a quote from her husband, attributed to him, not a feeling the writers
assert on their own. They keep their own voice level and let the facts and that
one quote do the feeling.

> "According to Lauren's records, Vaclavik did order a preeclampsia lab test around 8:40 p.m., but a nurse noted a half-hour later: "No abnormal labs present." (According to Larry, the results were borderline.) Larry began pushing to call in a specialist. Vaclavik attributed Lauren's pain to esophagitis, or inflammation of the esophagus, which had afflicted her before, he said in his deposition."

This reconstructs a medical failure step by step, and every step is sourced:
"according to Lauren's records," the nurse's exact note, "he said in his
deposition." Where two accounts differ, the writers mark it in a parenthesis
rather than pick one. The term of art is defined the moment it appears,
"esophagitis, or inflammation of the esophagus," so a lay reader never falls
behind.
