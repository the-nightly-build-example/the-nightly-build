# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/nh-predict (01)

This article keeps the press's plain-explainer register (the Yglesias standard: plain
claims, concrete stakes, teach don't summarize) and the declared reader (smart, widely
read, no clinical or insurance background — define Medicare Advantage, prior
authorization, and post-acute care in plain words at first use). What is different here,
against the settled-fact incidents this desk usually runs, is that this is a named
company in active litigation. That changes sentences in four ways.

1. **Weld every load-bearing claim to its source inside the sentence, and make the
   source's type visible.** Three registers of claim must stay legible on the page: what
   the record establishes (the docket, CMS guidance, the Senate report, federal
   reversal data), what the complaint alleges (the ~90% appeal-reversal figure; the
   pressure to hold patients within a narrow band of the tool's projected days; that the
   projections were decisive in practice), and what UnitedHealth says (nH Predict is a
   guide, not a coverage-decision tool). The exemplars carry this without hedging every
   clause: they attach a claim to its owner once, cleanly — "the complaint alleges,"
   "court records show," "the company said" — and let that verb carry the epistemic
   weight. Do not smear "allegedly" across a paragraph as insurance; name the document,
   then write the sentence.

2. **Make the mechanism land by showing the gap, not naming it.** The teachable core is
   a population average with error bars converted into a per-patient coverage cap. The
   exemplars never explain this abstractly first. They set the tool's prediction beside
   the documented person and let the reader see the daylight between them (a
   down-to-the-minute discharge date against a patient the notes still record in pain).
   Build the mechanism from one such visible gap before generalizing to distribution
   shift and automation bias.

3. **Let the record indict; keep your own adjectives out of it.** The force in the
   exemplars comes from selecting the damning documented fact, not from prosecutorial
   diction. This paper has no appetite for hype or doom, and a live suit makes a
   sharpened sentence the costly error. Resist the line that turns an allegation into a
   verdict. Where cause is disputed (UnitedHealth's "only a guide" against the
   plaintiffs' "decisive in practice"), state the strongest form of each side and name
   the evidence that would settle it — internal directives on adherence to the tool, the
   actual denial-and-appeal data. Never write that the company "hinted" or "signalled."

4. **Anchor the abstraction in one or two named, dated people from the record.** The
   plaintiffs in Estate of Lokken v. UnitedHealth both died; give dates and roles
   exactly, and source each biographical or clinical detail to the complaint or the
   docket, not to the mechanism you are teaching.

Transfer the exemplars' discipline, never their wording. Do not adopt their signature
phrases ("black box," "unknowable journey," "under the guise," "tidy series of numbers,"
"spat out a score").

## Licenses

form: a body section opened on one named, dated person drawn from the record
move: STAT and ProPublica open on a single patient or defendant with a precise number
  attached (a discharge estimate; a risk score), then widen to the system; the person is
  the proof, not the ornament, and every detail is filing-sourced
bar:  the person carries a specific step of the mechanism, not mood; every biographical
  and clinical detail is cited to a filing or the docket; introduced once, not milked
  for pathos

form: silent juxtaposition of the tool's prediction against the documented reality
move: the exemplars place the algorithm's output next to the record of the person (a
  target discharge date against notes showing the patient still in pain) and let the
  reader see the contradiction, with no connective verb bridging the two
bar:  both halves are separately cited and factually on the record; the contradiction is
  real, not staged by omitting context; no editorializing verb ("shockingly,"
  "absurdly") supplies a reaction the facts already carry

form: a single statistic used as the argument's hinge
move: "Machine Bias" turns on one reversal ("only 20 percent... actually went on to");
  here the ~90% appeal-reversal figure is the tell that the population-to-individual
  conversion was invalid — one number, placed where the argument pivots, its inference
  shown
bar:  attributed to its owning document as an allegation (the complaint); given a
  comparison the reader already holds; and its logic spelled out (why a high reversal
  rate among the few who appeal implies many wrong denials that were never appealed and
  stood) rather than asserted

form: dry understatement resting on a cited precision
move: STAT lets a "down-to-the-minute" prediction and a decimal day-count do the ironic
  work — false exactness against an unmeasurable recovery — without the writer stepping
  in to editorialize
bar:  the irony lives entirely in a true, cited detail and targets the tool's false
  precision, never the company's motive or good faith; at most once or twice in the
  piece; if the sentence still needs an adjective to land, it fails

## Recently used, do not reuse

- **The system-as-agent headline verb.** This desk's recent headlines make the system
  the grammatical actor: "taught itself to penalize," "learned racism," "overshot the
  CDC," "missed two-thirds," "illustrated 1943 German soldiers." The commission licenses
  one personification move for the whole piece. If the headline spends it, the dek must
  not, and the reverse.
- **The comma-and dek.** Every recent dek joins two or three clauses with commas and
  closes on "and" (amazon: "...traces to a single 2018 Reuters investigation and five
  anonymous sources, and the flaw it describes now lives..."; flu: "...and the search
  engine feeding it kept changing..."; gemini: "...and Google pulled the feature three
  weeks after launch"). The headline standard bans this mold and the commission flags it
  again. Write the dek as one lean sentence with a stance, not a clause chain.
- **The comma-joined heading.** "Winter flu, summer pandemic" and "Two accounts, one gap
  in the timeline" carry the same stamped cadence. Vary the heading shape.
- **The worn present-day-parallel close.** The series requires a "where it lives today"
  step, but the recent phrasings are used up: "The same weakness in today's screeners,"
  "Where the same blind spot runs today." Name the specific present-day system (automated
  prior-authorization; the Cigna PXDX parallel) rather than reaching for "the same
  weakness today."
- **The disputed-cause heading from flu-trends** ("Two accounts, one gap in the
  timeline"). This article also steelmans a dispute; keep the both-sides content, drop
  that heading mold.
- **Reflex heading shapes recently leaned on** (not banned, but avoid by reflex): the
  negation-explainer ("Why deleting the word did not delete the bias") and the "-ing"
  nominal ("A resume engine that learned to prefer men," "A signal answering a different
  question").

## Casey Ross and Bob Herman, "Denied by AI" (STAT News)
Source: https://www.statnews.com/2023/03/13/medicare-advantage-plans-denial-artificial-intelligence/
Craft:
- cadence: opens on a name and a false-precise number, subject-verb then accumulating
  qualifiers; alternates short percussive lines with longer documentary ones
- argument: traces harm backward from one patient to the incentive structure to the
  regulatory vacuum; the mechanism (a prediction used as a proxy for medical judgment,
  with providers locked out of the reasoning) is stated early and re-shown inside each
  case
- evidence: court records, federal reversal data, the tool's own six-page report, the
  ownership-and-money trail; company statements quoted and bracketed off from findings
- stance: let-the-record-speak; the critical charge is carried by what is selected to
  show, not by a verdict the writer pronounces
- notice: names what the tool cannot capture — the gap between a target discharge date
  and a patient still recorded in pain
- diction: forensically plain surface; the rare loaded word ("tidy," "down-to-the-minute")
  undercuts false precision and is anchored in a real, cited detail
- reader: assumes competence, not expertise; explains the algorithm without condescension
  and pivots from mechanism to ethical stakes within a sentence
- the important move: strict separation of silence from statement ("did not respond
  directly... But a spokesperson said") — precision about exactly what the company did and
  did not say

## Patrick Rucker, Maya Miller and David Armstrong, "How Cigna Saves Millions by Having Its Doctors Reject Claims Without Reading Them" (ProPublica)
Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/cigna-pxdx-medical-health-insurance-rejection-claims
Craft:
- cadence: setup, expectation, reversal — opens on a patient's ordinary expectation of
  coverage, then the denial; short declaratives building into explanatory sentences
- argument: built-to-do versus actually-did stated as a contrast; the economic logic
  (human review costs more than a low-dollar claim is worth) makes the system legible as
  profit translated into workflow, no insurance expertise required
- evidence: internal spreadsheets, corporate documents, named former employees, one
  insider's on-record explanation; a single figure (average 1.2 seconds per claim) does
  the load-bearing work
- stance: prosecutorial but not inflammatory — the company gets space to respond and its
  refusals are documented rather than read as guilt. For transfer: take the documentation
  discipline, leave the prosecutorial edge; this paper's register is cooler
- notice: the one damning number selected out of a spreadsheet and set where the reader
  cannot miss it
- diction: verbs frame the speed as the problem ("instantly reject," "bypasses those
  steps") yet stay attributable; "said" becomes a thin container for the company's
  unverified assertions while documents earn fuller attribution
- reader: positions the reader as a potential victim of the same system; opens on a
  credible, sympathetic protagonist
- the important move: attribution weight is calibrated by source type — documents get
  "reviewed by," the company gets a bare "said"

## Julia Angwin, Jeff Larson, Surya Mattu and Lauren Kirchner, "Machine Bias" (ProPublica)
Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing
Craft:
- cadence: paired-case parallelism ("rated high risk / rated low risk"); short
  declaratives early, lengthening as context accumulates
- argument: shows the misclassification as narrative fact before proving it
  statistically; locates responsibility in the design choices (137 questions, race not
  among them) rather than vaguely in "the data"
- evidence: the outlet's own statistical analysis, isolated and labelled as its finding;
  the maker's rebuttal quoted and bracketed; the designer's own ambivalence in his words
- stance: restrained, almost bureaucratic, which sharpens the indictment when a judgment
  finally surfaces
- notice: the reversal that makes the failure obvious without stats literacy ("only 20
  percent... actually went on to")
- diction: editorial distance held by word choice; irony without editorializing
- reader: assumes curiosity, not expertise; poses a question, then answers it, so the
  reader follows the reasoning instead of being handed a conclusion
- the important move: each named person anchors exactly one statistical claim, so a
  relative-risk figure resolves to a specific individual who did or did not reoffend
