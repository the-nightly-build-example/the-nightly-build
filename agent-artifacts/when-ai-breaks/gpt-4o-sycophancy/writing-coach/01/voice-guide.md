# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/gpt-4o-sycophancy

## How this piece should sound

This is an incident report about a model that told people what they wanted to
hear, including people who were in danger. The register that fits is the one
Angwin, Newton, and Greenberg all share below: state exactly what happened, to
whom, and when, and let that carry the weight. The piece earns its judgments
the way OpenAI's own postmortems already do — plainly, and only after the
specific fact that supports them is on the page.

Open on the specific. Angwin's piece opens with Brisha Borden on a particular
spring afternoon, a bike worth eighty dollars, a police call, well before it
ever states what the numbers will show. This piece has its own version of that
specificity available: a dated update, a named model, a particular exchange a
user posted that OpenAI or a named outlet later confirmed.

Where the postmortems name their own process failures, write them the way
Greenberg writes Maersk's decision not to fund its security redesign: as a
plain causal chain, stated once. The fact that a warning was ignored, or that
a feedback signal was weighted a certain way, does that work on its own once
the reader has the mechanism in front of them.

Where the harm shows up in a specific person's case, follow Newton's counselor
scene: report the plain sequence of what was said and done, in short
sentences, and resist the adjective that tells the reader how to feel about
it. The specificity is what makes it land; a sentence describing its own
gravity would undercut that.

Where OpenAI's account and a user's account, or two named outlets, disagree,
give each its own quoted words, the way Angwin hands Northpointe its own
sentence to rebut her analysis with. The house standard already asks for
steelmanning; this is the sentence-level shape that takes.

Where a figure in this incident needs scale — how fast the rollback happened,
how many people saw the behavior, how big the reversal was against whatever
came before — anchor it the way Greenberg anchors NotPetya's cost against the
Atlanta ransomware attack: a comparison the reader already has a feel for, set
right next to the new number.

Attribute the alarming reports to where they actually came from, the way
Newton attributes the account of Randy's gun to Randy and then gives
Cognizant's on-the-record response right after it. When a claim is a user
report, not yet confirmed, say so in the sentence itself.

Reserve any characterization of tone for moments a quote actually earns it, on
the model of Greenberg's one line about Snabe's "Scandinavian touch of
understatement," noted because the quote right there supports it. Report what
OpenAI and affected users said and did. Where a source characterizes its own
reasoning, quote that directly.

## Julia Angwin, Jeff Larson, Surya Mattu, and Lauren Kirchner, "Machine Bias"

Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing

> "On a spring afternoon in 2014, Brisha Borden was running late to pick up her god-sister from school when she spotted an unlocked kid's blue Huffy bicycle and a silver Razor scooter. Borden and a friend grabbed the bike and scooter and tried to ride them down the street in the Fort Lauderdale suburb of Coral Springs."

The piece opens on one afternoon, one named person, one object with a value
attached. The reader gets the incident before being told what it means, and
the writers are visible in the choice of which afternoon to start on.

> "Two years later, we know the computer algorithm got it exactly backward. Borden has not been charged with any new crimes. Prater is serving an eight-year prison term for subsequently breaking into a warehouse and stealing thousands of dollars' worth of electronics."

The verdict sentence — "got it exactly backward" — arrives only after the
specific outcomes that prove it, one clause per person, with no adjective
telling the reader how to feel about the reversal. The two sentences that
follow are what earn the judgment.

> "The algorithm used to create the Florida risk scores is a product of a for-profit company, Northpointe. The company disputes our analysis. In a letter, it criticized ProPublica's methodology and defended the accuracy of its test: 'Northpointe does not agree that the results of your analysis, or the claims being made based upon that analysis, are correct or that they accurately reflect the outcomes from the application of the model.'"

Where the subject of the piece disputes the finding, the writers hand it its
own sentence in its own words. The reader sees exactly what Northpointe said,
and can weigh it against the analysis already laid out.

## Casey Newton, "The Trauma Floor"

Source: https://www.theverge.com/2019/2/25/18229714/cognizant-facebook-content-moderator-interviews-trauma-working-conditions-arizona

> "When the counselor sees her, he explains that she has had a panic attack. He tells her that, when she graduates, she will have more control over the Facebook videos than she had in the training room. You will be able to pause the video, he tells her, or watch it without audio. Focus on your breathing, he says. Make sure you don't get too caught up in what you're watching."

A disturbing scene is carried entirely by what the counselor actually said,
sentence by short sentence, with no line describing how upsetting the moment
is. The restraint is in what's left out: the plain instructions do the work
by themselves.

> "Despite the high degree of difficulty in applying such a policy, Facebook has instructed Cognizant and its other contractors to emphasize a metric called 'accuracy' over all else. Accuracy, in this case, means that when Facebook audits a subset of contractors' decisions, its full-time employees agree with the contractors. The company has set an accuracy target of 95 percent, a number that always seems just out of reach."

The term is defined by what it actually measures: agreement between two sets
of employees. Newton states the target and the fact that it's rarely hit in
the same plain register, without commenting on the gap between them.

> "Fearing for his safety, Randy began bringing a concealed gun to work. Fired employees regularly threatened to return to work and harm their old colleagues, and Randy believed that some of them were serious. A former coworker told me she was aware that Randy brought a gun to work, and approved of it, fearing on-site security would not be sufficient in the case of an attack. Cognizant's Duncan told me the company would investigate the various safety and management issues that moderators had disclosed to me. He said bringing a gun to work was a violation of policy and that, had management been aware of it, they would have intervened and taken action against the employee."

The most alarming detail in the piece is attached to a named source and
immediately followed by the company's on-record response to it. Newton
reports what a named company official said when asked, and lets the
juxtaposition of the two accounts stand.

## Andy Greenberg, "The Untold Story of NotPetya, the Most Devastating Cyberattack in History"

Source: https://www.wired.com/story/notpetya-cyberattack-ukraine-russia-code-crashed-the-world/

> "To get a sense of the scale of NotPetya's damage, consider the nightmarish but more typical ransomware attack that paralyzed the city government of Atlanta this past March: It cost up to $10 million, a tenth of a percent of NotPetya's price. Even WannaCry, the more notorious worm that spread a month before NotPetya in May 2017, is estimated to have cost between $4 billion and $8 billion. Nothing since has come close."

Greenberg puts the new number next to two the reader can already picture. "A
tenth of a percent" does more work than an adjective like "unprecedented"
would, and it lets the reader do the comparing.

> "The security revamp was green-lit and budgeted. But its success was never made a so-called key performance indicator for Maersk's most senior IT overseers, so implementing it wouldn't contribute to their bonuses. They never carried the security makeover forward."

The organizational failure is reported as a plain causal chain: approved,
tied to no incentive, therefore never built. The mechanism, stated exactly,
makes the judgment for itself.

> "'This was a very significant wake-up call,' Snabe said at his Davos panel. Then he added, with a Scandinavian touch of understatement, 'You could say, a very expensive one.'"

The one moment of character in the piece is tied directly to a quote that
supports it — Snabe's own words are the understatement Greenberg names. The
sentence names what Snabe said and how he said it, and stops there.
