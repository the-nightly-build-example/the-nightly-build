# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/sports-illustrated-ai-authors

## How this piece should sound

Open on the invented person before naming what happened to them. Angwin's team
put Brisha Borden and Vernon Prater on the page, with their ages, their thefts,
and their prior records, before the sentence that tells you a computer scored
one of them dangerous and the other safe. This lesson has its own paired
figures ready-made: a masthead that names a real magazine and a byline that
names nobody. Let the invented author's name, face, and biography sit on the
page first. A reader who has already met the fake person as a person has more
reason to care that the byline was invented than a reader who is told
up front that it was, the same way Borden and Prater's mismatched scores mean
more for having met both of them first.

Newton opens "The Trauma Floor" on one flat sentence with nothing hedging it.
This lesson can afford that kind of opener once, probably at the top: state
what was published, or who the vendor was, or what the record shows, without
a qualifier riding along to soften it. Save the qualifiers (disputed, unclear,
contested) for the places the commission says the record actually forks:
how much of the text itself was AI-written, and who chose the persona. Angwin's
paragraph does this cleanly, isolating exactly one variable (race) after
naming everything else that could have explained the disparity and ruling it
out first. When this lesson reaches the vendor-versus-publisher dispute, the
same move applies: name what both sides agree happened, then isolate the one
thing they don't.

Numbers earn their place the way Newton's wage figures do: not "AI makes
fabrication cheap" on its own, but a figure set against a figure the reader
already holds, the way $28,800 sits next to $240,000. If the record gives this
incident a comparable pair (what a stock AI headshot costs against what a real
contributor or staff photo costs, how many bylines or articles AdVon's
persona farm produced against a newsroom's), that comparison belongs in the
body, not a summary adjective like "cheap" standing alone.

Hill's closing move is instructive for where this lesson's body has to end,
since the lesson template forbids a Verdict block: she gives the last line to
Ton-That's own answer, unglossed, rather than writing the moral herself. This
piece has an equivalent moment already staged in the commission: Arena Group's
and AdVon's statements, blaming each other. Consider letting the last thing
the body does be handing the reader that exchange, or the specific fact that
settles it, rather than a sentence that tells the reader how to feel about it.
The takeaway bookend can then do its own job: what the reader now knows to
watch for, not a repeated verdict.

The register throughout is Hill, Newton, and Angwin's own: declarative
sentences that carry a fact each, quotes left to do their own work without an
adjective stacked in front of them, and a verdict stated once, in plain words,
the way Angwin's team writes "the computer algorithm got it exactly backward"
rather than reaching for something like "deeply flawed." None of the three
writers reach for scale words like "watershed" or "reckoning." When this
incident's stakes need stating, state the mechanism and the specifics and let
those carry it.

## Kashmir Hill, "The Secretive Company That Might End Privacy as We Know It"

Source: https://www.nytimes.com/2020/01/18/technology/clearview-privacy-facial-recognition.html

> "Until recently, Hoan Ton-That's greatest hits included an obscure iPhone
> game and an app that let people put Donald Trump's distinctive yellow hair
> on their own photos.
>
> Then Mr. Ton-That — an Australian techie and onetime model — did something
> momentous: He invented a tool that could end your ability to walk down the
> street anonymously, and provided it to hundreds of law enforcement agencies,
> ranging from local cops in Florida to the F.B.I. and the Department of
> Homeland Security."

The opening paragraph lists two throwaway apps before the second paragraph
turns on one word, "momentous," and immediately backs that word with a
specific claim (what the tool does) and a specific list (which agencies got
it). The stakes are not asserted on their own; they are the direct object of
a plain verb, "invented." Hill is visible in the flat, deadpan setup, which is
what lets "momentous" read as a claim the next sentence proves rather than as
color.

> "While the company was dodging me, it was also monitoring me. At my request,
> a number of police officers had run my photo through the Clearview app.
> They soon received phone calls from company representatives asking if they
> were talking to the media — a sign that Clearview has the ability and, in
> this case, the appetite to monitor whom law enforcement is searching for."

Hill puts herself in the sentence only as the instrument of a fact: her own
reporting attempt became evidence of exactly the capability she was reporting
on. She narrates what happened to her, not what the piece is doing or what she
thinks readers should take from it. The last clause draws the conclusion in
the same plain register as the rest, from the specific incident just
described.

> "Even if Clearview doesn't make its app publicly available, a copycat
> company might, now that the taboo is broken. Searching someone by face
> could become as easy as Googling a name. Strangers would be able to listen
> in on sensitive conversations, take photos of the participants and know
> personal secrets. Someone walking down the street would be immediately
> identifiable — and his or her home address would be only a few clicks away.
> It would herald the end of public anonymity.
>
> Asked about the implications of bringing such a power into the world, Mr.
> Ton-That seemed taken aback.
>
> "I have to think about that," he said. "Our belief is that this is the best
> use of the technology.""

The piece's biggest claim, the end of anonymity, gets stated in short, literal
sentences (Googling a name, a few clicks away) rather than an abstraction, and
then Hill hands the very last word to the subject's own answer instead of
writing a closing verdict herself. The founder's own answer stands in for the
verdict Hill never writes.

## Casey Newton, "The Trauma Floor: The secret lives of Facebook moderators in America"

Source: https://www.theverge.com/2019/2/25/18229714/cognizant-facebook-content-moderator-interviews-trauma-working-conditions-arizona

> "The panic attacks started after Chloe watched a man die."

One sentence, one fact, no qualifier ahead of it. Newton trusts the sentence
to carry the whole opening on its own nouns (Chloe, panic attacks, a man
dying) rather than building up to it with framing. A reader who has read only
this sentence already knows what kind of piece this is.

> "The median Facebook employee earns $240,000 annually in salary, bonuses,
> and stock options. A content moderator working for Cognizant in Arizona, on
> the other hand, will earn just $28,800 per year. The arrangement helps
> Facebook maintain a high profit margin."

Instead of writing that contractors are underpaid, Newton puts the two salary
figures next to each other and lets the gap between them be the argument. The
comparison is doing the work a judgment word would otherwise have to do, and
it survives fact-checking in a way an adjective wouldn't.

> "They do the work as long as they can — and when they leave, an NDA
> ensures that they retreat even further into the shadows.
>
> To Facebook, it will seem as if they never worked there at all.
> Technically, they never did."

The piece's final judgment (that the invisibility of this labor is a design
choice, not an accident) arrives as the last beat of the reporting, stated
once, plainly, and left alone. Newton doesn't restate it a second time for
emphasis; the two short sentences that close the piece just complete the
thought and stop.

## Julia Angwin, Jeff Larson, Surya Mattu and Lauren Kirchner, "Machine Bias"

Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing

> "On a spring afternoon in 2014, Brisha Borden was running late to pick up
> her god-sister from school when she spotted an unlocked kid's blue Huffy
> bicycle and a silver Razor scooter. Borden and a friend grabbed the bike
> and scooter and tried to ride them down the street in the Fort Lauderdale
> suburb of Coral Springs."

The piece opens on a named person doing something small and specific, with
nothing yet said about an algorithm or a finding. The detail (a kid's bike, a
particular suburb) is doing the work of making Borden a person before she
becomes a data point three paragraphs later.

> "Yet something odd happened when Borden and Prater were booked into jail:
> A computer program spat out a score predicting the likelihood of each
> committing a future crime. Borden — who is black — was rated a high risk.
> Prater — who is white — was rated a low risk."

By the time this sentence arrives, the piece has already given the reader
each person's actual crime and record, so the racial disparity doesn't need
announcing. It falls out of a plain, parallel sentence structure that simply
states what each person was rated, with nothing else added.

> "Two years later, we know the computer algorithm got it exactly backward.
> Borden has not been charged with any new crimes. Prater is serving an
> eight-year prison term for subsequently breaking into a warehouse and
> stealing thousands of dollars' worth of electronics."

The verdict ("got it exactly backward") comes in plain words and immediately,
with no hedging ahead of it, and it is backed in the very next two sentences
by what actually happened to these two specific people afterward, not by an
appeal to the aggregate data yet to come.
