# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/meta-ad-delivery-discrimination

## How this piece should sound

This lesson has two jobs in order: tell what the ad-delivery system did, then
explain why an optimizer produces that kind of skew without anyone choosing
it. Keep those jobs visibly separate the way Julia Angwin's team keeps
Borden's and Prater's booking-day facts apart from the statistical test that
follows them — state what happened, then state what the analysis of it
showed, and don't let the second do the first's work.

The reader has never bought an ad or trained a ranking model, so every term —
targeting, delivery, relevance score, engagement optimization — gets built up
before it is used, the way Kashmir Hill builds "probe image" and "confidence
score" into the Williams narrative only at the moment the investigator
actually uses them, each anchored to what happened next rather than defined
in the abstract. Do the same with delivery: introduce it at the point in the
timeline where it starts doing something the advertiser's targeting did not.

Numbers carry this piece; Angwin's paragraph on the 7,000 Broward County
scores gives the population, the years, and the benchmark in the same breath
as the finding, and the next paragraph gives the 20 percent figure with what
it was measured against. The delivery study's own skew figures, the
housing-charge numbers, and the settlement's terms deserve the same treatment
— named denominator, named period, stated once plainly rather than gestured
at as "significant."

Where the piece steelmans Meta's account of its own system, let Meta's own
word do the work the way Oremus lets Facebook's "sometimes" carry the
weakness of its own ranking claim, rather than reaching for an adjective to
signal skepticism. Attribute the position, quote the term the company itself
used to defend it, and move on; the flatness of just repeating the company's
word is the point.

Close sections on the documented fact rather than on a verdict. Hill ends the
Williams narrative on a plain sentence of what he was actually doing that
night, not on a line assessing what it means — the lesson template's own
bookends are where judgment belongs, and the body should leave the last word
in each section to something a reader could check, not to a summary
sentence grading it.

The desk has leaned on a two-sentence headline whose second sentence flatly
reverses the first, and on "X, not Y" section headings; neither is available
here by default. If the mechanism section needs to correct something the
piece has actually shown a reader believing — that the discrimination was in
the targeting, not the delivery — that specific correction can carry a
negative-parallel heading. Nothing else should reach for the mold.

## Julia Angwin, Jeff Larson, Surya Mattu, and Lauren Kirchner, "Machine Bias"

Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing

> "On a spring afternoon in 2014, Brisha Borden was running late to pick up her god-sister from school when she spotted an unlocked kid's blue Huffy bicycle and a silver Razor scooter. Borden and a friend grabbed the bike and scooter and tried to ride them down the street in the Fort Lauderdale suburb of Coral Springs."

The piece opens on a single afternoon with a bicycle and a scooter, not on
the algorithm. Every noun is a specific one — the make of bike, the town, the
season — and none of them needs the reader to already know what a risk score
is. The mechanism enters only once this scene has given it something to act
on.

> "Yet something odd happened when Borden and Prater were booked into jail: A computer program spat out a score predicting the likelihood of each committing a future crime. Borden — who is black — was rated a high risk. Prater — who is white — was rated a low risk.
>
> Two years later, we know the computer algorithm got it exactly backward. Borden has not been charged with any new crimes. Prater is serving an eight-year prison term for subsequently breaking into a warehouse and stealing thousands of dollars' worth of electronics."

The two outcomes sit next to each other with no adjective attached to either
one. "Got it exactly backward" is the only interpretive phrase in the
passage, and it is earned by the two sentences that immediately follow it,
which report what actually happened to each person. The writer's judgment
and the reader's evidence for it arrive in the same breath.

> "We obtained the risk scores assigned to more than 7,000 people arrested in Broward County, Florida, in 2013 and 2014 and checked to see how many were charged with new crimes over the next two years, the same benchmark used by the creators of the algorithm.
>
> The score proved remarkably unreliable in forecasting violent crime: Only 20 percent of the people predicted to commit violent crimes actually went on to do so."

The method comes before the finding, in one sentence: what was obtained, from
where, over what period, measured against whose own benchmark. The 20
percent that follows means something specific because the paragraph before it
already said what was counted and for how long.

## Kashmir Hill, "Wrongfully Accused by an Algorithm"

Source: https://www.nytimes.com/2020/06/24/technology/facial-recognition-arrest.html

> "On a Thursday afternoon in January, Robert Julian-Borchak Williams was in his office at an automotive supply company when he got a call from the Detroit Police Department telling him to come to the station to be arrested. He thought at first that it was a prank."

The day of the week, the workplace, and the caller are all named before
anything about facial recognition appears. The second sentence is short and
holds one plain fact — that he assumed it was a prank — which does more to
place the reader in the moment than any description of his emotional state
would.

> "Five months later, in March 2019, Jennifer Coulson, a digital image examiner for the Michigan State Police, uploaded a "probe image" — a still from the video, showing the man in the Cardinals cap — to the state's facial recognition database. The system would have mapped the man's face and searched for similar ones in a collection of 49 million photos."

The technical step is narrated as an action a named person took on a named
date, not as a process description. "A collection of 49 million photos" is
the only number in the passage, and it is doing the job a vaguer phrase like
"a huge database" could not: it tells the reader the actual size of the
haystack the system searched.

> "He has since figured out what he was doing the evening the shoplifting occurred. He was driving home from work, and had posted a video to his private Instagram because a song he loved came on — 1983's "We Are One," by Maze and Frankie Beverly."

This sits near the end of the piece and states a fact rather than its
significance. The reader is left to notice on their own that this is an
alibi the police never checked; the sentence itself just says where he was
and what he was doing.

## Will Oremus, "Who Controls Your Facebook Feed"

Source: https://www.slate.com/articles/technology/cover_story/2016/01/how_facebook_s_news_feed_algorithm_works.html

> "Every time you open Facebook, one of the world's most influential, controversial, and misunderstood algorithms springs into action. It scans and collects everything posted in the past week by each of your friends, everyone you follow, each group you belong to, and every Facebook page you've liked. For the average Facebook user, that's more than 1,500 posts. If you have several hundred friends, it could be as many as 10,000."

The scale of the system is given in two concrete numbers before any claim
about what's wrong with it. A reader who has never thought about a feed
algorithm now has a fixed quantity to hold onto — 1,500 posts, up to 10,000 —
before the piece asks anything else of them.

> "When I arrive at Facebook's sprawling, Frank Gehry–designed office in Menlo Park, I'm met by a lanky 37-year-old man whose boyish countenance shifts quickly between an earnest smile and an expression of intense focus. Tom Alison is director of engineering for the news feed; he's in charge of the humans who are in charge of the algorithm."

Alison is introduced as a specific, physically described person before his
title does any work. The line "in charge of the humans who are in charge of
the algorithm" states the piece's whole argument about where responsibility
sits, in the middle of what is otherwise a plain introduction — it isn't set
off as a thesis statement, it just falls out of the description.

> "The result? The algorithm's rankings correspond to the user's preferences "sometimes," Facebook acknowledges, declining to get more specific."

The company's own word, "sometimes," is quoted and left alone. The writer
adds nothing to it — no adjective, no aside about how weak an answer that
is — because the word does that on its own once it's attributed and placed
next to the question it was answering.
