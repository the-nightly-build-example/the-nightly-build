# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/google-photos-gorilla

## How this piece should sound

This lesson tells one real failure and then teaches why that kind of system
fails that way, for a reader who is sharp and widely read but new to how
automatic image labeling works. Keep the register plain and serious from the
first sentence to the last. This incident is disturbing on its own, so the prose
does not need to push; the facts can be stated level and left to do their own
work.

Narrate what happened in order, the way Somers narrates the 911 outage: short
declarative sentences, each carrying a single fact, the reader meeting what the
system did before reading why. The part of this story that does harm is a label
with a racist history, filed against photographs of a real person. Report it the
way Somers reports the death in the Bookout paragraph, as a plain statement set
at the same level as the facts around it, with no intensifier and no reach for
effect. Report it plainly, and heightening the language would not make it more
serious.

When you reach the mechanism, teach each unfamiliar idea the way Travis teaches
angle of attack: the plain definition first, then something the reader already
holds, his hand out the car window. Build the explanation one step at a time, as
Travis builds from the larger engine to the shifted thrust line to the rising
nose, so a reader with no background can follow the chain to its end. When you
name the specific technical decision at the center of the error, do what Travis
does with the single active computer. Slow down and state it in the plainest
sentence in the passage, not the most emphatic one.

Much of this lesson rests on what researchers and reporters found, and the reader
cannot check it themselves, so show your footing as you go. Where a study or a
figure carries a claim, state its strength and its limit in the same place, the
way Luu does with his fifty-percent number, so the reader can weigh how much it
proves. Where the record is settled, say so plainly; where you are reasoning past
it, mark that line. A flat, exact sentence can hold a real verdict, as Somers's
does and as Luu's closing line does, but only when the facts in front of it have
earned it.

When the piece turns to where this same weakness sits in systems the reader uses
now, hold the plain, naming voice of the rest of it. Name the specific systems
and what they share with this one, and let the piece end on that connection.

## James Somers, "The Coming Software Apocalypse"

Source: https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/archive/2017/09/saving-the-world-from-code/540393/

> "There were six hours during the night of April 10, 2014, when the entire
> population of Washington State had no 911 service. People who called for help
> got a busy signal. One Seattle woman dialed 911 at least 37 times while a
> stranger was trying to break into her house. When he finally crawled into her
> living room through a window, she picked up a kitchen knife. The man fled."

Somers narrates the outage as a run of plain declarative sentences, each carrying
one fact: the six hours, the busy signal, the 37 calls, the knife. This is the
passage where the harm is most frightening, and he reports it with no intensifier
and no adjective reaching for effect. The restraint is where Somers shows: he
adds nothing to the woman with the kitchen knife, and the sentences are worse to
read for it.

> "But software doesn't break. Intrado's faulty threshold is not like the faulty
> rivet that leads to the crash of an airliner. The software did exactly what it
> was told to do. In fact it did it perfectly. The reason it failed is that it
> was told to do the wrong thing. Software failures are failures of
> understanding, and of imagination."

Here Somers gives the cause in the same flat register he used for the narration,
so the explanation carries the authority of the reporting. He draws one contrast,
the software against the faulty rivet that fails an airliner, then states plainly
what went wrong: the code did what it was told, and it was told the wrong thing.
The verdict in his final sentence is earned by the sentences under it; he does
not assert it before he has shown it.

> "In September 2007, Jean Bookout was driving on the highway with her best
> friend in a Toyota Camry when the accelerator seemed to get stuck. When she
> took her foot off the pedal, the car didn't slow down. She tried the brakes but
> they seemed to have lost their power. As she swerved toward an off-ramp going 50
> miles per hour, she pulled the emergency brake. The car left a skid mark 150
> feet long before running into an embankment by the side of the road. The
> passenger was killed. Bookout woke up in a hospital a month later."

The Bookout narration is built from specifics a reader can picture: the stuck
accelerator, the dead brakes, 50 miles per hour, the 150-foot skid mark. When the
passenger's death comes, it is a four-word sentence in the middle of the
paragraph, set in the same plain form as the skid mark before it. Somers shows in
that choice: he reports the killing without raising his voice, and trusts the
reader to feel it.

## Gregory Travis, "How the Boeing 737 Max Disaster Looks to a Software Developer"

Source: http://www.gregorytravis.com/HowLooksSoftware/ (the author's own reprint of his IEEE Spectrum article)

> "The solution was to extend the engine up and well in front of the wing.
> However, doing so also meant that the centerline of the engine's thrust
> changed. Now, when the pilots applied power to the engine, the aircraft would
> have a significant propensity to "pitch up" — raise its nose."

Travis walks the cause forward one step at a time: the bigger engine, the new
mounting position, the shift in the thrust line, the nose that now wants to rise.
Each sentence hands the reader exactly what the next one needs, so a person with
no aviation background can follow a mechanical chain to its consequence. Travis
the pilot is visible in the confidence of the sequencing, the sense that he knows
which single fact to add at each step.

> "Pitch-up in an aircraft increases something called the "angle of attack."
> That's the angle between the wings and the airflow over the wings. Think of
> sticking your hand out a car window on the highway — if your hand is level, you
> have a low angle of attack. If your hand is "pitched up," you have a high angle
> of attack. When the angle of attack is great enough, the wing enters what we
> call an aerodynamic stall."

To define angle of attack, Travis reaches for the reader's own hand out a car
window, an experience they already have, and maps the unfamiliar term onto it. He
gives the plain definition first, then the physical picture, then the failure
point, the stall. This is the pilot-engineer teaching the way he must have taught
in a cockpit, using the body of the person in front of him to explain the wing.

> "In the 737 MAX only one of the flight management computers is active at once.
> Either the pilot's flight management computer or the co-pilot's flight
> management computer. And that computer takes inputs ONLY from the sensors on the
> side of the aircraft corresponding to which flight computer is in control."

After the buildup, Travis names the design decision at the center of the failure
in three short sentences, ending on the flat fact that the active computer takes
its inputs from one side of the plane. He does not raise his voice for the crucial
part. He slows down and states it, and the one word he capitalizes, ONLY, is the
single place he lets emphasis show.

## Dan Luu, "Reading postmortems"

Source: https://danluu.com/postmortem-lessons/

> "I love reading postmortems. They're educational, but unlike most educational
> docs, they tell an entertaining story. I've spent a decent chunk of time reading
> postmortems at both Google and Microsoft. I haven't done any kind of formal
> analysis on the most common causes of bad failures (yet), but there are a
> handful of postmortem patterns that I keep seeing over and over again."

Luu opens by telling you plainly why he finds the subject worth his time, in the
first person, with no throat-clearing. The sentence that matters is the admission
in parentheses: he has not done the formal analysis yet, so what follows is a
pattern he has noticed, not a measured result. Luu is visible in that honesty, in
his care to tell the reader exactly how much weight his claims can bear.

> "Configuration bugs, not code bugs, are the most common cause I've seen of
> really bad outages. When I looked at publicly available postmortems, searching
> for "global outage postmortem" returned about 50% outages caused by
> configuration changes. Publicly available postmortems aren't a representative
> sample of all outages, but a random sampling of postmortem databases also
> reveals that config changes are responsible for a disproportionate fraction of
> extremely bad outages."

Luu makes a claim, gives the figure that supports it, and then tells you why the
figure is soft: the postmortems he searched are not a representative sample. He
does not drop the number and move on, and he does not let the caveat cancel the
claim. He states the strength of the number and its limit in the same place, so
the reader can weigh it.

> "One of the things I find to be curious about these failure modes is that when I
> talked about what I found with other folks, at least one person told me that
> each process issue I found was obvious. But these "obvious" things still cause a
> lot of failures. In one case, someone told me that what I was telling them was
> obvious at pretty much the same time their company was having a global outage of
> a multi-billion dollar service, caused by the exact thing we were talking about.
> Just because something is obvious doesn't mean it's being done."

Luu takes a claim that could sound like a truism, that known problems still cause
failures, and attaches one real example: someone called his finding obvious while
their own multi-billion-dollar service was going down from that exact cause. The
example does the work, so the point does not rest on the phrasing. His flat
closing sentence carries because the story sits in front of it.
