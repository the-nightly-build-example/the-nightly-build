# evidence: when-ai-breaks/nyc-mycity-chatbot (01)

The record covers what the MyCity Business chatbot was built to do, the
specific answers three newsrooms recorded from it, the laws those answers
contradicted, the Adams administration's response, and the Mamdani
administration's decision to shut the tool down. It is strongest on the outputs
The Markup and the Associated Press captured against reproducible prompts, on
the two versions of the on-page disclaimer, and on the statutes and NYC agency
guidance that own the counter-claim. It is thin in three places worth naming.
First, the chatbot's answers varied between runs, so any single prompt/response
pair records what one query returned rather than what every query returned;
the ten-of-ten Section 8 result in the Markup piece is the closest thing to a
distribution. Second, the exact procurement dollars are reported only
approximately ("nearly $600,000" to build, "around half a million dollars"
running cost as Mayor Mamdani described it); I did not find a signed contract.
Third, the tool has been offline since February 4, 2026, so the current live
site cannot be re-queried; the record relies on what the two newsrooms captured
while the tool was live and on the screenshots preserved with their stories.
Microsoft's role is documented (Azure OpenAI service, Nuvalence as SBS's
integration partner), but neither company has published its own account.

## Contradictions

- The chatbot did not always give the wrong answer. The Markup writes: "the
  bot doesn't return the same responses to queries every time. At one point,
  it told a Markup reporter that landlords did have to accept housing
  vouchers, but when ten separate Markup staffers asked the same question,
  the bot told all of them no." The article records that answers to the
  Tyrrell rent-withholding question shifted between runs after the story
  went up. Any single prompt/response pair records what one query returned,
  not a rate. The ten-of-ten Section 8 result is the closest measurement.
- The Adams administration's stance in October 2023 promised "clear-eyed"
  handling of AI risks and framed the AI Action Plan as first-of-its-kind
  responsible governance. The March 2024 investigation and the internal
  panel Kim spoke on show no pre-launch legal-accuracy testing was
  described. The tension is between the press-release framing and the
  operating tool, not a factual contradiction between sources.
- Adams and Lewis-Martin's April 2 framing treats the chatbot as normal
  early-technology iteration ("this is how it's done"; the MapQuest
  analogy). The academic sources in AP (Stoyanovich, West,
  Venkatasubramanian) frame the same conduct as reckless deployment of an
  unproven system by a government whose users cannot be expected to
  double-check legal answers. Ted Ross's LA comment gives an operational
  counterpoint: LA's chatbots were content-curated and not built on LLMs.
- On disclaimers: the chatbot's own answers contradicted the on-page
  disclaimer even after the April 2 update. Prompt "Can I use this bot for
  professional business advice?" returned "Yes," while the page told users
  not to.
- On mechanism framing: Ars Technica describes the failure mode as generic
  LLM confabulation over token statistics, and points to RAG as the
  industry mitigation. That framing understates that MyCity was already an
  Azure OpenAI service pointed at the "more than 2,000 NYC Business web
  pages" set: retrieval alone did not force the generated sentence to
  match. The Verge and Gothamist stories, both of which searches surface
  for this incident, could not be retrieved (Verge returned a 404 on the
  URL search results pointed to; the Gothamist article page loaded no
  extractable text). Those absences are recorded here rather than papered
  over.
