# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/clearview-ai

## How this piece should sound

This lesson tells what Clearview AI did and then teaches why a scraped
face-search engine gets found unlawful. The reader is new to the technology and
new to the privacy-law frame, so the register is plain throughout: state the
action, name the actor and the date, and let the sequence run. Where Clearview
scraped photos, built the index, and sold searches to police, the plain verb and
the exact object do the work, the way Kashmir Hill opens on a father who
"grabbed his Android smartphone and took photos." Grand words about surveillance
stay out until the record has shown their size.

Each term of art the piece cannot avoid gets defined in plain words the moment it
appears. Biometric data, a lawful basis for processing, a data controller, an
injunction: gloss each one in the same sentence that introduces it, the way Amy
Howe names the "third-party doctrine" and explains it in the same breath before
using it. Assume the reader is quick and assume they hold none of this yet.

Clearview was found unlawful by more than one regulator and court, so the piece
can walk those findings the way Howe walks a case: who acted, what they found,
and what they ordered, each said plainly and in order. Where the exact words of a
ruling or a regulator's finding carry the precision, quote the operative line and
then say in plain words what it required, as Hill quotes the investigator's own
sentence and then states what the police decided. When the reasoning behind a
finding is the thing being taught, render it as plain cause, the way Howe renders
why the court could not treat years of cell-site records like older business
records.

The scale of the scraping and the size of the fines are figures, so give the
figure and the exact thing it counts, the way Julia Angwin's report fixes an
incident to a Huffy bicycle, a Razor scooter, and the dollars they were worth.
When the piece reaches the harm a scraped face database does to the people in it,
let the specific loss or exposure carry it, as Hill lets "a decade of contacts,
emails and photos" carry hers, without a word like devastating standing in for
the detail.

The lesson has to show why this kind of system fails. Where it shows the failure,
set the facts side by side and let the reader see the gap before any verdict, the
way Angwin places a defendant's rated risk against what that person actually did
two years later. A verdict is welcome once the facts that earn it are on the
page, and it is held to the same plainness as the reporting around it. No hype
and no doom: when the evidence is genuinely alarming, the dates and the numbers
carry that on their own.

## Kashmir Hill, "A Dad Took Photos of His Naked Toddler for the Doctor. Google Flagged Him as a Criminal."

Source: https://www.nytimes.com/2022/08/21/technology/google-surveillance-toddler-photo.html

> Mark noticed something amiss with his toddler. His son's penis looked swollen and was hurting him. Mark, a stay-at-home dad in San Francisco, grabbed his Android smartphone and took photos to document the problem so he could track its progression.

The three sentences state what happened in the order it happened, with the
plainest verbs available: noticed, looked swollen, grabbed, took photos. Hill
adds no adjective to tell the reader how to feel, and the plainness is the choice
of a reporter who trusts the facts. She is visible in the decision to write
"document the problem" rather than anything heavier.

> With help from the photos, the doctor diagnosed the issue and prescribed antibiotics, which quickly cleared it up. But the episode left Mark with a much larger problem, one that would cost him more than a decade of contacts, emails and photos, and make him the target of a police investigation.

The harm is measured in named things, a decade of contacts and emails and photos
and a police investigation, instead of in a word for how bad it was. Hill settles
the medical question in one clause and turns to the cost in the next, keeping
both flat. Her judgment shows in what she counts.

> "I determined that the incident did not meet the elements of a crime and that no crime occurred," Mr. Hillard wrote in his report. The police had access to all the information Google had on Mark and decided it did not constitute child abuse or exploitation.

Hill renders an official finding by quoting the investigator's operative sentence
and then stating in her own plain words what the police decided. The quotation
holds the precision and her sentence holds the meaning. Naming Mr. Hillard and
saying what he had access to keeps the finding checkable.

## Julia Angwin, Jeff Larson, Surya Mattu, and Lauren Kirchner, "Machine Bias"

Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing

> On a spring afternoon in 2014, Brisha Borden was running late to pick up her god-sister from school when she spotted an unlocked kid's blue Huffy bicycle and a silver Razor scooter. Borden and a friend grabbed the bike and scooter and tried to ride them down the street in the Fort Lauderdale suburb of Coral Springs.

The scene is fixed to exact objects, an unlocked kid's blue Huffy bicycle and a
silver Razor scooter, and to a named suburb. Nothing is left generic, so the
reader sees the event instead of a summary of it. Angwin's eye for the small true
detail shows in the choice to name the brands.

> Yet something odd happened when Borden and Prater were booked into jail: A computer program spat out a score predicting the likelihood of each committing a future crime. Borden — who is black — was rated a high risk. Prater — who is white — was rated a low risk. Two years later, we know the computer algorithm got it exactly backward. Borden has not been charged with any new crimes. Prater is serving an eight-year prison term for subsequently breaking into a warehouse and stealing thousands of dollars' worth of electronics.

The judgment lands because two facts are set beside each other: the risk each
person was rated, then what each did in the two years after. Angwin states the
verdict, that the algorithm got it exactly backward, only once the sentences that
follow have earned it with the outcomes. The matched construction of the two
ratings lets the reader make the comparison first.

> The score proved remarkably unreliable in forecasting violent crime: Only 20 percent of the people predicted to commit violent crimes actually went on to do so.

A central finding is delivered as one plain sentence built around its figure. The
clause before the colon says what was tested and the clause after gives the
number, with no decoration between them. Angwin lets the 20 percent stand without
comment.

## Amy Howe, "Opinion analysis: Court holds that police will generally need a warrant for sustained cellphone location information"

Source: https://www.scotusblog.com/2018/06/opinion-analysis-court-holds-that-police-will-generally-need-a-warrant-for-cellphone-location-information/

> Over 40 years ago, the Supreme Court outlined what has come to be known as the "third-party doctrine" – the idea that the Fourth Amendment does not protect records or information that someone voluntarily shares with someone or something else. Today the Supreme Court ruled that, despite this doctrine, police will generally need to get a warrant to obtain cell-site location information, a record of the cell towers (or other sites) with which a cellphone connected.

Howe names the "third-party doctrine" and defines it inside the same sentence, in
words a non-lawyer can hold, before she uses it. The next sentence states what the
court ruled and glosses "cell-site location information" the same way. She writes
as someone who decided the reader should get the term and its meaning together.

> The issue came to the Supreme Court in the case of Timothy Carpenter, who was convicted and sentenced to almost 116 years in prison for his role in a series of armed robberies in Ohio and Michigan. Law-enforcement officials used cell-site records from his cellphone provider to place him in the vicinity of the crimes, but Carpenter argued that the jury should not hear about those records because the government had not obtained a warrant for them. A federal appeals court upheld his conviction, explaining that the government was not required to seek a warrant because Carpenter could not have expected cellphone records maintained by his service provider to remain private. This morning the Supreme Court reversed that ruling.

The procedural history is told in order and in plain words: who was convicted,
what he argued, what the lower court held, and what the Supreme Court did. Every
sentence moves the sequence forward and none assumes the reader already knows the
case. Howe's command of the record shows in how little she strains to make it
clear.

> When the Supreme Court decided the cases establishing the doctrine, he explained, it was dealing with relatively "limited types of personal information"; no one could have envisioned that cellphones would be so ubiquitous and provide so much information about their users for so long.

Howe renders the court's reasoning as plain cause: the doctrine was built for
limited kinds of records, and no one then foresaw how much a cellphone would
reveal. She keeps the court's exact quoted phrase where it carries weight and
carries the rest of the logic in her own plain sentence around it. The passage
reads as someone who understood the opinion well enough to say it simply.
