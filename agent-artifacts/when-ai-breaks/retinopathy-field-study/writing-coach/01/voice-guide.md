## How this piece should sound

This is a lesson on the When AI Breaks desk, told for a reader who is quick but has never worked inside a screening clinic or a model. It narrates one deployment in order and then teaches why a system that scored like an expert on graded retinal images faltered once nurses used it on real patients. Keep the register plain and the claims direct, the way you would explain something you understand well to a smart friend. When a sentence could sound good or be understood, make it understood.

The hardest thing to hold steady is the double fact the piece rests on: the model was accurate on the bench and the deployment still failed the people in the room. Leveson and Turner state their method before they narrate any accident, that a single cause is usually the wrong story, and the calm of that stance is worth carrying into the clinic. You can say plainly what went wrong there without making the model, the nurses, or the engineers the villain of it.

The nurses and patients are the people this failure happened to, and the passages show how to keep them on the page as people. The Therac operator is written as someone skilled doing routine work, her ordinary competence established in facts about her job before any fault is named, so the reader's sympathy has something under it. Where the piece describes a nurse retaking a photograph the system rejected, or a patient who traveled hours to be screened, the same discipline is available: report what they did and why it was reasonable, and let the facts carry the sympathy rather than adjectives about how hard it was.

Langewiesche sets aside the easy explanation, that the pilots were simply incompetent, in a single clause, and turns to the harder one. The easy version here is that the clinics or the nurses were not ready for the technology. If the evidence points elsewhere, say so early and spend the piece on the harder account. When he does reach a verdict on the crew, it arrives as one unsentimental question after a named engineer's plain words. A judgment about this deployment, once the reporting earns it, can be that spare.

When the cause comes down to numbers, such as the share of images the system rejected, the accuracy it was validated to, or the time a screening took in practice, work them on the page the way Langewiesche works out Dubois's four hours a year at the controls, so the reader follows the arithmetic instead of taking a figure on faith. A rate the reader cannot scale on their own can be anchored to something they already hold.

Gawande reports a death in a few sentences, each stating the routine step, the specific slip, and the outcome once. That economy suits the clinic sequence: what the system was asked to do, what it did with a real image under real light and a real connection, and what that cost the nurse and the patient in front of it. When you reach the reason it failed that way, state it once and let the evidence already laid down hold it up, rather than restating it as a moral.

## Nancy Leveson and Clark S. Turner, “An Investigation of the Therac-25 Accidents”

Source: https://web.mit.edu/6.033/2004/wwwdocs/papers/Therac_1.html (Part I) and https://web.mit.edu/6.033/2004/wwwdocs/papers/Therac_2.html (Part II)

> “Most accidents are system accidents; that is, they stem from complex interactions between various components and activities. To attribute a single cause to an accident is usually a serious mistake.”

The authors state their method as a plain claim before they narrate a single accident, so the reader knows the investigation will not end at one villain. Leveson and Turner are visible in the flat confidence of “is usually a serious mistake,” which is a working safety engineer's refusal to simplify.

> “The operator had held this job for some time, and her typing efficiency had increased with experience. She could quickly enter prescription data and change it conveniently with the Therac's editing features. She entered the patient's prescription data quickly, then noticed that for mode she had typed "x" (for X ray) when she had intended "e" (for electron). This was a common mistake since most treatments involved X rays, and she had become accustomed to typing this. The mistake was easy to fix; she merely used the cursor up key to edit the mode entry.”

The passage treats the operator as a skilled person doing routine work. Every clause explains why an experienced operator would type the wrong mode, correct it, and move on, so her ordinary competence is on the page before the machine's fault is. The sympathy is built entirely from facts about her job.

> “A lesson to be learned from the Therac-25 story is that focusing on particular software bugs is not the way to make a safe system. Virtually all complex software can be made to behave in an unexpected fashion under certain conditions.”

Here the writers reach the cause without dramatizing it, moving from the one coding error to what it shows about the design as a whole. The judgment is stated once, plainly, and left to rest on the evidence the piece has already set out.

## William Langewiesche, “The Human Factor”

Source: https://www.vanityfair.com/news/business/2014/10/air-france-flight-447-crash (Vanity Fair, October 2014; the web version carries the title “Should Airplanes Be Flying Themselves?”)

> “To the question of why, the facile answer—that they happened to be three unusually incompetent men—has been widely dismissed. Other answers are more speculative, because the pilots can no longer explain themselves and had slid into a state of frantic incoherence before they died. But their incoherence tells us a lot. It seems to have been rooted in the very advances in piloting and aircraft design that have improved airline safety over the past 40 years.”

The opening sets the easy explanation aside in one clause, “has been widely dismissed,” and turns at once to a harder one, so the piece commits to explaining rather than accusing. Langewiesche is visible in the exactness of “frantic incoherence” and in his willingness to locate the cause in the safety improvements themselves rather than in the men.

> “A renowned cockpit designer at Boeing—himself a transport pilot—once said to me, “We don’t believe there are any bad pilots. We believe there are average pilots who have bad days.” He called this a principle that underlies Boeing’s cockpit designs. But if Bonin was an average pilot, what does that say about the average?”

A named designer gives a design principle in plain speech, and the writer follows it with a single question instead of a verdict. The sympathy and the severity sit in the same breath: Bonin is granted the status of an average pilot, and the average is what the question puts in doubt.

> “On Air France 447, for instance, Captain Dubois had logged a respectable 346 hours over the previous six months but had made merely 15 takeoffs and 18 landings. Allowing a generous four minutes at the controls for each takeoff and landing, that meant that Dubois was directly manipulating the side-stick for at most only about four hours a year. The numbers for Bonin were close to the same, and for Robert they were smaller.”

The cause is carried by exact figures: hours flown, takeoffs, landings, and a calculation down to about four hours a year, worked out where the reader can see it. Dubois is not called lazy or careless. The numbers show a competent captain who was rarely allowed to fly, which is both harder to write and fairer to him.

## Atul Gawande, “When Doctors Make Mistakes”

Source: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1999/02/01/when-doctors-make-mistakes

> “The fact is that virtually everyone who cares for hospital patients will make serious mistakes, and even commit acts of negligence, every year. For this reason, doctors are seldom outraged when the press reports yet another medical horror story. They usually have a different reaction: That could be me.”

The claim that error is universal is made with counts and a distribution, and only then does the reader arrive at the plain human line. Gawande is visible in “That could be me”: he places himself and his colleagues inside the failure instead of standing over it.

> “He described the bad lighting and her extremely thick neck, and was careful to make those sound not like excuses but merely like complicating factors. Some attendings shook their heads in sympathy. A couple of them asked questions to clarify certain details. Throughout, Ball’s tone was objective, detached. He had the air of a CNN newscaster describing unrest in Kuala Lumpur.”

This shows how a serious failure can be reported without drama. The surgeon lists the conditions, keeps them from becoming excuses, and holds a flat tone, while the room's sympathy is registered in one small gesture rather than announced. Gawande's own eye is in the closing comparison, which is dry and precise about how detached the account sounded.

> “But it was one case in particular that galvanized him. Friends of his had taken their eighteen-year-old daughter to the hospital to have her wisdom teeth pulled, under general anesthesia. The anesthesiologist inserted the breathing tube into her esophagus instead of her trachea, which is a relatively common mishap, and then failed to spot the error, which is not. Deprived of oxygen, she died within minutes.”

A death is reported in four sentences, with the ordinary procedure, the specific error, and the outcome each stated once. The quiet distinction between the common slip and the failure to catch it does the analytic work, and a named reformer is given the reason he acted.
