# Voice guide: when-ai-breaks/michigan-midas

## How this piece should sound

This is a lesson for When AI Breaks: one incident, reconstructed from the record and then explained, for a reader who is sharp and widely read but has never looked inside an unemployment system. Hold the register plain, factual, and humane, the way Matt Yglesias explains something he understands well. Report what MiDAS did and keep your own hand light. The facts of this one are strong enough to stand without help.

Build the human anchor the way Colloff builds Mickey Bryan and Gawande builds Anthony DeFilippo, from exact ordinary particulars, before the machinery reaches them. A named claimant, the wording of the determination letter, the wages garnished, the dates, will do more than any description of a flawed system. The commission warns off the recent house openers, so the anchor is better found in one person's own facts than in a figure-scene or a two-applications setup.

Let the numbers land the way Fink lets hers. She reports that mortuary workers carried 45 corpses from Memorial, more than from any comparable-size hospital in the city, and adds nothing to it. MiDAS turns on a small set of figures: the share of determinations the state later found wrong, the number of people, the size of the penalty multiplier. Each is strongest stated plainly beside the comparison that lets a reader scale it, not pressed on.

Keep the record's language exact where the record is contested. Fink writes that health care workers "charged" the doctor and nurses, and that a fact "appears" once documents are matched, holding apart what is proven from what is alleged. This piece needs that same care on one word above all: fraud was the accusation the system made, and actual fraud was rare. Say which is meant each time. Where the cause itself is disputed, the state's account of what MiDAS decided against the claimants', or the vendor's role against the agency's own configuration, give each side its strongest form and say what evidence would settle it, in the same even voice.

The lesson has to move from a person to the mechanism underneath, the way Gawande moves from one patient at the bedside to how the work actually goes wrong. Make that move with MiDAS's own parts: what a data-mismatch flag was, what a human examiner did before, what changed when the examiner was taken out of the loop. Teach the mechanism this incident shows. What it adds up to is yours to reach from the research, not to borrow from these exemplars.

Vary the sentences and trust the short ones. Colloff's quiet "Most mornings" opening and Gawande's bare inventory of a ward both work without a single adjective. Close on where this design still runs, named in Michigan's own nouns and in the article's own words.

## Sheri Fink, "The Deadly Choices at Memorial"

Source: https://www.propublica.org/article/the-deadly-choices-at-memorial-826

> "The smell of death was overpowering the moment a relief worker cracked open one of the hospital chapel's wooden doors. Inside, more than a dozen bodies lay motionless on low cots and on the ground, shrouded in white sheets. Here, a wisp of gray hair peeked out. There, a knee was flung akimbo. A pallid hand reached across a blue gown."

Fink opens on a fixed scene and builds it only from what a person in the doorway could see: the cots, a wisp of gray hair, a knee, a hand across a gown. She reports no reaction and reaches for no adjective, and that restraint is what makes it land. The reporter is visible in the decision to stop at what the door showed and let the reader supply the rest.

> "The hurricane knocked out power and running water and sent the temperatures inside above 100 degrees. Still, investigators were surprised at the number of bodies in the makeshift morgue and were stunned when health care workers charged that a well-regarded doctor and two respected nurses had hastened the deaths of some patients by injecting them with lethal doses of drugs. Mortuary workers eventually carried 45 corpses from Memorial, more than from any comparable-size hospital in the drowned city."

Here Fink moves from the scene to the record and lets the figures do the work: temperatures above 100 degrees, 45 corpses, more than any comparable hospital in the city. The phrase "a well-regarded doctor and two respected nurses" keeps the people's standing in view at the moment they are accused, and she writes the accusation as an accusation, "health care workers charged," not as a finding. The judgment about who did what is held back; the sentence carries only what the record supports.

## Atul Gawande, "The Checklist"

Source: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2007/12/10/the-checklist

> "When he arrived in our I.C.U., at 1:30 A.M. on a Sunday, his ragged black hair was plastered to his sweaty forehead, his body was shaking, and his heart was racing at a hundred and fourteen beats a minute. He was delirious from fever, shock, and low oxygen levels. "I need to get out!" he cried. "I need to get out!" He clawed at his gown, his oxygen mask, the dressings covering his abdominal wound."

Gawande introduces a patient by the hair on his forehead, his heart rate, the words he shouted, and his hands pulling at the tubes, and a stranger becomes a specific person in four sentences. The detail is exact and never decorated. Gawande the surgeon is visible in what he chooses to notice, the clinical number and the human panic held in one paragraph.

> "I accompanied a team on 7 A.M. rounds through one of the surgical I.C.U.s. It had eleven patients. Four had gunshot wounds (one had been shot in the chest; one had been shot through the bowel, kidney, and liver; two had been shot through the neck, and left quadriplegic)."

Gawande conveys how overwhelming the ward is by listing exactly who is in it rather than calling it overwhelming. The specifics carry the weight a summarizing adjective would drop. He trusts a plain inventory, and the reader feels the load without being told to.

## Pamela Colloff, "Blood Will Tell"

Source: https://features.propublica.org/blood-spatter/mickey-bryan-murder-blood-spatter-forensic-evidence (Part One); https://features.propublica.org/blood-spatter/joe-bryan-conviction-blood-spatter-forensic-evidence (Part Two)

> "Most mornings, the sky was still black when Mickey Bryan made the short drive from her house on Avenue O, through the small central Texas town of Clifton, to the elementary school. Sometimes her car was the only one on the road. The low-slung, red-brick school building sat just south of the junction of State Highway 6 and Farm to Market Road 219 — a crossroads that, until recent years, featured the town's sole traffic light."

Colloff spends her first sentences on an ordinary commute, the black sky, the empty road, the crossroads with the town's one traffic light, so the reader knows Mickey Bryan as a person before anything happens to her. Nothing is foreshadowed, and the patience is what builds the momentum. Colloff is visible in the willingness to withhold and let the plain detail set the scene.

> "As W. Leon Smith neared the East Texas town of Huntsville, he did not know what to expect. It was a warm September day in 1991, and Smith, a mild-mannered 38-year-old newspaperman whose wire-rimmed glasses lent him a slightly owlish look, had come to interview an inmate named Joe Bryan."

Colloff introduces the newspaperman with a few exact strokes, the warm September day, his age, the wire-rimmed glasses, that he "did not know what to expect," and the reader is already moving with him toward the prison. She characterizes a person and advances the story in the same two sentences, which is how a reconstruction keeps its pace without hurrying past the people in it.
